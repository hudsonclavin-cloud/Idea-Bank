# Kirkwood's Ruler
> A printable ruler of the asteroid belt, made from the orbits of 1,576,490 real asteroids. Its top edge drops into four notches, then falls away where the main belt ends. Kepler's law, given nothing but the length of Jupiter's year, says where all five are. No picture of the sky shows them: they are gaps in the sizes of orbits, not in space.

**Kind:** printable object
**Run:** open `ideas/72-kirkwood-ruler/index.html` in a browser (classic scripts, works from `file://`, no network). Choose a population and a stretch of the belt, then *Download STL*. `default.stl` is the default ruler, ready to slice. Settings can also come from the URL: `?pop=big&a0=2&a1=3.5&bin=0.01&scale=sqrt&count=a&length=160&relief=22&depth=14&ledge=1` (and `match=0` to give the count by distance its own height scale). Tests: `node --test ideas/72-kirkwood-ruler/belt.test.mjs` from the repo root.
**Made by:** Notchwright

## What it is

A bar 166 mm long with a scale of 100 mm to the astronomical unit (au, the Earth's distance from the Sun), so 1 mm on it is 0.01 au, about 1.5 million km. It spans 1.8 to 3.4 au: the Hungaria group and the main asteroid belt. Its top edge is a histogram: one 1 mm bar for every 0.01 au of orbit size, as tall as the number of known asteroids whose orbits are that size. The tallest bar, 2.64 to 2.65 au, stands for 23,653 asteroids, and in this default the height is proportional to the count, so every cubic millimetre of the relief stands for about 77 asteroids.

In front of the bars runs a two-step ledge. The lower step is a ruler: a bump every 0.1 au, taller at 2.5 and taller still at 2.0 and 3.0. The upper step has a groove at each orbit size where an asteroid would go round the Sun a whole number of times for a whole number of Jupiter's years: 3 for 1 at 2.501 au, 5 for 2 at 2.824 au, 7 for 3 at 2.957 au and 2 for 1 at 3.277 au, the four gaps that JPL's own histogram of the belt labels. Above the first three grooves the bars drop into notches 8.9, 7.7 and 2.6 mm deep: three of the Kirkwood gaps. At the fourth, the 2:1, there is no notch to find. The bars have already fallen, from 21 mm at 3.16 au to under half a millimetre by 3.26 au, and they stay that low to the end of the ruler at 3.4 au. At this scale the 2:1 is where the main belt ends: the orbits just beyond it hold a few hundred asteroids to a bar against 23,653 at the tallest, too few to give it an outer rim. Sliced at 0.2 mm layers (I read the G-code once, see the honest assessment), the bars from 3.27 to 3.32 au print one layer lower than those either side: a dip 0.2 mm deep and 5 mm wide at the foot of the fall. Between the 3:1 and the 5:2 the fingernail will find a fourth notch with no groove under it: 3.6 mm deep at 2.70 au, deeper than the 7:3. It sits at 2.705 au, where an asteroid goes round 8 times for every 3 of Jupiter's years, a weaker resonance that I did not give a groove (see the honest assessment). Every other dip along the top edge is under 1 mm, the deepest 0.96 mm. The idea is that you find the gaps by running a fingernail along the top, and that at three of the notches, and once more where it falls off the belt, a finger of the other hand on the ledge below is in a groove. (Nobody has tried that yet: see the honest assessment.) The square-root and log scales lift the sparse orbits; on the same stretch they make the 2:1 a dip 0.6 and 1.1 mm deep, against 1.5 to 11 mm for the other three grooved gaps.

The page lets you choose what to print within the data:

- **Which asteroids:** every known asteroid (1,576,490), only those with the best-determined orbits (1,141,495), only the larger ones with absolute magnitude H below 15 (84,216), the first 1,000 numbered, or the 91 found by the end of 1866, when Kirkwood saw the gaps.
- **Count each asteroid by** the size of its orbit (the ruler), or by where it is, averaged over its year. The second makes a smooth hill with no notches at all, from the same asteroids. Unless you untick it, the second is drawn on the height scale of the first over the same stretch, so that a cubic millimetre of either stands for the same number of asteroids. Printing the pair is the point of the page (see *Why I made it*).
- **Which stretch:** anywhere from 1.5 to 5.5 au, from a 0.1 au close-up of one gap to Mars-to-Jupiter, with bins from 0.0025 au, never narrower than 1 mm on the object.
- **Bar height** proportional to the count, its square root, or its logarithm (the last two make the sparse outer belt, with its Hildas and Trojans, something you can feel).
- **The object:** length of the scale (50 to 170 mm), tallest bar (4 to 40 mm), depth (8 to 30 mm), with or without the ledge.

Seven presets cover the interesting cases. A 3D view (drag sideways, or arrow keys) shows the solid exactly as it will be downloaded; below it, the front silhouette can be read bar by bar by touch, mouse or keyboard. Two live tables say what the reader's fingernail will find. One gives, for each groove, the asteroids there against the belt on each side, and what the bars of this ruler do above it: a notch so many mm deep, an edge, or nothing. The other gives what a notch finder that knows nothing about Jupiter finds in the same counts.

## Why I made it

The brief was a printable object whose shape *is* real space data, something no one could feel before it was printed. I sketched three candidates:

1. **Saturn's rings as a record:** a Cassini occultation profile spun into a disc, the Cassini Division a groove you could feel. The physics is lovely (the B ring's outer edge sits at a resonance with Mimas) but the data host was unreachable from here, and at a printable scale most of the ring structure is finer than a nozzle.
2. **A constellation in depth:** pins standing on a plate, one per star, heights from Hipparcos parallaxes. A familiar idea, and tall thin pins are poor prints.
3. **The asteroid belt by orbit size:** a histogram of real orbits, printed so a finger can run along it.

The third won on every count. The data is a catalogue anyone can query (JPL's Small-Body Database answered from here, all 1.58 million orbits), it reduces honestly to counts in bins, and the shape it makes is famous on paper; a short search found no printed or tactile version of it. It also has exactly the two halves this bank asks for.

**The part that can be proven.** Where the grooves go is pure arithmetic: Kepler's third law and the length of Jupiter's year, nothing fitted to asteroids. Where the notches are is pure data: 1.58 million orbits, counted. That the two agree can be checked, and the page checks it twice: once by measuring how empty the belt is at each groove, and once with a notch finder that is never told where the resonances are. On the default stretch its four strongest notches are at the 5:2, 3:1 and 7:3 and at the 8:3, a weaker resonance that has no groove. Its fifth is the 2:1, which it finds as a step in the belt rather than a dip in it, and its sixth is the 9:4. All six lie within 0.0059 au of the resonance's orbit size.

**The part that can only be felt.** The Kirkwood gaps cannot be seen in a picture. They are not dark rings in the sky: an asteroid's distance from the Sun swings back and forth through the year (by 0.39 au either side of its orbit size, for the median main-belt asteroid), straight across the gap distances. A picture of the whole belt from far above would show each asteroid where it is, not where its orbit size says; counted by distance from the Sun and averaged over their orbits, the same catalogue makes a smooth hill. The gaps exist only in a list, as missing *orbit sizes*, which is to say missing *lengths of year*. That is why the page has the second count. Print the ruler and its twin, counted by where the asteroids are, and hold one in each hand. They are the same asteroids on the same scale, both along the bar and in height, with notches in one and not the other. The twin is the lower hill, 12.3 mm at its top against 22, and holds a little less: 1,383,202 asteroids on average against 1,512,033, because eccentric orbits spend part of each year outside 1.8 to 3.4 au. What your fingernail finds is not a place that is empty but a rhythm that is: years that are a simple fraction of Jupiter's. I found that hard to believe even after computing it, and I made the pair so that it could be checked with two hands instead of a chart.

## How it works

**The data.** `reduce.mjs` reads six pages from the JPL Small-Body Database Query API (`sb-kind=a`, `full-prec=1`, fields `pdes, a, e, i, H, condition_code, epoch`), fetched on 23 October 2026, 1,576,490 asteroids, every row unique, the API's own count matched. 1,480,209 of the orbits osculate at JD 2461200.5 (9 June 2026); the rest at 6,368 other epochs. It keeps, for five populations, the number of asteroids whose osculating semi-major axis falls in each 0.0025 au bin from 1.5 to 5.5 au (98.4% of the catalogue; the rest are counted as below or above), and the time-averaged number at each heliocentric distance in the same bins (below). The result, `belt-data.js`, is 52 KB; it records the SHA-256 of every raw page. The raw pages (150 MB) are not bundled; the header of `reduce.mjs` gives the exact URLs. As a check on the reduction, `crosscheck.py` redoes it independently in Python and numpy from the same raw pages: run once, it reproduced every orbit-size count in all five populations exactly, and every distance count to within 0.05 of an asteroid (the rounding to tenths). The populations:

| Population | Rule | Count |
|---|---|---|
| all | every asteroid | 1,576,490 |
| good | orbit condition code 0 (the Minor Planet Center's U parameter, smallest uncertainty) | 1,141,495 |
| big | H < 15, roughly 3 to 6 km and up depending on albedo (D = 1329 km / √p × 10<sup>−H/5</sup>) | 84,216 |
| first1000 | numbered 1 to 1000 (discovered 1801 to 1927, per JPL's discovery dates) | 1,000 |
| by1866 | numbered 1 to 91: every one discovered by 4 November 1866; (92) was found in July 1867, and no asteroid numbered 92 to 1000 was found before 1867 | 91 |

**The second count.** An orbit with semi-major axis *a* and eccentricity *e* spends the fraction (*E* − *e* sin *E*)/π of its year within *r* = *a*(1 − *e* cos *E*) of the Sun (Kepler's equation). `reduce.mjs` spreads every asteroid over the bins between its perihelion and aphelion by that fraction, exactly, and stores the sums to a tenth of an asteroid. Five orbits with *e* ≥ 1 are left out. This is what a snapshot from above would show on average, counted by distance: the same asteroids, smoothed by their own ellipses.

**Kepler.** Jupiter's mean longitude advances 3034.74612775° per century (JPL's *Approximate Positions of the Planets*, Table 1, after Standish & Williams 1992), a sidereal year of 11.8626 years. An asteroid making *p* laps per *q* of Jupiter's has mean motion (*p*/*q*) *n*<sub>J</sub>, and Kepler's third law with the Gaussian constant (*GM*<sub>☉</sub> = *k*²) gives its semi-major axis. The tests check this against the other route, Jupiter's mean semi-major axis 5.20288700 au scaled by (*q*/*p*)<sup>2/3</sup> and corrected for Jupiter's mass (Sun/Jupiter 1047.3486): every resonance agrees within 0.0005 au.

| Resonance | Orbit size | Period | In the data |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3:1 | 2.501 au | 3.95 yr | Kirkwood gap |
| 5:2 | 2.824 au | 4.75 yr | Kirkwood gap |
| 7:3 | 2.957 au | 5.08 yr | Kirkwood gap (the shallowest) |
| 2:1 | 3.277 au | 5.93 yr | Kirkwood gap, the Hecuba gap; in these counts, where the main belt ends |
| 3:2 | 3.969 au | 7.91 yr | the Hildas pile up |
| 1:1 | 5.201 au | 11.86 yr | the Trojans share Jupiter's orbit |

**Checking the data against Kepler.** Two measures, both computed live for whatever the reader chooses:

- *Emptiness at a groove:* the asteroids in the 0.005 au slice centred on the resonance, divided by what the belt 0.01 to 0.05 au away puts in a slice that wide, on each side separately. For every known asteroid: 3:1 holds 8% of the level on its inner side and 5% of the level on its outer side, 5:2 5% and 11%, 7:3 53% and 23%, 2:1 9% and 98%. At the 2:1 the outer side is as sparse as the slice itself, so it is a step in the belt rather than a gap in it, and the page says so whenever the two sides differ more than threefold. For the larger ones (H < 15) the 2:1 is a step too (3% and 65%). With both sides averaged together, as the first version reported it, the figures are 6%, 7%, 32% and 16% (1%, 0%, 25% and 6% for H < 15), and at 3:2 the Hildas make it 189%. Counted by distance instead, every side of every one of the four is between 93% and 108%.
- *What the bars do there* (`grooveDepth`), on the object itself. The floor is the lowest bar within 0.005 au of the resonance, and each rim is the tallest bar within 0.06 au on that side. A notch is a lower rim at least 1 mm above the floor (five 0.2 mm layers: my threshold, not one anyone has tested with a fingernail). An edge is a groove where only one side rises that far. On the default ruler: 3:1 a notch 8.9 mm deep, 5:2 7.7 mm, 7:3 2.6 mm, and at the 2:1 an edge, the bars 7.5 mm lower than on the inner side and only 0.1 mm lower than on the outer. On the square-root scale the 2:1 is 0.6 mm deep (still an edge), and on the log scale 1.1 mm (just a notch). Counted by distance, no groove has a dip at all: the bars rise or fall through every one. `objectNotches` takes the same measure from every bar and lists every notch, groove or no groove. On the default ruler it finds exactly four, at the 3:1, 8:3, 5:2 and 7:3; the page names any that have no groove under them.
- *The blind notch finder* (`findNotches`): it looks at every 0.005 au slice of the chosen stretch that starts on one of the data's 0.0025 au bins, so where the stretch starts makes no difference. For each slice no fuller than the slices either side of it, it takes the mean count *B* of the 16 slices 0.015 to 0.05 au away (eight each side) and scores the slice by (*B* − *c*)/√*B*, how many standard deviations it falls below its surroundings if asteroids were scattered at random. It keeps the highest scores, at least 0.05 au apart; the page shows six. Only then is each compared with a list of 24 resonances (every *p*:*q* with *p* ≤ 9 and order *p* − *q* ≤ 5, inside 1.5 to 5.5 au), eight of which lie between 1.8 and 3.4 au: the 4:1, 7:2, 3:1, 8:3, 5:2, 7:3, 9:4 and 2:1. On the default stretch it finds 2.8250 au (67 standard deviations, 94% below its surroundings), 2.5025 (66, 95%), 2.9550 (46, 71%), 2.7050 (22, 23%), 3.2825 (21, 83%) and 3.0275 au (18, 21%): the 5:2, 3:1, 7:3, 8:3, 2:1 and 9:4, 0.0013, 0.0020, 0.0016, 0.0002, 0.0059 and 0.0017 au away. The 2:1 notch is a step: 91% below the belt inside it but only 17% below the belt outside it, and the page marks it so. The same six, in the same order, came out of every stretch I tried: 2,286 of them, starting at every 0.0025 au from 1.5 to 2.45 au and ending at 3.4, 3.45, 3.5, 3.6, 4.0 or 5.5 au. At 0.0025 au slices it finds the same six with the 2:1 and 8:3 swapped. The best-determined orbits give the same six, the 2:1 fourth. The larger asteroids give the 5:2, 3:1, 7:3, 9:4, 2:1 (a step again) and 8:3, from 15 down to 9 standard deviations. In the first 1,000 and the 1866 populations nothing scores above 3. Scored against the lower of its two sides instead, so that a step cannot pass for a notch, the first four are the same, and the 2:1 is only a weak dip (6 standard deviations) at 3.2975 au, 0.021 au outside the resonance. Counted by distance, it finds no notch at all in the full catalogue.
- *How likely is that by chance?* If six notches fell anywhere between 1.8 and 3.4 au, each would land within 0.006 au of one of the eight listed resonances there with a chance of about 8 × 0.012/1.6 = 6%, so all six would together have about 5 × 10<sup>−8</sup>. As a check that does not assume the notches are scattered evenly, the tests slide the whole catalogue by every 0.0025 au step from 0.02 to 0.15 au, each way: 106 shifts. Unshifted, all six notches are within 0.006 au of a listed resonance. Shifted, the most that were was one, in 28 of the shifts. (A flat belt gives no notch at all, which is only a sanity check: it cannot fail.)
- *Changes I made after looking.* My first finder ranked slices by fractional depth, 1 − *c*/*B*. On the full catalogue it found the same four, but in sparse populations every empty slice scores 100%, so it filled its list with chance gaps (and, in the larger asteroids, with the sparse inner edge of the belt). I switched to the standard-deviation score after seeing that. The page still shows the fractional depth beside each notch. After the independent review (see the revision note) I changed it twice more. It no longer cuts its slices from the start of the stretch, which had made half of all starting points give a different fourth notch. And its list of resonances now runs to order 5 instead of 4, because the fourth notch, once the finder was fixed, is the 8:3.

**The solid.** `belt.js` lays out the object as terraces running the length of the bar, front to back: the tick step (1.6 mm, with 1 mm-wide bumps to 2.2, 2.6 or 3.0 mm), the groove step (3.8 mm, with 1.2 mm grooves down to 3.0 mm), then the bars (a 4.6 mm base plus the relief). Each terrace is a run of flat-topped segments on a 0.01 mm grid. The mesher builds every top, every vertical wall between terraces and between segments, the four outer walls, a 0.5 mm-high, 0.4 mm-deep bottom chamfer and the flat bottom, zipping each face's two edges so that every vertex on a shared edge belongs to both faces. That closes up exactly as long as each terrace is at least as tall as the one in front of it at every point, which the layout guarantees and the mesher checks. Each triangle is wound to its face's outward normal. The STL writer is about twenty lines: an 80-byte header, a count, then 50 bytes per triangle.

Rules that keep it printable: bins are never narrower than 1 mm on the object (the page widens the bin and says so); minor and half-au ticks are left out when they would come closer than 3 mm; a groove that would come within 2.4 mm of another is left out, and the page names it; the whole thing is at most 176 × 38 × 44.6 mm. The page also counts *fins* and warns when there are any. A fin is a bar, or a run of neighbouring bars under 3 mm wide in all, that stands 8 mm or more above the bars on both sides of it. The first version counted only single bars under 2 mm, and missed pairs that stand free together. Fins appear wherever the counts are sparse: in the 1866 and first-1000 populations, and with fine bins in the thinly populated outer belt (19 of them between 4.4 and 4.9 au with 0.005 au bins, the finest the page allows there at 160 mm, and 40 mm relief; the freest stands 33 mm clear on 1.6 mm). The page only warns about them. It does not lower the relief or change the scale on its own.

**The page.** `app.js` draws the mesh itself (orthographic, flat-shaded, back faces culled, painted far to near) and the front silhouette, and writes the tables from `belt.js`. Nothing animates; the view moves only while you drag or press a key, and the switches (presets, population, count, shared height scale, scale, ledge, viewpoints) redraw at most once every 350 ms, so a held key or fast taps cannot make the picture flicker faster than about three times a second. Light and dark schemes follow the system. Nothing is stored or sent.

## Honest assessment

- **[Certain]** The object has not been physically printed. The parts most likely to need adjusting on a real printer: the 1 mm bars and the 1 mm slots of empty bins (an over-extruding printer may close a slot; a coarser bin or a longer scale widens both); the 0.6 mm tick bumps and 0.8 mm grooves, which are meant to be felt and may need to be deeper for some fingers; and, wherever the counts are sparse (the 1866 and first-1000 populations, or fine bins in the outer belt) at high relief, narrow fins that stand free and may wobble or snap during printing or later. In the sweep's sparse outer-belt extreme, fins 1.6 mm wide stand 33 mm clear, and the print check passes it: it checks how thick walls are and how much faces overhang, not how tall a thin wall stands. The page warns about fins; it does not prevent them.
- **[Certain]** Every solid the page can make that I tried is a closed, consistently wound solid with Euler characteristic 2 and the volume the layout predicts: the default, the 17 extremes, the 7 presets, 400 random settings, a corner sweep of 1,440 combinations of every population, scale and ledge setting at the ends of length, relief, depth, range and bin, and 240 more counted by distance with the shared height scale on and off (tests). The 17 extremes (the default among them) also pass the repository's trimesh and PrusaSlicer check with no warnings; that check says nothing about whether a free-standing fin survives the nozzle. I have not tested every possible setting, and I make no claim beyond these.
- **[Certain]** `default.stl` is byte-identical to what the code produces, and its float32 coordinates still pair every edge.
- **[Certain]** The grooves are where Kepler's law puts the resonances, for Jupiter's mean motion; the bars are the bundled counts, checked bar by bar against the data.
- **[Certain]** The default ruler, as downloaded, has four notches at least 1 mm deep (my threshold), at the 3:1, 8:3, 5:2 and 7:3 (8.9, 3.6, 7.7 and 2.6 mm), and every other dip along it is under 1 mm. Three of the notches are over grooves. The fourth groove, the 2:1, is under an edge, where the bars have fallen to under half a millimetre and stay there. The first version said the top edge drops into four notches, meaning the four grooved gaps. That was wrong for the 2:1, and it did not mention the 8:3 notch at all.
- **[Certain]** On the default stretch, the six strongest notches the blind finder finds are within 0.0059 au of the 5:2, 3:1, 7:3, 8:3, 2:1 and 9:4 resonances. The one at the 2:1 is a step in the belt, not a dip in it. The slices at the four grooved resonances hold 5% to 53% of the belt on either side, except the 2:1's outer side, where the belt is as sparse as the slice (98%). This is a measurement on today's catalogue of osculating orbits, made with a finder I changed three times after seeing results (above): its score, where its slices start, and how far its list of resonances goes. Other choices give other lists. The old depth score put the belt's sparse inner edge into the larger asteroids' top four. Scoring against the lower side drops the 2:1 to a weak dip outside the resonance.
- **[Certain]** Counted by distance from the Sun, averaged over their orbits, the same asteroids show no notch at the resonances (93% to 108% of the belt on each side, 99.8% to 100.5% of both together), no groove has a dip in the bars, and the finder finds none. That is a calculation from Kepler's equation over the catalogued orbits, not a photograph; it is what a snapshot would show on average.
- **[Likely]** The H < 15 population is less shaped by which faint asteroids happen to have been found than the full catalogue is. The full catalogue has 0.83 asteroids in the inner belt (2.1 to 2.5 au) for each one in the outer belt (2.83 to 3.27 au); the larger ones have 0.35. Faint asteroids are easier to find nearer the Sun, which I expect is much of that difference, but I have not modelled discovery completeness, and part of it may be real.
- **[Likely]** Proper (long-term averaged) semi-major axes would show the gaps sharper than these osculating ones; Gallardo (2018) shows both kinds of histogram, but I read his review in extracts, and I did not compute proper elements.
- **[Likely]** Kirkwood announced the gaps, and Jupiter as their cause, in 1866. In his 1888 book *The Asteroids* he wrote that when the law was first stated in 1866 only ten asteroids had been found beyond 3 au. With today's orbits I count 13 of the 88 asteroids discovered before July 1866 (from the raw pages) and 14 of the 91 found by the year's end (from the bundled counts) beyond 3.0 au. I do not know which list or orbits he used, and I read his book only through search extracts.
- **[Speculative]** That holding the two rulers, by orbit size and by distance, makes the point that the gaps are in time and not in space land better than a chart does. That is the bet of the whole piece, and no one has held them yet.
- **Rough edges.** The 3D preview sorts triangles by depth, which can show small errors where a long ledge face meets a bar; the STL is not affected. There is no Braille or embossed lettering: the scale is read by counting ticks from the ends, which the page's profile labels. The resonance list in the finder stops at order 5 and *p* ≤ 9. Other resonances are real and not on it. Only six resonances have grooves: the four gaps JPL's histogram labels, the Hildas' 3:2 and the Trojans' 1:1. The 8:3 has none, although on the default ruler it is a notch 3.6 mm deep, deeper than the 7:3. I kept the grooves to the list I chose before looking rather than add one where the data turned out to dip, so that a groove over a notch still means a prediction that came true. The 9:4 at 3.029 au is a 0.9 mm edge on the default ruler. The depths in mm come from the layout's bar heights, which the tests check. I also sliced `default.stl` once with PrusaSlicer at the print check's settings (0.2 mm layers) and read the top of the bar row from the G-code: the 2:1 stretch prints as a dip one layer deep and 5 mm wide (z 4.75 mm from 3.27 to 3.32 au, 4.95 mm on either side), and the 8:3 notch 3.0 mm deep (18.75 mm against 21.75). That reading is not in the test suite.

## Tests

`node --test ideas/72-kirkwood-ruler/belt.test.mjs` runs `belt.test.mjs` (24 tests, Node built-ins only):

- **Data:** every population adds up to its total and nests inside the full catalogue bin by bin; the reduction's bin edges and population rules on hand-made rows; the time-averaged distance spread against brute-force sampling of Kepler's equation.
- **Kepler:** each resonance's period is exactly *p*/*q* of Jupiter's; the second route through Jupiter's semi-major axis and mass agrees within 0.0005 au.
- **Data against Kepler:** the blind finder's six notches on the default stretch, at both slice widths and with the two-sided score; the same six from 2,286 stretches with every start phase; the shift null over 106 shifts and the flat belt; its results for the other populations, as quoted above; the emptiness figures on each side, as quoted; the count by distance shows no notch.
- **The object against the words:** the notch depth in mm at every groove on the default ruler (three notches and an edge) and at the 2:1 on the square-root and log scales; every bar of the default ruler scanned for notches (exactly four of 1 mm or more, one without a groove); the twin on the ruler's height scale (same asteroids per mm³, 12.3 mm tall) and off it; fins counted as runs, against hand-made bars and the 1866 whole-belt case the first version undercounted.
- **The solid:** the default (size, triangle count, volume); bars against counts for all three scales; linear relief volume times asteroids per mm³ gives back the count within 0.2%; tick and groove positions and spacing over 300 random ranges; closure, Euler characteristic, volume, no zero-area triangles, bed contact and fit for the 17 extremes, the 7 presets, 400 random settings (both counts) and the 1,440- and 240-setting corner sweeps; input cleaning.
- **The file:** `default.stl` regenerated and compared byte for byte; its float32 edges pair up.
- **Not in the suite:** `crosscheck.py` (needs the raw pages and numpy; result above) and `sweep.mjs` (needs trimesh and PrusaSlicer; result under *Printing it*).
- **The words:** the figures quoted in this README and on the page (finder results, emptiness, notch depths, swing, 1866 bars, fins, print estimate), the tagline's four notches and an edge, the absence of the first version's overstatements, and the page's viewport tag.

`node tools/check.mjs ideas/72-kirkwood-ruler` runs these, the print check and the headless smoke test at 1280 px and 390 px. `node ideas/72-kirkwood-ruler/sweep.mjs` runs the print check on the 17 extremes (a few minutes; needs trimesh and PrusaSlicer).

## Where it could go next

- **Print it**, and find out whether a fingernail really does find the four notches, whether the 7:3, the shallowest at 2.6 mm, is felt or only seen, and whether the fall at the 2:1 reads as the end of the belt.
- **An 8:3 groove.** It would put a groove under every notch on the default ruler, at the cost of choosing a groove after seeing the data. A different colour or shape of groove for the ones added afterwards would keep the two apart.
- **A finer print.** The page can already make 2.1 to 3.3 au at 168 mm with 0.0075 au bins (1.05 mm), where the 8:3 dip at 2.705 au shows in the profile. Whether a printer and a fingertip resolve it is untested.
- **Proper elements** from the asteroid families literature, as a sixth population, to see how much sharper the gaps get.
- **Braille labels** on the ledge for the whole-au ticks and the resonances, checked with someone who reads Braille.
- **Saturn's rings**, the candidate I put aside: the same idea, with the outer edge of the B ring at a resonance with Mimas.

## Revision note

An independent reviewer fetched the catalogue again and reduced it in its own code. It reproduced the data, the resonance positions, the meshes and the downloaded file, and found one serious problem and five smaller ones. I reproduced each with my own code before changing anything, and all of them held. Checking the first one turned up a sixth problem the review had not seen.

- **The default ruler does not drop into the four notches it promised.** At the 2:1 the bars have already fallen to the floor (0.16 to 0.22 mm of relief from 3.27 to 3.31 au) and stay there: it is the outer edge of the belt, not a notch, and sliced at 0.2 mm it is a dip one layer deep. Scanning every bar of the default ruler with the same measure then showed a notch the first version never mentioned: 3.6 mm deep at 2.70 au, the 8:3 resonance, with no groove under it. The default has four notches of 1 mm or more (3:1, 8:3, 5:2, 7:3) and an edge at the 2:1. The tagline changed from "drops into four notches … says where they are" to "drops into four notches, then falls away where the main belt ends … says where all five are", and the page, the first preset's name and this README now say which notch has a groove and which groove has no notch. The page measures this for any setting: `grooveDepth` gives the depth in mm at each groove, and `objectNotches` lists every notch on the object, groove or none. I kept the grooves to the list chosen before looking, and did not add one at the 8:3.
- **The notch finder depended on where the stretch started.** It cut its slices from the start of the stretch, and one slider step (1.8 to 1.8025 au) changed its fourth notch from the 2:1 to 2.705 au, which it then matched to the 5:2, 0.12 au away. It now looks at every 0.005 au slice that starts on a 0.0025 au bin, and gives the same six notches from all 2,286 stretches tested. With that fixed, its fourth notch is the 8:3 and the 2:1 is fifth, so its list of resonances now runs to order 5 (24 entries, 8 in the default stretch) instead of 4 (20), and the page shows six notches instead of four. "The same four from any stretch" is gone.
- **The 2:1's emptiness averaged an edge.** "2:1 holds 16% of the level around it" averaged a dense inner side with a sparse outer one; on its own the outer side holds 98%. Emptiness is now reported for each side, and the page flags a step when the two sides differ more than threefold, both in the grooves table and in the finder's list (the 2:1 notch: 91% below the belt inside it, 17% below the belt outside it). Scored against the lower side, the 2:1 is only a weak dip 0.021 au outside the resonance.
- **The two "twin" rulers were not on the same height scale.** Each was scaled to its own tallest bar, so each cubic millimetre of the twin's relief stood for 43 asteroids against the ruler's 77. Counted by distance, the page now uses the height scale of the count by orbit size over the same stretch, unless that would overshoot the relief (a checkbox, `?match=0`, turns it off). The twin is now 12.3 mm tall instead of 22, and the README says it holds fewer asteroids (1,383,202 on average against 1,512,033) because eccentric orbits spend part of their year outside the stretch.
- **The controls could not fail, and "20 resonances" overstated the test.** Shifting the catalogue by 0.05 au, with resonances at least 0.13 au apart, cannot leave a notch on one, and a flat belt has no notches to find. The tests now slide the catalogue by 106 amounts from 0.02 to 0.15 au each way: shifted, at most one of the six notches landed within 0.006 au of a listed resonance, against all six unshifted. The README also gives the chance of the match if the notches fell at random (about 5 × 10<sup>−8</sup> for six) and says how many listed resonances lie in the stretch.
- **Fins were undercounted, and one fin count used the wrong bin width.** The counter saw only single bars under 2 mm, so it missed pairs that stand free together. It now counts runs of bars under 3 mm wide in all that stand 8 mm clear, which finds 7 fins instead of 6 in the 1866 whole-belt case. The README's 19 fins between 4.4 and 4.9 au are at 0.005 au bins, the finest the page allows there, not 0.0025 au. The page still only warns about fins, and the honest assessment now says the print check passes 1.6 mm fins standing 33 mm clear.

`belt.test.mjs` grew from 18 to 24 tests. The new ones pin the notch depths in mm and the scan of every bar on the default object, the finder's independence of where the stretch starts, the shift null, the emptiness on each side, the shared height scale and the fin runs, and the words above; each of those fails on the first version. One more, a corner sweep counted by distance with the shared height scale on and off, checks that every such solid closes. `default.stl` did not change: the fixes are in what the page measures and says, not in the default object.
