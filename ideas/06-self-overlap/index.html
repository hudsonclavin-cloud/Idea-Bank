The Pattern That Trips Over Itself

A hands-on essay about coin flips, waiting, and what happens when a pattern overlaps itself.

1 An even-looking wager

Flip a fair coin, over and over. I’m waiting for two heads in a row: HH. You’re waiting for a head followed by a tail: HT. Take any two consecutive flips and they are HH with probability ¼ and HT with probability ¼. The coin plays no favorites.

So, on average, which of us waits longer?

Run thousands of games and the averages settle near 6 flips for HH and 4 for HT. Those are the exact answers, not quirks of the random numbers. Waiting for HH takes 50% longer.

The panel’s fourth number deserves a second look. Each game hunts for both pairs in the same flips, and HT turns up first in about 50% of them. Head to head, neither pair is favored: both need a head first, and once that head appears, the very next flip decides. HH loses no more races than HT does, yet it keeps you waiting longer on average. Both facts are true. By the end of the page they should feel obvious.

2 Same count, different rhythm

If HH is slower to arrive, maybe it’s rarer? It isn’t. Here are 160 flips, with every HH marked above the coins and every HT marked below.

Press New flips a few times. Neither pair is reliably ahead: in 160 flips, each is expected 39.75 times. What differs is the rhythm. Two HTs can never overlap, because a pair that ends in T can’t also be the start of one that begins with H, so HTs always sit at least two flips apart. HHs can share a coin: three heads in a row contain two of them, four heads contain three. HH comes in clumps.

Clumps are the whole story. Each pair turns up at one position in four over the long run, so for both of them the average gap between occurrences is 4 flips. But half of HH’s gaps are a single flip: one more head, right after an HH. For the average to stay at 4, the remaining gaps must be long, and they average 7.

Now ask where a fresh game starts. Not inside a clump. With no flips behind you, you’re exactly where you’d be just after a clump ends in a T: at the start of a drought. A drought gap is 7 flips on average, and the first of those is the T itself, so from a standing start HH takes 7 − 1 = 6. HT has no clumps to ride. Every gap between HTs starts from scratch, so its standing-start wait is just its average gap, 4.

Clumps also make HH’s count swing more from strip to strip. Over 160 flips its standard deviation is about 7.0, against 3.2 for HT. Same average, much less predictable.

3 Slide the pattern across itself

The same bookkeeping works for any pattern, and there is a way to do it at a glance, usually credited to John Conway. Lay a copy of the pattern under itself and slide it right, one coin at a time. At each shift, look only at the coins that overlap. If every overlapping pair agrees, the pattern overlaps itself there, and that overlap is worth 2k, where k is the number of coins in it. Add up the values, including the starting position, where the copy covers all n coins and is worth 2n. The total is the average number of flips you’ll wait.

Drag the lower copy sideways. To change the pattern, tap its coins at the top of the panel.

Our two pairs first. HT never overlaps itself, so it costs just 22 = 4. HH overlaps itself on a single head, worth 21, for a total of 6. That extra 2 is the price of clumping.

A few more. HHT never overlaps itself: exactly 8. HTH starts and ends with H, a one-coin overlap: 8 + 2 = 10. HHHH overlaps at every shift: 16 + 8 + 4 + 2 = 30. Among patterns of n flips, the ones that never overlap themselves are the quickest, at exactly 2n; all heads and all tails are the slowest, at 2n+1 − 2.

A puzzle, if you want one: find a five-flip pattern with an average wait of exactly 36. (It has to overlap itself on two coins and nowhere else.)

4 Why sliding works: a casino full of gamblers

The sliding rule looks like numerology. Here is an argument, based on a 1980 proof by Shuo-Yen Robert Li, that makes it look inevitable.

Picture a fair casino. Just before each flip, a new gambler walks in with $1 and bets it on the first letter of the pattern. If she’s right, her dollar becomes $2, and she bets all of it on the second letter; then all of that on the third; and so on. One wrong call and she’s broke. Every bet is double-or-nothing on a fair coin, so nobody in the room has an edge.

Play until the pattern appears, at flip T. The gamblers have brought in a dollar apiece: T dollars in all. What do they walk out with? Anyone who made a wrong call has nothing. The one who arrived n flips before the end has called the whole pattern and holds 2n. Any other survivor arrived k flips before the end and has called k flips in a row, so the last k flips match the first k letters of the pattern. But the pattern just appeared, so the last k flips are also its last k letters. She is still standing exactly when the pattern’s last k letters equal its first k, which is a self-overlap, and she holds 2k.

Drag through a game. Each row is one gambler, and each cell is one of her bets.

Look at the survivors when the game ends: their rows are the slid copies from the last section, standing on the flips that finished the game. And their winnings don’t depend on luck at all. Every game of HTH ends with exactly $8 + $2 = $10 in the survivors’ hands. Since the game is fair, what the gamblers take out must, on average, equal what they put in.1 They put in T dollars. So the average of T is 10. The sliding rule is this ledger, laid flat.

5 Races, and why going second wins

Now the head-to-head puzzle from the first panel. A pattern’s average wait depends only on how it overlaps itself. A race between two patterns also depends on how they overlap each other. Conway’s rule stretches to cover it. Write X·Y for the sum of 2k over every k where the last k letters of X equal the first k letters of Y, so X·X is X’s average wait. Then, for two different patterns of the same length, the odds that B shows up before A are (A·A − A·B) to (B·B − B·A).

For HH against HT: HH·HH = 6, HH·HT = 2, HT·HT = 4 and HT·HH = 0. The odds are (6 − 2) to (4 − 0): 4 to 4, an even race, just as the first panel found.

Here is the race in words, and it settles the puzzle. Once the first head appears, the next flip decides the race, a fair toss. But the two ways of losing are not alike. If HT loses, the coin is showing H, so HT needs only the next tail, 2 more flips on average. If HH loses, the coin is showing T, and HH must start from nothing: 6 more. Equal chances of winning, unequal costs of losing. That is enough to get both averages. The first head arrives on flip 2 on average, one more flip settles the race, and each pattern loses half the time, paying its extra wait only then. So HH takes 2 + 1 + 6/2 = 6 and HT takes 2 + 1 + 2/2 = 4.

Walter Penney turned this into a game in 1969. You choose a pattern of three flips. I choose mine after seeing yours. We flip until one of them appears. It sounds fair, and it isn’t: whatever you choose, I can pick a pattern that comes first at least 2/3 of the time.

For three flips the counter has a simple recipe: take the first two letters of your pattern, and put the opposite of your middle letter in front of them. So HHT is beaten by THH, 3 times in 4. That one is easy to see. HHT needs two heads in a row first, and unless the game opens with HH, the first HH is preceded by a T, which means THH has just won.

Since every pattern has a counter, there is no best pattern, only a loop:

It works like rock-paper-scissors, with four throws instead of three. I checked by exhaustive computation that every pattern of 3 to 8 flips can be beaten by some pattern of the same length; Guibas and Odlyzko worked out the general theory in 1981. At two flips the trick fails: nothing beats HT or TH, and the best you can do is tie.

Two more results that look wrong and aren’t:

  • HHT and THH have the same average wait, 8 flips. Yet THH comes first 3 times in 4.
  • THTH takes 20 flips on average and HTHH takes 18. In a race, the slower THTH comes first 9 times in 14.

A wait depends only on how a pattern overlaps itself. A race also depends on how two patterns overlap each other. They are different questions, and the coin answers each one honestly.

6 Where the overlap shows up

Self-overlap matters wherever people search long sequences for patterns. The Knuth–Morris–Pratt string-search algorithm (1977) begins by computing, for each prefix of the word it is looking for, the longest overlap of that prefix with itself, short of the whole thing. That table lets the search slide ahead after a mismatch without missing a match or re-reading text. People who count short “words” in DNA run into the clumping from section 2: a self-overlapping word’s count swings more than a non-overlapping word’s, so a significance test that ignores this can mistake ordinary clumps for signal.

The coin, meanwhile, was fair the whole time. Every pair of flips was HH one time in four and HT one time in four. Overlap doesn’t change how often a pattern happens. It changes how those happenings are spaced out in time, and waiting is all about spacing.
