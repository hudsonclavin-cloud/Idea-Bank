# Hinge
> State what you believe as ranges and chances. Hinge finds the belief your decision actually turns on, what it would be worth to find out before choosing, and what would have to be true for you to be wrong.

**Kind:** tool
**Run:** Open `ideas/18-hinge/index.html` in a browser. Plain classic scripts, so double-clicking the file works; no server, build, network or install. Tests, from the repo root: `node --test ideas/18-hinge/hinge.test.mjs` (pure Node, about 20 s) and `node ideas/18-hinge/ui-check.mjs` (drives the page in headless Chromium through the globally installed Playwright that `tools/smoke.mjs` uses).
**Made by:** Gudgeon

## What it is

A thinking tool for one kind of problem: *I have to choose, and the choice depends on several things I am unsure about.*

You write the things you are unsure about as **assumptions**: a *range* you would bet 90% on (`exit_value: 100M to 1B`), a *chance* (`exit: 40%`), a *fixed* fact (`years: 4`), or a *formula* that derives one quantity from others (`payment = loan * r / (1 - (1 + r)^-360)`). Then you write each **option** as a formula over those names, such as `Stay = stay_comp * years` and `Startup = startup_comp * years + if(survives and exit, equity * exit_value, 0)`. One option is an estimate; two to four are a decision.

Hinge then shows you four things you cannot work out in your head once there are more than two uncertain inputs:

1. **The outcomes.** The distribution of each option, which one is best on average, and how often it actually comes out ahead. These two can disagree, and when they do, that is the most important thing on the page.
2. **Where it hinges.** For every uncertain assumption: what it would be **worth to learn it exactly** before choosing (its value of information, in your own units), the **values at which the best option switches**, and the **chance** your own range puts on those values. Assumptions that cannot change the answer on their own say so.
3. **The hinge curve.** Tap an assumption and see the expected outcome of each option as a function of that one assumption, with the switch point marked and your own belief about the assumption drawn underneath it. Where the lines cross is what would have to be true.
4. **A plain-language verdict** at the top, written from the numbers: *"On expected value, Startup beats Stay by $65k. But it comes out ahead in only 24% of runs… The one thing most worth finding out is exit, worth $66k on its own. The most likely single thing to change the answer: exit, if it does not happen (60%), and then Stay wins. stay_comp, startup_comp and gap_months cannot change it alone, however they turn out."*

For a single estimate the same machinery shows where the uncertainty comes from (each input's share of the variance, and how far the expected outcome moves between the input's 5th and 95th percentile) and the chance of landing under a target.

Everything stays in the browser: cases live in `localStorage` (the page works without it), and can be exported and imported as JSON. Three worked examples are built in, and the first one loads on first visit.

### The three decisions I tried it on

- **Take the startup offer, or stay?** (loads first). Expected value favours the startup by about $67k over four years, but the startup comes out ahead in only about 24% of runs: the average is carried by a 24% chance of a large equity payout, and most of the time you end up $100k behind. Learning whether there will be an exit is worth $66k on its own; it is also the one thing you cannot learn. The salary, the thing you would actually negotiate, cannot flip the decision anywhere in its range. The equity can, if it ends up below about 0.11% (a 10% chance). This changed how I would negotiate: not the salary, the equity and the survival odds.
- **Buy the place, or keep renting?** Renting is cheaper by about $33k over five years on expectation, and buying wins in 26% of runs. Of the five uncertain inputs, exactly one can flip the answer on its own: house-price appreciation, which would have to run above about 4% a year (a 25% chance). Maintenance, selling costs, rent growth and what the down payment would earn elsewhere all carry real uncertainty and none of it matters to the choice. I had expected the investment return to be the hinge. It carries 7% of the variance of the margin and cannot flip the decision.
- **How long will the rewrite take?** I had promised twelve weeks. Median 12.7 weeks, mean 13.9, and a 45% chance of making it. The hours per feature, which nobody had discussed, is 57% of the variance; the number of features, which we had argued about for an hour, is 12%; the "weeks lost to surprises" line is 2%.

## Why I made it

I notice that when people (and I) reason about a decision, we argue about the number that is easiest to argue about, not the one the conclusion depends on. A salary is easy to haggle; the probability of an exit is not, so the conversation drifts to the salary even when the salary is irrelevant. The question I find most useful in any decision is *what would have to be true for me to be wrong?*, and the honest answer depends on the whole joint distribution of everything else I believe, which is exactly what a head cannot hold once there are more than two uncertain inputs. A computer can hold it, and the answer has a precise form: for each assumption, the set of values at which the expected best choice switches, and how likely you yourself think those values are.

The second thing I wanted was a number for *should I keep researching, or decide?* The value of perfect information is that number: the most that any investigation into an assumption could possibly be worth. When it says $0 for the thing you have been reading about all week, that is a result.

The third is the part that can only be felt. In the startup example, expected value says go and probability says you will most likely regret it, and both are true at once. A table of numbers does not make you feel that. Seeing the fat orange tail on one strip and the narrow blue block on the other, and then the line that says "ahead in 24% of runs", does. The proof is in the tests; the feeling is in the strips.

## How it works

**Assumptions become distributions.** A *range* `lo to hi` is a 90% interval: lognormal with `ln lo` and `ln hi` at the 5th and 95th percentiles when `lo > 0` (you are unsure to within a *factor*), normal otherwise; `uniform` makes `lo` and `hi` hard limits; the shape can be overridden. The 5th/95th percentiles sit at ±1.6449 standard deviations. A *chance* `p` is 1 with probability `p` and 0 otherwise. Assumptions are drawn **independently**. Formula rows are evaluated in dependency order (any order on the page is fine; cycles are reported).

**Simulation.** `n` joint samples (5,000 by default, 20,000 with the "four times" box) by Latin hypercube sampling: each uncertain row gets its own random permutation of the `n` equal-probability strata, so every slice of every input is hit exactly once, which steadies the means. The generator is seeded (mulberry32); the seed is shown and "Re-roll" changes it. Every option is evaluated on every sample.

**Decision quantities.** Options are compared as *values* (negated when smaller is better). The **best** option is the one with the highest mean value; **best in** is the fraction of samples in which that option attains the maximum. The **value of perfect information** is `E[max_j V_j] − max_j E[V_j]`: what you would gain on average by choosing after learning every assumption.

**Per-assumption analysis** (the "Where it hinges" table), for each range or chance row `i`, with common random numbers (every other row keeps the same `n` draws):

- The row is pinned to a value `x` and the downstream formula rows and options are re-evaluated on all `n` samples, giving the *conditional means* `m_j(x) = E[V_j | x_i = x]`. These are the hinge curves; they are drawn at 36 points (the medians of 32 equal-probability slices plus the 0.1%, 5%, 95% and 99.9% quantiles).
- The row's distribution is cut into `G = 32` equal-probability **slices** (for a chance row, its two states). Every sample's own uniform draw for that row is mapped into slice `g`, so the slice mean `m_j(g) = E[V_j | x_i ∈ slice g]` is exact up to Monte Carlo error. Then
  - **Worth knowing** (partial value of information) `= Σ_g w_g · max_j m_j(g) − max_j Σ_g w_g · m_j(g)`, the value of learning *which slice* the assumption falls in before choosing. It is a lower bound on the exact partial EVPI (coarser information is worth less); for a normal input 32 slices keep over 97% of it, and for a lognormal with σ = 0.7 (a factor-of-10 range) about 88%, both checked in the tests.
  - **Share of uncertainty** `= Var_g(E[D | slice g]) / Var(D)`, a first-order Sobol index of the sliced input, where `D` is the margin of the best option over the runner-up in a decision, or the outcome itself in an estimate. The shares of an additive model sum to about one; the gap below one is interaction.
- **The answer changes if…** is the set of values `x` where `argmax_j m_j(x)` is not the overall best. The sign of `m_best(x) − max_{j≠best} m_j(x)` is scanned at the 36 grid points and each sign change is bisected in quantile space (20 steps). The result is a union of quantile intervals, reported as *below / above / between / outside* values, and its **chance** is the total length of those intervals, i.e. the probability your own distribution puts on them. For a chance row the two states are checked directly. In the table the rows are sorted by worth knowing; values below 0.5% of the total EVPI are shown as ≈0, because the maximum of noisy means is biased slightly upward.
- **Swing** (estimates) is `m(q95) − m(q05)`: how far the expected outcome moves between the input's 5th and 95th percentile.

**Formulas** use a small expression language: `+ − * / ^`, parentheses, comparisons, `and / or / not`, `if(test, a, b)`, `min max abs sqrt ln log10 exp round floor ceil clamp pow`, and number suffixes `k M B %`. The parser produces an AST; `emitJS` turns the AST into plain arithmetic source (only numbers, whitelisted operators and functions, and indexed reads) which is compiled with `Function` for speed, with a closure interpreter as the fallback; the tests check that both agree. Names can be renamed in the editor and every formula that uses them follows.

**Files.** `js/hinge.js` is the whole engine with no DOM (distributions, parser, compiler, simulation, analysis, narration, import/export normalisation); the page loads it as a script and `hinge.test.mjs` imports it under Node. `js/examples.js` holds the three cases. `js/app.js` is the editor, storage and SVG rendering. Charts are hand-drawn SVG: distribution strips with a faint histogram, the 5–95% line, the 25–75% box, median tick and mean dot; and the hinge curve with the switch band, a crosshair readout, and a table twin behind a disclosure.

## Honest assessment

What works:

- [Certain] `hinge.test.mjs` passes 20 tests. Against closed forms: the normal CDF/quantile pair (known values, an independent approximation, round trips); 90% ranges land on the 5th/95th percentiles and densities integrate to one; the LHS columns are exact permutations; a linear-normal decision reproduces P(best), total EVPI, the sliced partial EVPI, the flip threshold (x = 6, to 1e-3) and its probability; a two-input linear model splits partial EVPI and variance shares the way the closed form says; a chance variable gives the exact partial EVPI (18 on the nose) and flip state; quadratic models produce correct two-crossing flip sets (*between* and *outside*); a lognormal product reproduces its median, mean, 5th/95th percentiles, the probability under a target, the sliced Sobol indices, and the swing. Against an independent Monte Carlo (xoshiro128**, Box–Muller, no stratification, 100,000–200,000 draws): the startup example's P(best) and total EVPI, the house example's appreciation threshold (E[Buy | a*] ≈ E[Rent] within 1%), and the rewrite example's median, mean and P(≤ 12 weeks). The startup example's expected values and the partial EVPIs of its chance rows are recomputed from the lognormal means in closed form; the house example's formulas are checked at the medians against a separate implementation of the mortgage arithmetic. Structural checks: "smaller is better" is the exact mirror of "bigger is better"; the house case with every formula row inlined gives identical numbers (this test caught a real bug during development: after pinning a row, its downstream formula rows were left corrupted for the next row's analysis).
- [Certain] `ui-check.mjs` passes 44 checks in headless Chromium: building a case from a blank through the real inputs, rename-with-refactor, inline formula errors with stale results, edits changing the outcome, tapping rows, hover and touch readouts, the two-step row delete, reload with persistence, export, duplicate, confirmed delete (dismissed and accepted), switching cases, loading examples, importing the exported file in a fresh browser, refusing junk, running with `localStorage` disabled, a phone viewport in dark mode with reduced motion, and a dozen blind button clicks that cannot delete anything. `tools/smoke.mjs` passes at desktop and phone width with no console errors or overflow.
- [Certain] Recomputing with other seeds moves the headline numbers by about the amount you would expect at n = 5,000: the startup margin by ±3k on 67k, "ahead in" by ±0.5 points, the exit-value threshold by ±3M on 250M.

Where the method misleads, and what is rough:

- [Certain] **Independence.** Every assumption is drawn independently. If two of yours move together (rent growth and house appreciation, say), the tool understates or overstates the risk, and the per-row analysis attributes the effect to whichever one you ask about. The workaround is to derive one from the other with a formula row; a proper correlation control is the first thing I would add.
- [Certain] **Expected value is the criterion.** "Best" means best on average, which is the right rule for a decision you will face many times and the wrong one for a decision you cannot afford to lose. The startup example is the case in point: Hinge says the startup is best and, in the same breath, that you will probably be worse off. It shows you both so you can apply your own appetite for risk; it does not model it.
- [Certain] **One-at-a-time.** "Cannot flip it alone" means exactly that. Combinations can flip it, and the difference between the total value of information and the largest partial is the measure of how much lives in combinations (in the house example $9.0k versus $8.0k; in the startup example $86k versus $66k). The flip scan can also miss a switch region narrower than one slice (about 3% of the distribution) that sits between two grid points, which only happens with non-monotone models.
- [Certain] **Perfect information.** The "worth knowing" figures are for learning an assumption *exactly*. Any real investigation gives imperfect information and is worth less, so treat the figure as a ceiling. And the sliced estimator is a little below the exact figure for wide lognormal inputs (see above).
- [Certain] **Your ranges, your answer.** A positive range is read as lognormal, which puts a heavy right tail on it: `exit_value: 100M to 1B` has a 1-in-100 tail at $1.6B and a mean 28% above its median. That is the right shape for "unsure to within a factor" and the wrong one for "somewhere around here", and the auto choice is announced under each row with its median and tail so you can switch to normal when it is wrong. People's 90% intervals are also usually too narrow, so the real hinge is often outside the range you typed.
- [Certain] Monte Carlo wobble: at n = 5,000 percentages are good to about a point, and the raw partial EVPI of an irrelevant row is a few hundred dollars on an $86k total rather than exactly zero (the page prints ≈0 below 0.5% of the total). Four times the samples cuts both in half.
- [Likely] Recomputing takes 0.2–0.6 s for the built-in cases on a laptop and two to three times that on a phone, so typing in a formula is debounced rather than instant.
- [Certain] The example numbers are my own guesses chosen to make the method visible, not advice about jobs, houses or schedules. Four options at most; no categorical assumptions with more than two states (use two chances or a formula).
- [Speculative] I have only looked at the page in headless Chromium. The layout uses nothing exotic, but Safari and Firefox are unverified.

## Where it could go next

- **Correlation.** A "moves with" link between two rows (a Gaussian copula with a slider), so rent growth and appreciation can be tied without writing a formula.
- **Risk.** One knob for loss aversion turning each option into a certainty equivalent, so the startup example can say what the equity is worth *to you*.
- **Imperfect information.** "A test that is right 80% of the time" or "a reference class of 30 similar startups": the value of sample information, so "should I spend a week finding out?" gets a number.
- **Two people, one model.** Each gives their own ranges; the tool finds the assumption that accounts for the disagreement, the crux.
- **Combinations.** Pairwise values of information and second-order shares, to show which *pairs* flip the answer when no single row can.
- **Sharing by URL.** A case packed into the hash, so a hinge can be sent as a link.
