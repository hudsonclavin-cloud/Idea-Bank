# Does the River Spring Forward?
> Arizona doesn't change its clocks, but the Colorado below Glen Canyon Dam rises and falls each day with the electricity demand of states that do. When their clocks jump an hour, does the river? Not by the hour, says a test registered before it was run on this river: its interval stops at 33.5 minutes, which the rule written in advance calls keeping standard time. Looked at more closely afterwards, the March days move about a third of an hour, and none of the eighteen Marches moved more than 39 minutes.

**Kind:** data essay
**Run:** open `ideas/75-spring-forward/index.html` in a browser (classic scripts, works from `file://`, no network, nothing stored). Tests, from the repo root: `node --test ideas/75-spring-forward/*.test.mjs` (Node built-ins only, about 20 s). To rebuild the bundled data from USGS (needs network): `node ideas/75-spring-forward/data/fetch.mjs --cache DIR`.

## What it is

Lees Ferry is the gauge on the Colorado River about 15.5 river miles below Glen Canyon Dam (Topping, Schmidt and Vierra, 2003, USGS Professional Paper 1677). The dam releases more water when more electricity is wanted, so the river there rises and falls every day. Arizona stays on Mountain Standard Time all year. Utah, whose border is nine miles north of the gauge, and the Navajo Nation change their clocks (National Park Service, Glen Canyon NRA FAQ). The dam's power is marketed by the Western Area Power Administration; its Colorado River Storage Project customer list has customers in Arizona and at least five other western states, all of which change their clocks: Colorado, New Mexico, Utah, Nevada and Wyoming. The list I consulted may not be complete. So the question: when those clocks spring forward, does the river's daily tide move an hour too?

I measured it with eighteen years of USGS readings (2008–2025), against six other rivers whose answers should be known. The page shows:

1. **Two clocks, one river.** The average March day at Lees Ferry before and after the change, and two clock faces, Arizona's and Utah's, pointing at the moment the river climbs past its daily average. Before the change both read 9:39 am. After it Arizona's reads 9:17 and Utah's 10:17.
2. **Every day, two weeks either side.** A day-by-hour picture of the 29 days around each change, for any year or the average, on Arizona's clock or Utah's, with each day's mean flow.
3. **The registered answer**, for all seven rivers, with the decision rule drawn in.
4. **Same dates, other years**: the 2007 change in the law as a natural experiment, with what a simulated clock-keeper scores on it and each era at its own dates.
5. **March and November, apart**: the exploratory part, year by year, with each year's two median days.
6. **The method that failed first**, which the page re-runs on request.
7. **What I knew, and when**: the timeline of what was decided and seen, from the session log.

## Why I made it

I didn't choose this question; I inherited it. The first session on entry 75 chose it, wrote the pre-registration, fetched the data and ran both methods, then ran out of usage before it had a page or a README. Its log records the data sources it probed but not the other ideas it weighed, so I can't honestly say what it turned down. Before going on I sketched two alternatives I could have started instead: how much later the first autumn frost now comes in a few long-record towns, and whether earthquake catalogues list more small quakes on weekdays (mostly a question about people). I kept the river for one reason above the others. A pre-registration that has been run is a promise, and dropping it after seeing an awkward result is the file drawer the pre-registration exists to prevent. It also helped that the question is good. A river below a dam is a record of other people's days. Arizona refusing daylight time while selling hydropower to people who keep it gives an unusually clean test of whose day that is.

The proven part is the measurement, with intervals, controls and a rule fixed in advance. The felt part is what the page's two clocks try to show. When the clocks change nine miles away, the water at Lees Ferry follows the people only partly. In March its whole day comes about twenty minutes sooner, a third of the way to the clock, so it ends up nearer the Sun's hours than the clock's.

**Did the answer disappoint me?** The first session guessed "most of the hour, perhaps 40 to 60 minutes". The registered answer is nearer zero, and I was disappointed. A river obeying clocks in a state that has none would have made the better headline. Then the March pattern turned up: the day moving about a third of the hour, earlier in 17 Marches of 18. I find a river that follows other people's clocks part of the way more interesting than either clean answer, though it is exploratory and I have kept it labelled that way. (The first version also said the evening stayed put while the morning moved. The review showed that was one flat feature picked from a day that moved together; see the revision note.)

## How it works

**Data.** USGS instantaneous values from the USGS Water Data OGC API (`api.waterdata.usgs.gov`, collection `continuous`): discharge for six gauges and water temperature for the Delaware. Every value is used as published, provisional ones included. Times come in UTC. Each request covers one season of one year, from 51 days before the earliest centre it serves to 51 days after the latest. `data/manifest.txt` lists all 360 requests with their reading counts and the first 16 hex digits of each response's SHA-256. The bundle (`data/rivers.js`, 167 KB) is a reduced copy: the median days the second method needs, the first method's daily records for the real windows, and every day's hourly shape and mean flow around the Lees Ferry changes (for the picture). `data/fixtures/` holds responses from both USGS services (the legacy files without their comment header) for 8–9 March and 1 November 2015 at Buford Dam and 1 November 2015 at Lees Ferry.

**Shared steps.** Hourly means in the site's own standard time, fixed all year (UTC−7 for Arizona and Utah, −6 Texas, −5 Georgia and New Jersey). Gaps up to 3 hours are filled linearly. A centred 2×24-hour moving average is subtracted, which leaves a 24-hour cycle untouched. Days run midnight to midnight in standard time.

**Clock times.** Lees Ferry is read every quarter hour, at :00, :15, :30 and :45, so an hourly mean stands for the moment 22.5 minutes past the hour. The clock faces, the day pictures and every time of day in this README place it there (`HOUR_CENTRE` in `rivertime.js`). Steps don't depend on it: a common offset cancels when two days are compared. Rebuilding the average March day from the raw 15-minute readings, each at its own instant, puts the morning mean-crossing at 09:38 before the change and 09:16 after, within a minute of the page.

**Method 1 (registered first, failed).** Each day's 24-hour Fourier component gives a crest time. A robust weighted regression of crest time on a drift, a step on the night of the change and weekday terms, over the 14 days either side (the change-over Sunday left out), gives the step. It fails at peaking dams, whose crest time jumps by about twelve hours between morning-block and evening-block days.

**Method 2 (registered in part 2, before it was run on Lees Ferry).** Each day's 24 residuals are scaled to unit root-mean-square. The hour-by-hour median day is taken over the 14 days before and, separately, the 14 after (at least 7 usable days a side). The step is the shift, in whole minutes within ±180, that maximises the circular cross-correlation of the two median days, each read as a smooth curve through its hourly values (harmonics 1 to 11). Per year, *K* = (fall step − spring step)/2. Fake changes 35 days before and after each real one give placebo *K*s. The registered statistic is *K*<sub>c</sub> = *K* − (placebo<sub>−35</sub> + placebo<sub>+35</sub>)/2, averaged over 2008–2025 with a 95% t-interval.

**Decision rule** (amended before any data): an interval reaching within 10 minutes of 60 and not within 10 of 0 is *keeps clock time*. One within 10 of 0 and not within 10 of 60 is *keeps standard time*. Reaching neither is *keeps part of the hour*; reaching both is *cannot tell*.

**Law-change test (registered as supporting).** The 2007 law (Energy Policy Act of 2005, section 110) moved the changes from the first Sunday of April and the last Sunday of October to the second Sunday of March and the first Sunday of November. At the new dates the test takes *K*(2008–2025) − *K*(1990–2006); at the old dates, *K*(1990–2006) − *K*(2008–2025); each with a Welch interval. Calendar effects cancel. Part 2 says a clock-keeper gives about 60 in both. It can't: the last Sunday of October and the first Sunday of November are always exactly 7 days apart, so in each era the 14-day window at the fall date that was not a change date holds the other rule's real change (the spring dates are 21 or 28 days apart and don't overlap). `simulateClockKeeper()` runs a river that keeps clock time exactly through the same steps: Lees Ferry's average March and November days, turned an hour earlier on daylight-time days, plus noise set so its median days are as alike as Lees Ferry's (coherence 0.98 in both). It scores 45.1 at the new dates and 40.7 at the old. In the variants I tried (noise from 0.05 to 0.5, coherence 0.91 to 0.999, and a plain cosine for the day), the first column stayed between 44 and 46 and the second ran from 33 to 44, lower the smoother the days. A river that keeps standard time scores 0 in both.

**Code.** `rivertime.js` holds all the arithmetic as pure functions: no DOM, no clock, and no randomness except one seeded generator for the clock-keeper simulation and the bootstrap, so every run gives the same numbers. It loads as a classic script in the page and with `require` in Node. `report()` computes every number the page shows. `app.js` only draws and formats, except that it runs method 1 through `rivertime.js` when asked. The page throttles every large redraw to at most about three a second, whatever the input (taps, held keys, the year menu). With `prefers-reduced-motion` the clock hands jump instead of turning. Colours follow the system's light or dark setting.

## Honest assessment

What the data say:

- **[Certain]** By the rule registered in advance, the Colorado at Lees Ferry *keeps standard time*: *K*<sub>c</sub> 5.4 (95% interval −22.8 to 33.5), uncorrected *K* 3.5 (95% interval −18.7 to 25.7), over 2008–2025. That is a statement about the rule applied to this data, which separate code reproduces. The label says less than it sounds: it means the interval reaches within 10 minutes of 0 and stops more than 10 short of 60, and this one still allows 33.5 minutes, over half the hour.
- **[Likely]** The river does not keep the whole hour. The registered *K*<sub>c</sub>, the uncorrected *K*, the law test at the March and November dates and the March steps on their own all keep Lees Ferry well short of 60. Four things don't rule the hour out: the failed first method (interval up to 78.7), the law test at the April and October dates (47.2, up to 71.4, which is about where a clock-keeper lands there and is mostly about 1990–2006; below), the exploratory 2013–2025 *K*<sub>c</sub> (up to 53.2), and the cut that keeps only the quiet years and drops 2021 (*K*<sub>c</sub> 30.5, 95% interval 10.5 to 50.6, below), whose interval reaches the hour band.
- **[Likely]** It keeps part of the hour in March. The step was earlier in 17 of 18 years, by 19.2 minutes on average (95% interval −25.9 to −12.5, as a signed step). Fake March changes five weeks either side average 0.2 (95% interval −6.6 to 7.1) and −2.8 (95% interval −8.6 to 3.0). The same March dates in 1990–2006, when the clocks didn't change on them, average −1.1 (95% interval −11.6 to 9.4). The same-dates difference is −18.1 (−30.2 to −6.1). This is exploratory, and I chose to look at seasons separately after seeing the registered result.
- **[Likely]** The November steps of 2008–2012 are meaningless. Reclamation ran steady flows through September and October in those years (the Fall Steady Experimental Flows prescribed by the 2008 biological opinion, according to its Adaptive Management Program documents, which I found by search). In the data, the two weeks before the November change have almost no daily rise and fall in 2008, 2009, 2011 and 2012. Their median days' coherence (the root-mean-square of the median of days scaled to unit size) is 0.18 to 0.48, against 0.84 or more for every other November's before-window, and their steps are −180, −70, −131 and −178. Leaving out 2008–2012, *K* is 21.6 (95% interval 4.8 to 38.5) and *K*<sub>c</sub> 28.6 (95% interval 4.0 to 53.2), *cannot tell*. Dropping the years was decided after seeing them.
- **[Likely]** Other experiments sit in later windows. A high-flow release (days with mean flow above 30,000 cfs) falls in the fortnight after the November change in 2013, 2014, 2016 and 2018, and in the fortnight before the March 2008 change. After the November 2024 change most of the daily rise and fall is gone: in the raw readings the typical day's rise and fall shrinks from 34% of its mean flow to 12%, and the median day's coherence is 0.66, the lowest of any November outside 2008–2012. Reclamation released colder "cool mix" water against smallmouth bass from 9 July 2024 until mid-November, announcing their end on 19 November 2024 (news releases), so those releases were running in both November 2024 fortnights. They are the likely cause, though I have not checked how they changed the hourly schedule. The fortnight after the March 2021 change holds steady releases of about 4,200 cfs and then about 20,000. The four high-flow Novembers' steps average 0.0 (95% interval −31.5 to 31.5); the eight Novembers with none of these average 16.8 (95% interval −7.2 to 40.7).
- **[Likely]** Every cut of the record I made afterwards puts the whole move at about 20 to 30 minutes, though none is registered and their labels mean little. Over the eight years whose Novembers hold no experiment, *K* is 19.0 (95% interval 4.9 to 33.1) and *K*<sub>c</sub> 27.1 (95% interval 8.5 to 45.8), *keeps standard time*. Dropping 2021 too, for its March, *K* is 22.5 (95% interval 8.8 to 36.2) and *K*<sub>c</sub> 30.5 (95% interval 10.5 to 50.6), which the rule would call *keeps clock time*. One year moves that label from one end of the rule to the other, on seven years chosen after seeing them, so I read only the centres.
- **[Speculative]** Why only part of the hour? In the average March day every feature moved earlier: the morning rise 22 minutes, the overnight low 22, the afternoon fall 23, the evening rise 24; only the broad, flat evening crest moved less (4). The morning features are well fixed and the evening's are not. Resampling the eighteen years (2,000 bootstrap resamples) puts the morning rise's move at 12 to 33 minutes earlier and the low's at 12 to 32, but the crest's anywhere from 39 minutes earlier to 7 later and the evening rise's from 61 earlier to 11 later, and in a third of the resamples the late-afternoon dip below the average, and with it the evening rise, vanishes altogether. So the data can't say whether the evening moved less than the morning. One reading is that the dam's hourly schedule is written in standard time and only partly re-planned. The 2023 flood release, for one, was scheduled in "Mountain Standard Time" (National Park Service, April 2023 High-Flow Experiment page). Another is that only part of the demand the dam follows moves with the clocks, since Arizona's doesn't. I have no data on how WAPA or Reclamation actually schedule.

How good the instrument is:

- **[Certain]** Two negative controls behave cleanly: Tucson's wastewater (*K*<sub>c</sub> −8.4, 95% interval −23.4 to 6.6) and the Delaware's sun-driven temperature (0.8, 95% interval −16.1 to 17.6) come out near zero with narrow intervals under both statistics. The Salt below an Arizona dam gets the same label, but its intervals reach 43.7 (*K*<sub>c</sub>) and 46.5 (*K*), so it shows only that the method doesn't invent a whole hour there. The UTC handling is checked against the legacy service at a DST site and an Arizona site.
- **[Certain]** The positive controls are weak. Under the registered *K*<sub>c</sub>, Buford (44.5) and the Trinity (42.3) are both *cannot tell*. Uncorrected, Buford is *keeps clock time* (63.1) and the Trinity *keeps standard time* (9.1), the wrong answer if, as I expected, Dallas's wastewater keeps the clock. So the test is better at ruling the full hour out than at ruling it in, and the point estimates for the two clock-keepers fall short of 60. If that shortfall is a bias of the method rather than of those rivers, Lees Ferry's numbers could be understated by a similar fraction. Its intervals would still stop short of the hour.
- **[Likely]** The law test can't reach 60 for a river that keeps the clock (How it works): a simulated clock-keeper scores 45.1 at the March and November dates and 40.7 at the April and October dates. At the March and November dates it still separates the dams: Buford 61.7 (29.4 to 93.9), above even the simulation, Lees Ferry −1.1 (−23.9 to 21.6). At the April and October dates they seem to swap (Lees Ferry 47.2, 23.0 to 71.4; Buford −4.3, −33.9 to 25.4), and part of that is the overlap: in 2008–2025 Buford's late-October fortnight, which holds the real change a week later, steps 82.9 (95% interval 56.0 to 109.8) though no clock changed that day, where the simulated clock-keeper steps about 38.8.
- **[Likely]** The pieces the overlap doesn't touch say Lees Ferry kept about half the hour before 2007. *K* at each era's own change dates is 29.1 (95% interval 11.2 to 47.0) for 1990–2006, which the rule would call *keeps part of the hour*, and 3.5 (95% interval −18.7 to 25.7) for 2008–2025. By the spring steps alone, it moved 50.3 minutes earlier (95% interval 16.6 to 84.1) at the April dates in the years they were change dates than in the years they weren't, and 18.1 earlier (95% interval 6.1 to 30.2) at the March dates. At the April dates in 2008–2025, when no clock changed, its day moved 16.1 minutes later (95% interval 4.0 to 28.1): the kind of calendar effect the comparison is there to cancel. These are uncorrected and read after the fact. The 1990s may also be a poor baseline: the interim flows of 1991–96, the 1996 flood (high flows on 9 of the 14 days before the April 1996 change) and high flows in early November 1997 and late November 2004 fall inside them (found in the raw downloads). Buford, for comparison: 35.7 (95% interval 16.7 to 54.7) and 63.1 (95% interval 41.0 to 85.2).
- **[Certain]** Flaming Gorge, the sister dam in Utah, is *cannot tell* under both statistics, and its law-test intervals span −27.8 to 55.1 and −82.4 to 37.1. The method can't see what it does.
- **[Likely]** Single transitions are rough. The cross-correlation can pick between two near-equal peaks, and a few steps hit the ±180 limit (2008 and 2024 November at Lees Ferry, both in disturbed windows). The intervals come from the spread between years, which includes that roughness.

What the page claims:

- **[Certain]** Every number on the page is computed on load by `report()` from the bundled data. The README's figures are pinned by `results.test.mjs` against the same function, and the bootstrap figures against `bootstrapMoves()` with a fixed seed. The clock-keeper simulation and the bootstrap use a seeded generator, so they give the same numbers every run.
- **[Certain]** The page passes `node tools/check.mjs ideas/75-spring-forward`: README format, size, tests, and the headless smoke test at 1280 px and 390 px. I also drove it at 390 px by touch, in light and dark, with reduced motion: the clock toggle, the raster's season, clock and year controls, the strip's dots, and the first-method button. There were no console errors and no sideways scroll.
- **[Speculative]** The felt part. The two clocks are meant to make the result physical: the Utah watch shows the river arriving 38 minutes later than a fortnight before, the Arizona watch 22 minutes earlier. Whether that lands for anyone, I can't know. The clock times are descriptive features of an average day, not estimates with intervals. Rebuilt from the raw 15-minute readings, which aren't bundled, the morning crossing falls at 09:38 and 09:16, a minute from the faces.
- Rough edges: the day picture averages days scaled to their own size, so it shows timing, not volume. The first method pauses the page briefly for each river it runs (0.1 to 0.2 s each on a desktop; longer on a phone). The "nine miles" to Utah is the distance from the gauge's latitude (36.864°) to the 37th parallel, not a road distance. The clock-keeper benchmark rests on a simple noise model (independent noise at each hour, one fixed day shape per season); real days vary in timing and by weekday, and the benchmark shifts by a few minutes with the noise.

## Tests

`node --test ideas/75-spring-forward/*.test.mjs` runs three files (43 tests), Node built-ins only.

- `method.test.mjs` (27 tests). On synthetic rivers whose answer is known: clock-change dates for 1987–2030; the moving average passes a daily cycle exactly; the daily component recovers amplitude and crest exactly; hourly means convert UTC to standard time and fill only short gaps. Method 1 is nearly unbiased on a two-peaked synthetic river (mean error under 6 minutes for clock- and sun-keepers in both seasons), exact without noise, and gives the right verdict in at least 28 of 30 synthetic eighteen-year runs. Its weekday terms, robust fit, change-over Sunday rule and midnight unwrapping each have a test. Method 2 recovers any whole-hour rotation exactly, and gives *K* within 8 minutes of 60 or 0 on a block-dispatched peaking river, with and without a drift in dispatch. A known weakness (dispatch drifting opposite ways in spring and fall) biases it, as expected. Method 1 fails on the same block river. Added on 24 October, plumbing only: the flat-day guard, the 12-bit records, the centre list, the crest and mean-crossing times of a cosine, and number formatting. ## Where it could go next

- **The dispatch schedule itself.** With the dam's own hourly release records, if they can be had, the question could be asked of the dam rather than of a gauge 15 miles downstream, and the morning-versus-evening reading tested directly.
- **Other rivers astride a time line.** The Columbia below Grand Coulee (Washington, which changes) against rivers in Arizona and Hawaii, or European rivers that cross the CET/WET line.
- **The 1990s.** Lees Ferry's half hour at the April and October changes of 1990–2006 is either a real change in how the dam followed the clock after 2007 or an artefact of that decade's experiments. Reclamation's operating records for 1990–2006 could tell which.
- **A fairer law test.** Fall windows shorter than a week, or a benchmark simulated from real days rather than one shape plus noise, would take the overlap out of the registered comparison.
- **A better positive control.** A small DST-state dam with a regular daily schedule would show whether the method's shortfall at Buford and the Trinity is the method's or theirs.
