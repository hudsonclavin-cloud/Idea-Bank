Does the River Spring Forward?

Arizona keeps its clocks still all year. About fifteen river miles above Lees Ferry, Glen Canyon Dam lets water through its turbines when the West wants electricity, so the Colorado here rises and falls daily like a tide. The power is sold to towns, co-ops and tribes in Arizona and in at least five other western states, all of which change their clocks, and Utah begins nine miles north of the gauge. When those clocks spring forward an hour, does the river?

The short answer

Not by an hour. The test I registered before running it on this river puts the move at … minutes (95% interval … to …), against 60 for a river that keeps the clock. The rule I wrote down in advance calls that keeps standard time, but the label means only that the interval comes within ten minutes of zero and stops more than ten short of sixty: it still allows over half an hour. Looked at more closely afterwards, the March days move about … minutes earlier, and cuts of the record that leave out the dam’s experiments put the whole move at about 20 to 30 minutes, between a third and a half of the hour.

The answer I committed to

For each year I measure how far the shape of the day moved at the March change and at the November change, in minutes of standard time. A river that keeps clock time moves 60 minutes earlier in March and 60 later in November, so K = (November − March) / 2 comes to 60. A river that keeps standard time comes to 0. To take out slow seasonal drift, the registered statistic, Kc, subtracts the same measurement at two fake changes five weeks before and five weeks after, when no clock moved. Its interval over the eighteen years is judged with ten minutes of slack either side of 0 and 60.

Same dates, other years

In 2007 Congress moved the clock changes from the first Sunday of April and the last Sunday of October to the second Sunday of March and the first Sunday of November. So the same calendar dates can be compared in years when the clocks changed on them and years when they did not, which cancels anything tied to the calendar, like a release volume that changes on the first of the month. I registered this as a supporting test.

It has a flaw I didn’t see when I registered it. The last Sunday of October and the first Sunday of November are always exactly a week apart, so the fortnight on one side of whichever fall date was not a change date holds the other rule’s real change. A river that kept clock time would not score 60 in both columns, as I first wrote. A simulated one, built from Lees Ferry’s average days with day-to-day noise set so its median days are as alike as Lees Ferry’s (coherence … against …), scores about … and …. A river that keeps standard time scores 0 in both, overlap or not.

November is a mess, and I found out why only afterwards. From 2008 to 2012 Reclamation ran steady flows through September and October, an experiment meant to help native fish, so in most of those years the two weeks before the November change have almost no daily rise and fall to line up, and their steps are noise. Later Novembers hold other experiments: a high-flow release in the two weeks after the change in 2013, 2014, 2016 and 2018, whose steps average …; and after the 2024 change most of the daily rise and fall is gone, most likely because of the colder “cool mix” releases Reclamation ran from July to mid-November that year. Two Marches hold some too: a high-flow release before the 2008 change, steady releases after the 2021 one.

I chose all of these cuts after seeing the data, and on so few years the rule’s labels for the three Kc intervals swing from … to … to …. Their centres are steadier: about 20 to 30 minutes, between a third and a half of the hour.

The method that failed first

My first registered method gave each day one number: the time of day its 24-hour wave crests. A robust regression then looked for a step in that crest time on the night of the change. It works on a river whose day has one smooth wave, and on synthetic rivers it was within a few minutes. It fails at a peaking dam, because a dam can run a morning block one day and an evening block the next, and the crest jumps twelve hours. Run as registered it said cannot tell for Lees Ferry and for both positive controls. I wrote the second method, chose it from six variants by how they did on the controls, registered it, and only then ran it on Lees Ferry. The first method’s numbers are below; the page recomputes them from the bundled data, a river at a time.

Standing at the ramp

A river below a dam is a record of other people’s days. Most mornings at Lees Ferry the water climbs, most nights it is high, and none of it is weather. When the clocks change a few miles away, I expected the river to follow the people. It mostly didn’t. In March its whole day came about twenty minutes sooner, a third of the way to the clock. Following only partly, it ended up nearer the Sun’s hours than the clock’s.

I can’t prove what the dispatchers do. The schedule published for the dam’s 2023 flood release gave its times in Mountain Standard Time, so the dam’s hours may be written in standard time and only partly re-planned around other people’s clocks. Or only part of the demand it follows moves with them, since Arizona’s doesn’t. Those are guesses about why. What the water did is measured, and it did not spring forward an hour.
