# Backtime
> Type the time you want to eat. Every dish gets its own start time, the oven and hob are checked for clashes, and a live "next up" card walks you to the table.

**Kind:** product
**Run:** Open `ideas/27-backtime/index.html` in a browser. Double-clicking the file works, and so does any static server. Tests, from the repo root: `node --test ideas/27-backtime/backtime.test.mjs` (pure Node, 41 tests) and `node ideas/27-backtime/ui-check.mjs` (104 checks in headless Chromium, using the globally installed Playwright that `tools/smoke.mjs` uses).
**Made by:** Pilot Light

## What it is

Radio presenters *back-time* a record: they know when the news starts, so they count backwards to find the second the song has to begin. Backtime does that for dinner.

You give it one thing, the time you want to serve, and a list of dishes. Each dish is a few steps in order: *in the oven 200° for 80 min, out to rest for 25, carve for 5*. The page counts back from the serve time and turns the lot into a single timeline of clock times, merged across every dish. These are the labels the page shows:

```
16:55  Oven on, 200°C                                    15 min to come up to heat
17:10  Roast chicken: In the oven                        200°C · 1 h 20 min
17:50  Roast potatoes: Parboil                           12 min · hob
18:02  Roast potatoes: Drain, shake, into hot fat        3 min
18:05  Roast potatoes: In the oven                       200°C · 55 min
18:18  Oven up to 220°C for Yorkshire puddings           5 min to come up
18:23  Yorkshire puddings: Tin with fat into the oven, until smoking
                                                         220°C · 10 min
18:30  Roast chicken: Out to rest, loosely covered       25 min
18:33  Yorkshire puddings: Batter into the hot tin       2 min
18:35  Yorkshire puddings: In the oven, no peeking       220°C · 22 min
18:45  Carrots: Simmer                                   15 min · hob
18:50  Gravy: In the roasting tin                        10 min · hob
18:55  Roast chicken: Carve                              5 min
       Greens: Boil                                      5 min · hob
18:57  Yorkshire puddings ready                          keep warm
19:00  Serve
```

That is the worked example the page opens with (set to serve two and a half hours from whenever you first open it, so the live view is live). In the US it opens in °F, with the cookbook equivalents: 400°F for the chicken and potatoes, 425°F for the Yorkshires. On top of the arithmetic it does the things a good cook does in their head:

- **It switches the oven on for you**, one preheat before the first thing goes in. When the dial has to go up mid-meal it gives that its own job ahead of time, the way a cold oven gets a preheat. In the example, "Oven up to 220°C" comes five minutes before the Yorkshire tin goes in to heat, so the batter goes into a tin that has had ten minutes in a hot oven. A drop in temperature is noted on the step itself. If the oven would be empty for half an hour or less it stays on; longer than that and you get an "oven is free" line.
- **It flags clashes.** Two dishes in the oven at the same time wanting temperatures more than 25°C (45°F) apart; the minutes the oven spends climbing to a hotter setting count as that hotter setting. More pans on the hob than you have rings. Two hands-on jobs that overlap (carving while mashing). Each flag says when, which dishes, and what to do about it.
- **It runs live.** A "Next up" card shows the next job and how long until it is due, a "now" line moves down the timeline, and you tick jobs off as you go. If you fall behind it says so plainly, and once you are more than four minutes behind it offers to push the serve time five minutes. Ticks belong to one cook: open the same plan next Sunday and last Sunday's ticks are cleared, with a button to keep them. "Start now" works the other way round: if you begin this minute, when is the earliest you could eat?
- **Dessert works**: a dish can finish *after* serving. Long leads work too: a brine that starts two days before is shown as "14:30, 2 days before", and midnight is just arithmetic.
- **Everything stays in your browser.** No account, no server. Save a dinner as a `.json` file and load it again, copy a link that carries the whole plan in its hash, or print a one-page sheet with tick boxes for the fridge door.

Temperatures in °C or °F, really converted (°F by default where ovens are set in °F, such as the US). Light and dark looks, phone and desktop layouts, keyboard and touch, reduced motion respected. No build, no dependencies, no network.

## Why I made it

I wanted to make something for the moment I think of as *the hour before people arrive*. There is a particular kind of stress in cooking several things that all need to be hot at once, and it has almost nothing to do with cooking skill. Every recipe tells you how long its dish takes. None of them tells you when to start, because that depends on everything else you are making, and on one oven that can only be one temperature.

What people actually do is get a pen and work backwards on the back of an envelope. Food magazines publish printable "Christmas dinner timelines" every December, which is an admission that the problem is real and that the tool is still paper. I found that charming and slightly absurd. The computation is trivial, and it was being done by hand, under stress, once a year, badly.

The thing I was curious about was whether the *trivial* computation was the whole product, or whether the feeling came from somewhere else. Having built it, I think the arithmetic is the part that can be proven, and the calm comes from three small things on top: the single merged list (so you never hold more than one next job in your head), the oven jobs (switching it on, turning it up: the thing you forget), and the "5 min behind, serve 5 min later?" button, which turns the one moment of panic into a decision that takes a second. A timeline you trust is a kitchen you can leave for a minute.

## The product

**Who it is for.** One person: the host cooking a bigger-than-usual meal a few times a year, with three or more dishes and one oven. That means Christmas, Thanksgiving, or dinner for six, with an unfamiliar menu and an oven that has to be in two places at once. The weekly Sunday roaster can use it too, but Roasto and the free multi-dish timers below already serve them, and after a few Sundays they know the timings by heart. Not a chef (they have a brigade and a tacit plan) and not someone making one pan of pasta (nothing to coordinate).

**The one job.** Tell me when to start each thing so that everything is ready together, and keep telling me as I cook.

**The moment they reach for it.** Two hours before guests arrive, with the shopping unpacked and the question "so when does the chicken go in?" Then again at the stove, with wet hands, wanting to know only *what is next and how long have I got*.

**Why what they use now fails them.**

- *Phone timers* count forwards from now. The problem counts backwards from a fixed end. You can set six timers only once you have already done the arithmetic.
- *Recipes* give durations, not clock times, and each one is written as if it were the only thing you are cooking.
- *Paper timelines* (the magazine printables) are for one fixed menu at one fixed time. Change the serve time by twenty minutes and you are back to the pen.
- *Planning apps.* These exist, and there are more than I first said. Some time a single joint: Tiny Roast Timer (Apple Watch, $0.99) and Big Roast Timer ($0.99) count back from a finish time for one roast. Some plan a whole roast dinner. Roasto (iOS, paid) works back from the time you want to eat across the whole roast, Yorkshire puddings included, with alarms that keep going when the phone is locked. Ian Renton's free page "The Great Roast Dinner Timing Chart" draws a chart from a meat, weight, accompaniments and serving time. And some are general multi-dish planners. Chef's Multi-Dish Timer (iPad, free) takes each dish's temperature and cook time, builds an oven timeline and sends notifications. SimpleTime (iPad, free with a premium tier) builds one schedule for all your dishes and adjusts cook times to the oven temperature you set. Mise (a free browser planner, launched in September 2026, which I know only from directory listings) schedules each dish backwards from sit-down time and flags hands-on steps that collide. So "nobody does multi-dish back-timing" is wrong. What I could not find in any of their listings is this combination: dishes with any number of steps of any kind; one oven treated as a resource that can only be one temperature, with clash flags and a lead time for turning it up; a hob-ring check; and running from a link in any browser with the data kept on the device. That gap is narrower, and the apps with lock-screen alerts already beat this build at the moment that matters most.

**What it would charge, and why that is fair.** Free covers planning and cooking any dinner with everything in this build, including the chime while the tab is open, save to file, links and print. That has to be good on its own, because free multi-dish planners exist. A one-off £2.99 / $3.99 "kitchen pass" would add the two things this build cannot do and the occasional host most wants. The first is notifications that still arrive with the phone locked. The second is a library of saved dinners, so your Christmas plan is there next December. No subscription: the host uses it a few times a year, and charging monthly for that would be hostile. For comparison, Paprika charges $4.99 one-off (US App Store) for a whole recipe manager. Roasto is a paid app built around the roast dinner (I could not confirm its price). Chef's Multi-Dish Timer and Mise are free. The price is a bet that lock-screen alerts plus next year's plan are worth a coffee to someone cooking for twelve. There is no evidence yet that it is right.

**What I would measure to know it is working.** This build measures nothing, because it talks to no server; a shipped version would ask for opt-in, anonymous counts of these five things:

1. *Ready-together rate.* Take cooking sessions with three or more dishes and at least one tick. Count the share where "Served" is ticked within five minutes of the serve time *as it stood when the first job was ticked*. That anchor matters: the page's own "Serve 5 min later" button moves the planned time, and measuring against the moved time would turn a late dinner into an on-time one. Two numbers go next to it: how many nudges each session used, and the share of sessions that tick "Served" at all. Ticking "Served" is the tap most likely to be skipped at the table, so the rate only covers the cooks who tick. This is the product's whole promise in one number, and it is easy to flatter.
2. *Activation.* Share of first visits that change the serve time or edit a dish. If people only look at the example, they did not see the point.
3. *Plan ahead, cook on the day.* Share of plans made on one day that are opened again, and ticked, on a later day. That is how the occasional host would use it, and it shows the plan was trusted enough to cook from. A second dinner within 90 days is the slower signal that the paid library would be wanted.
4. *Entry friction.* Median seconds from "Add a dish" to that dish's first timeline event, and the share of added dishes removed within a minute. Dish entry is the one place this could be too much work.
5. *Clash resolution.* Of plans that ever showed an oven or hob clash, the share that were cooked without one. If clashes are shown but never fixed, the flags are noise.

## How it works

**Two files do the work.** `backtime.js` is the core: pure functions, no DOM, loaded by the page with a plain `<script>` (so `file://` works) and by Node with `require()` for the tests. `app.js` is the page. Nothing in the core knows what time it is; "now" is passed in.

**Offsets, not clock times.** Every step is placed as minutes relative to the serve time, negative before and positive after. A dish's last step ends at `-readyBefore` and each earlier step is laid end to end before it. Clock times are only produced for display (`clockOf` splits an offset into a day shift and a minute of the day), so a step that starts before midnight, or two days earlier, needs no special case; the test file checks it anyway.

**The oven as a resource.** Oven steps sorted by start are merged into *stretches*: a step joins the current stretch if it starts within `max(30, preheat)` minutes of the stretch's end, otherwise the oven is switched off and preheated again. That rule guarantees an "off" never lands after the next "on". Each stretch gets one preheat event at `start − preheat`, showing the temperature of whatever goes in first. The dial is then taken to be whatever went in last. Turning it *up* takes time, so a hotter step gets an "Oven up to T°" job a lead time before it starts. The lead is the preheat scaled by the share of the climb from room temperature, `preheat × rise / (T − 20 °C)`, rounded up to 5 minutes and never more than a preheat. With a 15-minute preheat, 200 → 220 °C takes 5 minutes. If that lead would start before anything is in the oven, the stretch simply preheats to the hotter setting. Turning it *down* is noted on the step ("oven to T°"), because the oven is already hot enough to start. Steps with no temperature are allowed and never cause a clash.

**Clashes by sweep.** For each kind of resource, the breakpoints of all its intervals are sorted and each elementary segment between consecutive breakpoints is examined. Oven segments are flagged where `max(temp) − min(temp) > tolerance`; the lead time for turning the dial up counts as oven time at the hotter setting, because the oven is climbing while the cooler dish is still in. Hob segments are flagged where active pans exceed the ring count, and hands-on segments where two or more jobs overlap (end-to-start touching is not an overlap). Adjacent flagged segments merge into one flag. The largest allowed plan (40 dishes × 30 steps) schedules in under about 40 ms; I measured 2–8 ms median and 15 ms worst on my test plans.

**Temperatures are stored in °C, always.** °F is only a way of showing and typing them. A step's box shows the number in your unit, and what you type is converted to °C and stored to 0.01°, so every whole degree typed in either unit comes back exactly as typed. Switching units changes nothing stored. The clash tolerance is a temperature *difference*, so it converts by the scale alone: 25 °C is 45 °F. A clash needs the spread to beat the tolerance by more than 0.1°, so the 0.01° rounding cannot invent one at the edge. A preset added in °F gets the cookbook equivalent (180 °C becomes 350 °F, 200 becomes 400, 220 becomes 425). The default unit comes from the browser's locale: °F for the US and the few other places that set ovens in °F, °C everywhere else, until you pick one in Settings.

**Live time without a date.** A plan has a serve time but no date, on purpose: the same roast dinner is right every time you cook it. So "now" has to be placed relative to the serve time by a rule: a clock time up to 18 hours before serve counts as before it, up to 6 hours after as after it (`nowOffset`). If the serve time has passed and nothing was ticked, the card says the plan is for another day rather than "4 hours behind". Jobs more than 18 hours ahead of serve show a clock time, not a countdown, and the brine is simply the first unticked thing to do. In that case there is no "serve 5 min later" button, which would be meaningless two days out.

**Ticks live in the plan**, keyed by dish and step id (`chicken/s2`), so they survive a reload and re-timing. Since ticks are a session and a plan is not, the plan also records which dinner its ticks are for (`doneFor`). That is the serve time nearest to "when this job was due, if it is being done now". A brine ticked on Friday evening therefore belongs to Sunday's dinner, and a tick up to 12 hours late or early still lands on the right one. Moving the serve time moves it too. Once that dinner is more than 6 hours past (or an hour past its last job, if that is later), the ticks are cleared. This happens when the page opens, or on the fly if the tab is left open, and a toast offers "Keep them". "Next up" is the first unticked job in timeline order, which is deliberately not the same as the first job whose time has passed: if you skip one, it waits for you.

**Import is defensive.** `validatePlan` takes anything, whether a saved file, a link, or `localStorage` from an older version, and returns a plan every other function can trust. Numbers are coerced and clamped and unknown kinds mapped to hands-on. Names are cut at 80 characters, counted as graphemes with `Intl.Segmenter`, so a flag, a skin-toned thumb or a ZWJ family is kept or dropped whole. Duplicate ids are regenerated, control characters stripped, and 500-dish files cut to 40. It also returns a list of what it tidied, and the page shows that list when it tidies a saved plan on load. The page itself stops at the limits: "+ Add a dish" and the preset menu switch off at 40 dishes, and "+ Add step" at 30 steps, so nothing is trimmed behind your back. A file whose dishes are all junk, or that has none, is refused rather than replacing your dinner with an empty one. A good file or link that replaces a dinner offers Undo. Links carry the plan as base64url JSON in the hash. Ticks are left out, because a link is a recipe, not a session. After a link loads, the hash is removed so a reload cannot undo your edits.

**Older plans are migrated.** Before this revision a plan stored whatever number was typed, and the °F setting only changed the label. A version-1 plan saved in °C is unchanged, apart from now following the locale for display. A version-1 plan saved in °F is ambiguous: presets added in that mode kept their °C numbers, while a cook who believed the label typed real °F. Any number above 260 cannot be a home-oven °C setting, so it is read as °F and converted; anything at or below 260 is kept as °C. The page then says how many of each it found and asks the cook to check. Old links and files go through the same path.

**Storage is a convenience, not a dependency.** Every `localStorage` call is wrapped; with storage throwing, the page still works for the visit (the UI check does exactly that). Durations can be typed as `90`, `1h20`, `1:20` or `1.5h`. Deleting a step gives an Undo toast, and so does loading a file or link over a dinner; deleting a dish or replacing the plan with the example asks first.

**Small details I cared about.** The tab title is the countdown ("In 12 min · Roast potatoes: In the oven"), so the kitchen laptop shows it from across the room. The "now" line pulses unless you prefer reduced motion. Dish colours are consistent between the cards, the timeline and the flags. The printed sheet is black on white regardless of the dark theme and has a tick box per job. Times follow the browser's locale (12-hour in the US, 24-hour in Britain) and can be forced either way.

## Honest assessment

No real user has tried this. I cooked the example in my head, not in a kitchen, and the one person I am sure about is the one I imagined.

What works:

- [Certain] The 41 Node tests pass. They cover the walk-back arithmetic, `readyBefore` before and after serving, event ordering and grouping, empty plans and zero-minute steps, and oven stretches (merge, keep-on gap, slow-oven case, off-before-on ordering). They cover turning the dial up (lead time, folding into the preheat, no lead without a preheat) and down. They cover oven, hob and hands clashes and their merging, including the lead time and touching versus overlapping steps. They check the worked example line by line in °C and °F, midnight and multi-day leads, duration and clock formatting in both styles, the `nowOffset` rule, `earliestServe` rounding and wrap, `nextItem`, and duration and time parsing. On temperatures, they check °C ↔ °F for every whole degree from 100 to 550 °F and 0 to 300 °C, the tolerance in both units, presets in °F, a clash at the tolerance edge in both units, and the locale default. They also cover version-1 migration, ticks going stale and following the serve time, validation of junk, coercion and clamping, grapheme-safe names, the 500-dish cap, and link encode/decode.
- [Certain] The 104 UI checks pass in headless Chromium, mostly at phone size. They cover editing a dish and watching the timeline move, invalid durations rejected, remove and Undo, creating and clearing an oven clash (including the 25° edge), presets, a typed dish, and ticking from the card and the list. They reload with storage, in a clean browser, and with storage that throws. They save to a file, load a changed file, load junk, and copy a link and open it in a fresh browser. They check the empty state, the "for another day" state, "Start now", keyboard opening, dark mode, reduced motion, print, 12- and 24-hour clocks, the desktop layout and `file://`. Added in the revision: °F as the en-US default, round trips between °C and °F, a preset added in °F, the °F tolerance edge, and a version-1 °F plan migrated from storage. With a controlled clock, they check that ticks survive a reload mid-cook, are cleared a week later with "Keep them" working, and clear on their own in a tab left open. They also check the nudge button at 2 and 6 minutes behind and beside a two-day brine, the 30-step and 40-dish limits, and the tidy message on load. Finally they check that junk and empty files are refused and that Undo works after a real import. `tools/smoke.mjs` passes at both sizes with no console errors or overflow.
- [Certain] The folder is about 190 KB, has no dependencies, makes no network requests and needs no build.

What is rough:

- [Certain] Entering a dish is the most work in the product: name, then for each step a kind, a label, minutes and maybe a temperature. The 18 presets cover a roast dinner and a few weeknight things; anything else is typed by hand. The entry friction metric above exists because I expect this to be the weak point.
- [Certain] The preset timings are plausible starting points, not tested recipes, and the page says so. There is no weight-based meat calculator and no food-safety logic. Check meat with a thermometer.
- [Certain] The oven model is simple on purpose. "Whatever went in last sets the dial" is what most cooks do, but it ignores the fact that a dish wanting 180° will carry on at 220° if something hotter goes in later, as long as the difference is within the tolerance. The tolerance (25 °C, shown as 45 °F) is a guess at "close enough"; it is adjustable, and it is the kind of number only real users can calibrate. Two ovens are not modelled.
- [Likely] The lead time for turning the oven up is an estimate. It assumes the oven climbs at the same average rate it preheats at, and rounds up to 5 minutes. Real ovens slow down near the set point, and a door opened for the tin costs heat. It is far better than announcing the rise at the minute the hotter dish goes in, which is what this did before, but nobody has timed a real oven against it.
- [Certain] The hob check counts pans, not sizes, and hands-on overlap is flagged whenever two hands-on steps overlap by any amount, which can be pedantic for two 2-minute jobs.
- [Certain] Chimes only sound while the tab is open and the browser lets it; there are no push notifications, so a phone that locks will go quiet. This is the first thing a paid version would need.
- [Certain] Because a plan has no date, the live view assumes you are cooking today. A plan with a two-day brine shows the brine as the next job, with a clock time, and relies on you to tick it. I think that is the right behaviour, but it is a rule, not a certainty.
- [Likely] The rule that clears ticks (6 hours after the dinner they were for) is a guess at when a cook is over. A dinner that runs more than 6 hours late will lose its ticks. Ticks saved before this revision carry no date, so they are taken to be for the dinner nearest to now: someone upgrading a week after their last cook sees those old ticks once.
- [Likely] Migrating version-1 plans saved in °F is a heuristic. A number above 260 is read as °F; anything else is kept as °C. A real 250 °F typed under the old label would come out as 250 °C (482 °F). The page says how many of each it found and asks the cook to check, but it cannot know. Only plans saved in °F mode before this revision are affected.
- [Likely] The competitor facts are from search results in October 2026, mostly snippets of App Store listings, because I could not open apps.apple.com from here. Tiny Roast Timer is listed at $0.99 and "only for Watch". Big Roast Timer is $0.99 for one roast. Roasto is listed as a paid iOS app that works back from meal time across the roast, Yorkshires included, with alarms on a locked phone; I could not confirm its price. Ian Renton's chart is a free page with its source on GitHub. Chef's Multi-Dish Timer is a free iPad app that builds a multi-dish oven timeline with notifications. SimpleTime is a free iPad app with a premium tier and multi-oven support. Mise is known only from aggregator pages. Paprika is listed at $4.99 one-off in the US. Prices and scope may have moved, and I have not used any of them.
- [Likely] The pricing is reasoning, not evidence: nobody has paid for this, free multi-dish planners already exist, and the free/paid split is a hypothesis to test with the plan-ahead and 90-day return metrics.
- [Speculative] The felt claim, that a merged list plus oven jobs plus a "serve 5 min later" button turns panic into calm, is exactly the kind of thing that cannot be proven here. It is what I would test first with a real Sunday roast and a real kitchen.
- I have only seen the page in headless Chromium. The CSS uses `color-mix()` for the dish-name tint, which degrades to no tint in browsers without it.

## Where it could go next

- **Notifications.** A service worker and `Notification` permission, so the phone buzzes at each job even when locked. This is the single feature a paying cook would ask for.
- **Recipe import.** Paste a recipe and pull out the steps and durations with a small parser (the "1h20" parser is a start), so entering a dish is one paste instead of five fields.
- **Two ovens, and ovens with a grill**, as named resources; hob ring sizes; a "keep warm" drawer as a place dishes can wait.
- **A dinner library** with the plans people actually cook (the paid tier), and a "what did I run late on last time?" note per dish, so the next Sunday's timings learn from this one.
- **Shared cooking.** Two phones on the same plan, one link, ticks in sync, so the person on gravy and the person on carving see the same next job. That needs a server, which is why it is not here.
- **A kitchen display mode**: huge type, the next two jobs only, for a tablet propped against the splashback.

## Revision note

An independent reviewer rebuilt the worked example by hand and confirmed the arithmetic: every time, the keep-on rule, the clash threshold and the "now" window were right. It also found real problems, and I reproduced each one before fixing it.

- **°F was a label, not a conversion.** This was the serious one. Switching to °F showed the chicken as "200°F". A turkey preset added in °F mode was "180°F". The 25° clash tolerance silently became 25 °F, and en-US got °C by default. Now every temperature is stored in °C and converted on display and input. The tolerance converts too (25 °C = 45 °F), presets added in °F get the cookbook equivalents (350/375/400/425 °F), and the default comes from the locale. Saved plans, files and links from version 1 are migrated, with a check-your-temperatures message where the old data was ambiguous. One old Node test had pinned the bug (`'into the oven 200°F'`); it now expects 392 °F.
- **Last week's ticks made "Next up" skip jobs.** Ticks now record which dinner they were for, and they are cleared (with "Keep them") once that dinner is more than 6 hours past.
- **The oven went up too late, and the example never heated the Yorkshire tin.** "Oven to 220°C" used to fire as the Yorkshires went in. A rise now gets its own "Oven up to" job a lead time ahead, counted at the hotter setting in the clash check. The example and the preset gain a "Tin with fat into the oven" step. The worked example grows from 13 to 15 rows (16:55 to 19:00 is unchanged).
- **The competitor scan overstated the gap.** Free multi-dish planners exist (Chef's Multi-Dish Timer, SimpleTime, Mise), and Roasto plans the whole roast with lock-screen alarms. The section now names them and claims only the narrower combination.
- **Smaller fixes.** The page now enforces the 40-dish and 30-step limits instead of trimming silently on reload, and shows what it tidied. A file with no surviving dishes no longer wipes the dinner, and replacing a dinner offers Undo. Names are cut by grapheme, not code point. "Serve 5 min later" is no longer offered beside a two-day brine, and the README says "5 min behind" and "overlap", which is what the code does. The pricing is now aimed at one person (the occasional host), no longer sells the chime that is already free, and no longer calls $3.99 "about" Paprika's $4.99. The ready-together metric is anchored to the serve time before any nudges.
