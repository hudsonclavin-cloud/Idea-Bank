Same Time Tomorrow

Catch the Sun at the same clock time every day for a year and it draws a figure-8 on the sky. Bend the orbit, retilt the planet, and find out why the earliest sunset comes before the shortest day: two weeks before, at the latitude of New York or Madrid.

1 A year of the same minute

Stand in one spot. Look at the Sun at one clock time, today, and again tomorrow, and every day for a year. It is never in the same place twice. Each dot below is where the Sun stands at that minute on one day of this year, seen from the ground. 2 Two reasons, one figure

The figure is tall because of the tilt. Over a year the Sun's declination swings between +ε and −ε, so at a fixed clock time it climbs and sinks. The figure is wide because the Sun runs fast and slow against the clock. Clocks keep mean solar time, a fiction in which the Sun moves at a perfectly even rate. The real Sun does not, for two separate reasons:

  1. The orbit is an ellipse. Earth moves fastest near perihelion (early January) and slowest near aphelion (early July), so the Sun drifts along the ecliptic unevenly. On its own this would put a sundial up to 7.7 minutes ahead or behind the clock, once each per year.
  2. The axis is tilted. Even perfectly even motion along the ecliptic is not even when projected onto the celestial equator, which is what a clock measures. Near the equinoxes the ecliptic crosses the equator at a slant, so only part of a day's travel is eastward and it counts for less. Near the solstices it runs parallel to the equator but 23° north or south of it, where the hour circles (the sky's lines of longitude) crowd together, so a day's travel crosses more of them and counts for more. This part is worth up to 9.9 minutes, twice each per year.

Together they make the equation of time: how far a sundial reads ahead (+) or behind (−) a clock set to its time zone's meridian. It is very nearly the sum of the two; for Earth the dashed curves add up to the solid one to within 0.6 min. Plot it against declination and you get the figure's own shape, the same at every latitude and clock time. Then change the planet.

3 The lopsided solstice

At most latitudes the shortest day falls around 21 December, but the earliest sunset comes before it and the latest sunrise after it. The reason is in the chart above. In December the equation of time is falling steeply, by about half a minute a day, which drags both sunrise and sunset later by that much every day. Around the solstice the length of the day is hardly changing, so the drag wins: sunsets have already started getting later while sunrises are still getting later too. June is the mirror image: the earliest sunrise comes before the longest day and the latest sunset after it.

How far apart the dates sit depends on latitude. Near the poles the day length changes so fast around the solstice that the drag hardly matters, and the three dates nearly coincide. Near the equator the day length barely changes at all, so sunrise and sunset simply follow the equation of time, and the earliest sunset is in early November.

4 Your noon

Your clock says 12:00 when the mean Sun crosses your time zone's meridian, not when the real Sun crosses yours. Set your longitude and UTC offset in section 1, pick a date, and see when noon actually happens where you stand.

Notes on the model

A planet on a fixed Kepler ellipse, spin axis tilted by ε, Kepler's equation solved by Newton's method; mean longitude, mean anomaly, eccentricity and obliquity from the J2000 values with their linear drifts (Meeus, Astronomical Algorithms, chapters 22 and 25); aberration of −20.5″ applied; nutation, the planets' and the Moon's tugs on Earth's orbit, and ΔT not. Mean solar time is taken as UTC. Against a full ephemeris (PyEphem's VSOP87 Sun, 2000–2050) that leaves the equation of time within about 2 seconds and the declination within 0.005°, but the instants of the equinoxes and solstices off by up to about a quarter of an hour. Every number on this page comes from sun.js, which is checked by sun.test.mjs against an independent NOAA/Meeus implementation, that PyEphem reference, closed forms, Allison & McEwen's Mars series, and published USNO and almanac values.
