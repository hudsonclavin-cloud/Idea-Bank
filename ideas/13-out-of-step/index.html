Out of Step

Everyone tells the same story about the Millennium Bridge: it wobbled because the crowd fell into step with it. Twenty years of work since, including a 2021 paper written by both sides of the argument, says the crowd probably never had to.

The question
Was footstep synchronisation, pedestrians falling into step with the sway, necessary to start the lateral vibration of the London Millennium Bridge on 10 June 2000?
My answer
Probably not. Walkers feed energy into a swaying deck simply by keeping their balance, in step or not, and the best modern work finds that synchrony is what the wobble produces, not what produces it. Falling into step can lower the threshold, though, and no data from the London onset rule out that it did.
Confidence that the common story is right about the onset
25%up from 15% after an independent review; and about 60% that falling into step helped the sway grow once it had started

1The question

On 10 June 2000 a new footbridge across the Thames opened to the public. Somewhere between eighty and a hundred thousand people crossed it that day, with up to about two thousand on the deck at once456. The deck began to sway sideways. Two days later it was closed, and it stayed closed until February 2002, after Arup had fitted 37 fluid-viscous dampers and 52 tuned mass dampers5.

The explanation everybody gives is a single sentence: the crowd fell into step with the sway and amplified it. The question I set out to check is narrower than "why did it wobble?" It is: did the crowd have to fall into step for the sway to begin? Onset is the part a bridge designer must prevent, and it is the part where the story and the evidence come apart.

2The common belief, and where it comes from

The belief has three roots, and they are respectable ones.

The engineers' name for it. Arup, who designed the bridge and investigated the movement, called the phenomenon synchronous lateral excitation15. In 2001 the Royal Academy of Engineering's magazine described "a little-known phenomenon called synchronous lateral excitation, due to overcrowding by pedestrians", in which a chance correlation of steps starts a slight sideways motion and walkers then tend to fall into step with it6. The name carries the theory: whatever it is, it is synchronous.

A precedent in Japan. Seven years earlier, Fujino and colleagues had filmed a severely congested footbridge at Toda Park and found that the walking of 20 per cent or more of the pedestrians was synchronised to the girder's lateral vibration; they modelled the result as a resonant force2. This is the scientific origin of the idea that a crowd locks on to a bridge.

A beautiful physics paper. In 2005 Strogatz, Abrams, McRobie, Eckhardt and Ott published Crowd synchrony on the Millennium Bridge in Nature3. They imported the mathematics of fireflies and neurons: each walker is an oscillator nudged by the deck, and above a critical crowd size the walkers phase-lock and the deck sways. The paper says that "many pedestrians fell spontaneously into step with the bridge's vibrations, inadvertently amplifying them", and that wobbling and synchrony "emerge together, as dual aspects of a single instability mechanism". Its model gave a critical crowd size, and coverage of the paper reported that it matched Arup's tests. Cornell's press release painted the scene: people "walking like tentative ice skaters, planting their feet wide and pushing out to the side with each step, left, right, in near-perfect unison"4. That image went into the public lectures and into the Wikipedia article, which today attributes the movement to "a positive feedback phenomenon, known as synchronous lateral excitation", like soldiers marching in lockstep but sideways5.

There is also a reason the story is so easy to believe, and it is worth naming because it is the exact point where it fails. Two thousand people stepping at random should produce forces that mostly cancel. If the deck nonetheless moved, surely they must have stopped being random. Surely they synchronised. The next section is about why that inference is wrong.

3What the evidence actually shows

  1. CertainArup's measured force law does not need synchrony, but it does not rule synchrony out either. After the closure, Arup ran crowd tests on the bridge and put walkers on moving platforms in the laboratory5. Their published finding is that the lateral force a pedestrian exerts on the moving deck "is found to be related to the movement" of the deck, and that the same thing "could occur on any bridge with a lateral frequency below about 1.3 Hz loaded with a sufficient number of pedestrians"1. A force proportional to the deck's velocity is, in engineering language, a negative damper: each walker cancels a little of the bridge's own damping, and past a critical number the net damping goes negative and any tiny sway grows by itself. Nothing in that description requires the walkers to be in step with each other. Nothing in it excludes it either. The 2007 synchrony model reproduces the same force-velocity law14, and Arup itself read its findings as synchronisation, with walkers falling into step once the deck began to move6. On its own, this measurement does not decide the question.
  2. SpeculativeThe frequencies did not line up, but only for one mode. In a 2010 letter to Physics Today, Bernard Feldman pointed out that the bridge's first lateral mode was at about 0.5 Hz while people sway sideways at about 1 Hz as they walk, and argued that the synchronous-lateral-excitation explanation "is at best partial and at worst completely incorrect"7. He drew the parallel with Tacoma Narrows, whose collapse is still taught as forced resonance though it was not. But the mismatch holds only for the centre span's first mode, at 0.49 Hz, and other modes moved too. Feldman's own letter notes a second lateral mode of the centre span, at about 1.0 Hz, moving with roughly equal amplitude7. Arup's account says the movement occurred more often on the south span, at 0.77 Hz, and in the centre span's first mode than on the north span, at 1.05 Hz6; the 2021 paper lists the modes as 0.5, 0.8 and 1.0 Hz13. All except the 0.49 Hz mode sit within or near the walkers' own sideways rhythm, which slows when a dense crowd slows the pace11. Even at 0.49 Hz a 2:1 ratio does not rule synchrony out: walkers swaying together at twice the deck's frequency can pump it parametrically18. The first version of this memo tagged this point Likely and said I could not find the other frequencies; two of my own sources had them. What the point shows is that something other than direct resonance moved one mode. As evidence that onset needed no synchrony, it is weak, and I tag it Speculative.
  3. CertainOther bridges did the same thing with no sign of synchrony. Macdonald instrumented the Clifton Suspension Bridge during crowd loading and reported that the observations "suggest that significant synchronisation of pedestrians did not actually occur", which "challenge[s] the commonly held view that synchronisation drives the initial rapid onset of lateral vibration"; the behaviour matched Arup's negative-damping model8. At Singapore's Changi Airport, 150 volunteers told to walk casually produced "a steady increase in lateral vibrations" resembling the Millennium Bridge phenomenon (at about a tenth of the amplitude)9. The 2021 study below says the same pattern fits observations on almost thirty bridges13.
  4. CertainThere is a mechanism that gives the negative damping for free. Macdonald's 2009 paper models a walker as an inverted pendulum balanced on the stance foot10. The biomechanics say people keep their lateral balance mainly by where they put the next foot, not when. On a deck that is sliding sideways, the foot placement that keeps you upright changes the sideways push on the deck, and averaged over many steps that push has a component in phase with the deck's velocity. The walker becomes a negative (or, at some frequency ratios, a positive) damper "even at a different frequency from their pace", with no change of stepping rhythm and no synchronisation. The same group's 2012 extension found the model in "very good agreement with measurements on full-scale bridges", and states plainly that "synchronisation with the bridge motion is not involved"11. The velocity-proportional force from single walkers has been measured directly on a laterally driven treadmill across 0.33 to 1.07 Hz12.
  5. CertainWhen the synchrony camp and the balance camp wrote a paper together, synchrony lost the onset. In 2021 Belykh, Bocian, Champneys, Daley, Jeter, Macdonald and McRobie (McRobie was a co-author of the 2005 Nature paper) published Emergence of the London Millennium Bridge instability without synchronisation13. They derived a formula for the negative damping contributed by each pedestrian and tested it in three crowd models, one of them built with a strong propensity to synchronise. In every case the trigger was the average negative damping of uncorrelated walkers; gait strategy only moved the threshold. Their abstract reverses the arrow of the story: "increased coherence of pedestrians' foot placement is a consequence of, not a cause of the instability." The University of Bristol's release called the older account "the synchronisation myth"17. I looked for a published rebuttal in the four years since and found none.

4The cancellation that doesn't happen

This is the part you can feel rather than cite. The intuition behind the belief is that random steps cancel, so the crowd must have synchronised. Half of that is true. Random forces at each walker's own rhythm do mostly cancel: their sum grows like the square root of the crowd, so two hundred people push about fourteen times harder than one, not two hundred times. But a balance correction is not at the walker's own rhythm. It is tied to the deck's velocity, which every walker shares. Those add like the crowd itself. Toy units throughout. The deck is driven only by the balance row; the step-rhythm row is drawn for comparison and is not applied. That each walker's correction follows the deck's velocity whatever the walker's phase is assumed here, from Arup's measurement and Macdonald's model110, not derived. The bridge's own damping is set so that, with random phases, onset comes at 165 walkers. That number is borrowed. It is the critical crowd Arup found for the London bridge in a crowd test on the north span in December 20003, whose first lateral mode is about 1 Hz6. The 2017 crowd model cites it and was tuned to reproduce it, and in that model onset coincides with the walkers phase-locking16. The toy puts the number on a 0.5 Hz mode for illustration. A faint ambient drive keeps a sliver of sway alive with no crowd, as wind and traffic would; everything else is chosen for visibility, and the sway is capped where real walkers would stop. The balance row is drawn in units of one walker's correction, normalised to the deck's current velocity, so the two force rows share a scale.

The "in step" switch changes the first row from √N to N. It also moves onset from 165 to 127 walkers, and it does that through the second row. Each correction pulses with its walker's ~1 Hz sway. With everyone in step, the pulses arrive together at exactly twice the deck's frequency and pump it parametrically, the route Piccardo and Tubino proposed for 0.5 Hz footbridges18. How far onset moves depends on the depth of the pulse, which I chose, so 127 is not a prediction. What the toy shows is that synchrony can lower the threshold but is not needed to reach one.

5The strongest case for the common belief

I tried to argue the other side as well as I could. Five things hold up.

People really did fall into step. Nobody in this literature denies it. Fujino filmed it in Japan2; every account of the London opening day describes it4; the 2021 authors themselves say coherence rises once the deck moves13. The dispute is about the direction of the arrow, and arrows are harder to see on video than lockstep is. (The 2021 paper's own table of bridges records "no direct evidence" of synchronisation on the London bridge13, so this rests on what people saw.)

The synchrony model fits the data. The 2005 Nature model was reported to reproduce Arup's critical crowd size3, and the fuller 2007 version reproduced "the linear relation between pedestrian force and bridge velocity seen in experiments"14. A model that fits the measurements is not refuted by a second model that also fits them. What the balance model has over it is parsimony and independent biomechanical support, which is a reason to prefer it, not a proof. And the London measurement the synchrony model matches was taken where synchrony is most plausible. Arup's controlled onset test was on the north span3, whose first lateral mode, about 1 Hz, matches the walkers' own sideways rhythm6, and the 2017 model was tuned to its critical crowd16. That is the onset test the models are compared with, so on their own the London data cannot tell the two models apart.

Timing coupling exists, and may be needed for the full size of the effect. Bocian, Burn, Macdonald and Brownjohn put 15 people through 137 walking trials on a treadmill mounted on a shaking table and found a weak modulation of footstep timing by the platform motion, which they call phase pulling15. They suggest it could explain why foot-placement models and lab trials underestimate the negative damping measured on real bridges. So the pure no-synchrony model may be short by some amount that only timing coupling supplies. The same trials also produced a few outright synchronised cases15.

Synchrony can drive even a 0.5 Hz mode. The frequency mismatch in section 3 rules out direct resonance on the centre span's first mode, not synchrony. Piccardo and Tubino showed that walkers swaying together at about 1 Hz can pump a deck at half that frequency parametrically, a 2:1 resonance, and cited the Millennium Bridge as an example18. The toy in section 4 shows the effect: put its walkers in step and onset falls from 165 to 127.

Lock-in may be what makes the wobble big. Belykh, Jeter and Belykh's 2017 crowd model asked "to what degree pedestrian synchrony must be present for a bridge to wobble significantly"16, and concluded that pedestrian lock-in was necessary for the bridge's large swaying even as they called the popular view of synchrony as the initiating cause an oversimplification. If that holds, the belief is wrong about the start and partly right about the spectacle.

6Conclusion

The sway of the Millennium Bridge was a self-excited instability. The best-supported account is that a crowd of ordinary, uncoordinated walkers acts as a negative damper on a lateral mode, because each person keeps their balance by adjusting foot placement, and those adjustments add up even when the steps themselves do not. Past a critical crowd size the bridge's damping is used up and any sway grows. The engineers' empirical model (2001) is consistent with this, though Arup itself read its results as synchronisation; the biomechanics explained it in 2009; the 2021 paper by both camps found that it triggers the instability even in a crowd built to synchronise.

I put the probability that the common story is right about the onset, that pedestrians falling into step is what started the sway, at about 25 per cent. It was 15 per cent before an independent review of this memo. Two London-specific arguments I had leaned on turned out not to discriminate. Arup's force law is reproduced by the synchrony model too, and the frequency mismatch covers only the centre span's 0.49 Hz mode, while the south span (0.77 Hz) and the centre span's second mode (about 1 Hz) moved as well. The crowd test the models are compared with was on the north span's ~1 Hz mode, where synchrony is most plausible, and walkers in step can pump even a 0.5 Hz mode parametrically. What keeps the number well below even is evidence from elsewhere: Clifton's onset without significant synchronisation, the treadmill measurements of the balance force, and the 2021 finding that average negative damping triggers the instability in all three crowd models. No one has per-walker phase data from a real onset, so my answer now rests more on other bridges and on models than on London itself.

I put the probability that synchronisation contributed materially to the amplitude once the sway was under way at about 60 per cent, unchanged, on the strength of the phase-pulling measurements, the few synchronised cases in the same trials, and the 2017 crowd model. The review added ways synchrony could amplify the sway (the spans that match the walkers' rhythm, the parametric route), but none is a measurement of amplification on the London bridge, and the 2021 paper's table lists no direct evidence of synchronisation there.

The sentence to replace the old one is this: the crowd probably did not have to fall into step to make the bridge sway; keeping its balance was enough.

7What would change my mind

Toward the common belief

  • Per-walker step-timing data from a real onset (Arup's 2000 crowd tests, or a new instrumented footbridge) showing phase coherence rising before the amplitude does.
  • Treadmill trials in which walkers' step timing is pinned by a metronome and the velocity-proportional force disappears.
  • A published reanalysis showing the 2021 per-pedestrian negative-damping formula fails on the ~30-bridge data set.
  • Evidence that the opening-day sway began on a span that matches the walkers' sideways rhythm (the south span at 0.77 Hz, the centre span's ~1 Hz second mode, or the north span at 1.05 Hz), with step timing locking before the amplitude grew. The modes that moved are already known to span 0.49 to 1.05 Hz613; what is missing is which one started first, and how.

Toward my answer, more firmly

  • A full-scale onset in which coherence is measured and stays flat while the amplitude grows.
  • The pure foot-placement model, with measured parameters, matching the on-site negative damping without any phase-pulling term.
  • Evidence that the sway began in the centre span's 0.49 Hz mode with no 2:1 locking of steps to the deck, which would close the parametric route.
  • Design codes dropping the word "synchronous" from the phenomenon's name.

9How this was researched, and what I could not read

I worked from web search results and the snippets and abstracts they expose, running about forty queries across journal pages, university repositories, press releases and the open-access record of the 2021 paper. The sandbox I run in blocks direct downloads from publishers and repositories, so I have not read the full text of any paper cited here. Every claim I attribute to a source is a claim I saw in that source's abstract or in a search summary quoting it; sources.json next to this page records, for each citation, the query, the page and the snippet I relied on, and which bibliographic fields I saw with my own eyes rather than supplied from memory. After a second verification pass every bibliographic field in the list was confirmed at source except two: the byline of the 2001 Ingenia article (reference 6), which rests on two secondary citations, and the volume and pages of reference 18, added in revision, which I supplied from memory.

In weighing the sources I ranked full-scale measurements on real bridges above laboratory treadmill trials, both above models, and models above press releases. Where I quote a press release it is to show how the belief was framed or to carry the authors' own characterisation, never as evidence about mechanism. The thing I most wanted to read and could not is the body of Arup's 2001 paper, which holds the critical-number formula and the details of the crowd tests that everything else refers to. The first version of this memo also said I could not find the frequencies of the modes that moved; they were in two of my own sources613.

Revision. An independent reviewer checked this memo and its toy. I reproduced each finding before acting on it. The changes: the toy's "in step" switch had been quietly lowering the onset threshold to 127 walkers by parametric pumping while its label still said the sway decays, and the page said the switch changed nothing about onset; the label now reports the actual onset in both modes, and the caption explains why. The 165-walker figure is now attributed to the north-span test it came from. The frequency-mismatch point is limited to the one mode it covers and tagged Speculative. Arup's force law is no longer presented as evidence against synchrony. The strongest case for synchrony gains the north-span test, the synchronised lab cases and the parametric route. One misquotation is corrected, and a Wikipedia quotation is trimmed to the words my notes show. My onset probability moved from 15 to 25 per cent; the amplitude probability stays at 60. For the revision I ran about twenty more searches and queried the open-access copies of references 13 and 16 directly for specific passages.
