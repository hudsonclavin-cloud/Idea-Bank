A printable instrument · cut for one place on Earth

First Light, Last Light

Two slits face each other across a printed box, one to the east and one to the west. At sunrise a blade of sunlight comes in through the east slit and lands on the inside of the west wall; at sunset it crosses back. Where it lands is the date. The grooves it lands on are cut for your latitude and your horizon, and on a day you choose, the light leaves through a slot of its own.

The box through the year

Your place

Horizon: how high the skyline is, in degrees, where the Sun rises (east) and sets (west). Open sea or flat land is 0; a line of trees a few hundred metres off is about 1 to 3. Above 0, the Sun is already up when the box is read, so the top of the far wall is in direct sun and only the lower part shows the stripe. Below 0 is a sea horizon seen from a height: it dips about 0.03° × √(your height in metres), so 0.13° from a 20 m roof, and a full degree only from about 1.2 km up. There the standard refraction formula is least sure. Longitude only moves the dates and times, not the grooves. The slit width is the one your test coupon printed cleanest.

The object

Print the coupon first: five slits from 0.8 to 1.6 mm (the number in front of each is its width in tenths of a millimetre), a groove and a slot, cut exactly as the box cuts them. Hold it up to a window and pick the narrowest slit that shows an even line of light all the way down; set the slit width above to that, unless the note below says your steepest light gets through only a sliver of it, and then pick a wider one. The coupon is seen head-on, but the box’s light comes through at a slant.

Why this shape: each wall is an arc of a circle centred on the slit opposite, the figure Euclid draws to build an equilateral triangle on a line. So the light meets the far wall very nearly head-on, and every degree of the Sun’s swing along the horizon is very nearly the same length of wall.

The key

Each groove is two dates: the Sun comes back to the same point on the horizon on the other side of the solstice. The dates are the mornings and evenings in 2026 whose stripe falls nearest the groove at your place. In every year from 2027 to 2060 they stay within a day of these (checked at six latitudes from 58° S to 58° N). The grooves are spaced 15° of the Sun’s path apart, the same steps as the 24 solar terms of the traditional Chinese calendar; their English names are the Hong Kong Observatory’s, and their seasons are the northern ones.

How far to trust it

Proven in code (firstlight.test.mjs): the page’s sunrise and sunset directions agree with NASA JPL’s Horizons ephemeris (DE441) to within 0.011° at all 148 sunrises and sunsets tested in ten cities, and its unrefracted Sun to within 0.008°. Both use the same standard refraction at the horizon, so that agreement does not test the air on your morning; the table above does. Sunlight is traced ray by ray through the triangles of the printed solid, and lands within 0.02 mm of the middle of each groove it is cut for; the second stripe your slot lets in, the band of direct sun under a raised skyline and the narrower beam a slanting slit passes are traced the same way. Not proven: that it prints this way, or that the stripe is easy to read at a real dawn. Nobody has printed it yet.

Setting it up

  1. Print. Coupon first. Then the box: PLA, floor down, no supports, 0.2 mm layers, 3 perimeters, 20% infill (about 57 g and five hours at the default size). On a 180 mm bed, turn the box 45°: lying straight, a box over about 96 mm puts the slicer’s skirt off the edge of the bed. PLA softens in a hot sun; for a box that lives outdoors all summer, PETG or ASA is safer. Use an opaque, light-coloured filament: the stripe shows best on a pale wall, and thin white PLA glows if light gets through it.
  2. Place. Somewhere that sees the sunrise or the sunset direction, or both: a wall top, a post, a windowsill. Level it by eye; the box reads the Sun’s direction, and a small tilt moves the stripe much less than a small turn.
  3. Turn it. The slits must face due east and due west, with the arrow on the floor pointing north. Each degree of error moves every reading by about 2 days’ travel near the equinoxes at your place (from about 1.3 days at 58° to 2.6 at the equator). Three ways, best first: on a clear morning, turn it until the light lands where this page says it will that day, then fix it down. Or hang a weight on a thread at solar noon (12:00): its shadow points due north–south, and the two tips of the box go on that line. Or use a compass corrected for your magnetic declination, the roughest. Near an equinox, the light going straight through both slits is the check.
  4. Read it. Look into the box, not at the Sun. The stripe appears as the top edge of the Sun clears your horizon and brightens as the rest comes up. The grooves are cut for the moment half the Sun is up, which this page times above for any day; read where the stripe is then. A few minutes later it has slid well away. With a raised skyline, read the lower part of the far wall: the top is in direct sun. If you cut a slot for your day, keep a strip of opaque tape over the outside of both slots except around your day and its twin; an open slot lets in light from the other end of the day as a second stripe.
