The Sky-Cooled Bed

A mattress pad plumbed to the night sky: no compressor, no pump, no plug, and it shuts itself off by day.

by Nightjar · October 2026 · status: unbuilt and untested; every number below is computed, not measured

The idea. On a hot night, don't cool the room. Cool the 0.30 m² of you that touches the mattress. A thin water pad under the bottom sheet is connected by two pipes to a radiator panel on the roof. A clear sky over a 31 °C night radiates like a surface at about 16 °C, so the panel chills the water a few degrees below the air. The cold water sinks to the bed, picks up body heat and rises back to the roof: a thermosyphon, with no pump. In daylight the panel gets hot, its water floats on top of the loop, and the flow stops by itself.

Verdict. Physically plausible, and modest. On the base-case night it removes about half of a sleeper's heat and cuts the sweat they need to produce by about 57%. The biggest unknown isn't the sky; it's your back. How much heat a sleeping back will actually give up to a cool pad is the number I trust least, and it swings the result more than the weather does. I put 44% on a well-built prototype doing what this page says, and 21% on it becoming something people actually use. The cheapest decisive test costs about $120 and three nights (§7).

§1The problem, and who has it

Heat waves kill quietly: mostly indoors, mostly older people, often alone. A few anchors I'm confident of: more than 70,000 excess deaths in Europe in the summer of 2003 (Robine et al., 2008); roughly 61,000 heat-related deaths in Europe in the summer of 2022 (Ballester et al., Nature Medicine, 2023); 619 heat-related deaths in British Columbia during the 2021 heat dome, which the BC Coroners Service's review found were mostly people at home indoors.

Nights matter because sleep is when the body is supposed to unload the day's heat. A case-control study of the 2003 French heat wave (Vandentorren et al., 2006) found housing factors, including a bedroom directly under the roof, among the risk factors for death of elderly people living at home. I'm quoting the direction of that finding from memory, not its effect sizes. Top-floor rooms under uninsulated roofs stay hot long after sunset because the roof slab keeps radiating the day's heat downward.

Air conditioning solves this when people have it, can pay for it and the grid holds. Many don't: the IEA's 2018 Future of Cooling report estimated that only around 8% of the 2.8 billion people living in the hottest parts of the world owned an air conditioner. During heat waves, AC demand is also what pushes grids toward brownouts.

The person this is for

An older adult in a top-floor flat under an uninsulated roof, somewhere heat-wave nights stay around 30 °C outdoors with moderate humidity: northern India and Pakistan before the monsoon, the inland Middle East, the US desert Southwest, southern Europe during a heat dome. (Not the humid coasts, for reasons in §3.7 and §6.) They have no AC, can't afford to run it, or lose it when the power fails.

Why the usual answers fall short on those nights

  • Open the window. This works when the night air is several degrees cooler than the room. On the nights that matter, dense cities often don't cool down that far. In the base case the outside air is only 31 °C against a 33 °C room.
  • A fan. Moving air helps only while it is cooler than skin (about 35 °C). At 33 °C a fan adds roughly 17 W of dry heat loss. At 36 °C it pushes about 17 W of heat into the body. Fans still help by speeding up sweat evaporation, and they pair well with this idea.
  • Gel or PCM cooling mats. These saturate. 2 kg of water gel allowed to warm by 8 K absorbs 67 kJ, which lasts about 32 minutes at the pad's base-case rate.
  • Sleeping on the roof. This is the original sky-cooled bed and it works, but it brings mosquitoes, safety risks, rain and no privacy, and it needs stairs that frail people can't climb.

The insight. For a sleeping body, the mattress is normally the worst thermal boundary there is. About a sixth of the skin is pressed into foam that soon warms to skin temperature and then sheds almost nothing. This idea turns that worst boundary into the best one.

§2The concept

The loop is a one-way valve with no moving parts

Why I think the idea fits its problem unusually well

  1. Sky cooling works at night, and people sleep at night. There is nothing to store.
  2. Heat domes are high-pressure systems, which usually means clear skies, so the sky tends to be open when the heat is worst. Likely Humid monsoon heat is the exception: it can be cloudy.
  3. The people at highest risk are the ones it can serve without a pump. A pumpless loop needs the panel above the bed, and for someone sleeping directly under the roof, the roof is right there.
  4. It fails safe. If the sky clouds over, the loop simply slows down. It cannot run backward and heat the bed.

§3Feasibility, from first principles

Base case: a clear night, outdoor air 31 °C, dew point 16 °C, bedroom air 33 °C under a ceiling still at 35 °C, a 2.0 m² bare panel 3 m above the pad. All inputs are listed with their basis in §9. Every number on this page is computed by the same code that runs the calculator in §4.

3.1 How much heat does a sleeper need to lose?

M = 0.7 met × 58.2 W/m² × 1.8 m² = 73 W ASHRAE lists sleeping at 0.7 met; 1.8 m² is a typical adult body surface area. Tmr = Troom + Fceil(Tceil − Troom) = 33 + 0.4 × (35 − 33) = 33.8 °C Mean radiant temperature: walls at room-air temperature, and the ceiling, still warm from the roof, filling about 40% of a supine sleeper's view (my estimate). Qdry = (Abody − Acontact)[hc(Tskin − Troom) + hr(Tskin − Tmr)] = (1.8 − 0.30) × [3.0 × (34.5 − 33) + 4.5 × (34.5 − 33.8)] = 6.8 + 4.7 = 11 W Convection to still air plus radiation to the room's surfaces from exposed skin. An ordinary mattress under the contact area is treated as adiabatic: the foam warms to skin temperature and stops conducting. Eneeded = M − Qdry = 62 W → 62 W ÷ 2.42 MJ/kg = 92 g of water per hour About 0.7 L over an 8-hour night, all of which has to evaporate. Respiratory heat loss (a few watts) is ignored here and in every other case.

The pad doesn't need to perform miracles. It needs to take over part of that evaporative load. For scale, 35 W sustained for 8 hours is 1.0 MJ. If a 70 kg body had no other way to get rid of that heat, it would raise core temperature by 4.2 K.

3.2 How cold is the sky?

εsky = 0.711 + 0.56 (Tdp/100) + 0.73 (Tdp/100)² Berdahl & Martin (1984) clear-sky correlation, dew point in °C. I omit its small hour-of-day and pressure corrections. = 0.711 + 0.56 (0.16) + 0.73 (0.16)² = 0.819 σTa⁴ at 31 °C (31 + 273.15 K) = 485.2 W/m² sky downwelling = 0.819 × 485.2 = 398 W/m², the same as a blackbody at Tsky = εsky¼ Ta = 16 °C a panel at air temperature radiates net 0.95 × (485.2 − 398) = 83 W/m² Drier air opens the window wider. At dew point 8 °C, εsky = 0.760 and the same panel radiates 110 W/m²; at 24 °C it is 0.887 and 52 W/m². Urban haze and thin cloud lower all of these. Measurements on a roof in a target city (Experiment 2) should replace them.

3.3 What the panel actually rejects

A panel colder than the air also gains heat from it. With light wind I take the front-side coefficient as 9 W/m²K, and the foam underneath leaks 1.0 W/m²K.

qnet = ε σ (Ts⁴ − εsky Ta⁴) − (hc + Uback)(Ta − Ts) at the solved panel temperature Ts = 27.8 °C: = 64 − (9 + 1.0) × (31 − 27.8) = 64 − 32 = 33 W/m² Qpanel = 2.0 m² × 33 W/m² = 66 W water runs qnet / hplate = 33 / 100 ≈ 0.3 K warmer than the plate: Tw = 28.2 °C Half of what the panel radiates is handed straight back by the warm air. A thin polyethylene windscreen above the panel (classic night-radiator practice) would cut that, but it costs some infrared transmission. In this model it lifts body cooling only from 35 to 38 W, so version 1 does without one.

3.4 How much heat will a sleeping back give up? (the biggest unknown)

Heat has to get from the body core, through tissue, skin, a sheet and the pad wall, into the water. These resistances act in series:

U = 1 / (1/ktissue + 1/Ufabric) = 1 / (1/20 + 1/40) = 13.3 W/m²K Ufabric = 1 / (sheet 0.010 + pad film 0.0015 + water film 0.003 + contact gaps 0.0105) = 1 / 0.025 m²K/W. These are my estimates; the gap term is the softest. UAbody = 13.3 × 0.30 m² = 4.00 W/K Qbody = UAbody (Tcore − Tw) = 4.00 × (37.0 − 28.2) = 35 W skin under the pad: 37.0 − 35 / (20 × 0.30) = 31.1 °C, which feels cool but not cold

Why ktissue is the number to distrust. How easily heat leaves the core depends on how much blood flows through the skin. In Gagge's two-node thermoregulation model, the core-to-skin conductance with no skin blood flow is about 5.3 W/m²K, and it rises steeply as heat stress opens up skin vessels. That argues for a high value. But cooling a patch of skin causes local vasoconstriction, which argues for a lower value right where the pad is. I picked 20 and treat it as unknown somewhere between 8 and 40. At 8 the body gives up only 19 W; at 40, 50 W. Contact area is the other soft number. A back-sleeper presses roughly 0.30 m² onto the bed (my estimate). A side-sleeper might manage 0.15 m², which gives 18 W. Speculative

Bigger panels give diminishing returns. The sleeper gets 30 W from a 1 m² panel, 35 W from 2.0 m² and 39 W from 4 m². There are two reasons, and neither is the body:

  • The panel has a floor temperature. In a light breeze it can never get colder than about 25.8 °C, where sky radiation exactly balances the heat leaking in from the air. Even an infinite panel would leave the water there, capping the sleeper at 4.00 × (37.0 − 25.8) ≈ 45 W. A windscreen lowers that floor and lifts the cap to about 56 W.
  • The mattress and room claim most of each extra watt, because UApar (5.86 W/K, §3.5) is larger than UAbody (4.00 W/K). Going from 2 to 4 m² adds 10 W at the panel: 4 W of it reaches the sleeper and 6 W goes to parasitic gains.

3.5 Parasitic gains, and closing the balance

UApar = (hc + hr)(Apad − Acontact) + UunderApad + UApipe = (1.5 + 5.5) × 0.42 + 3.5 × 0.72 + 0.4 = 5.86 W/K Qpar = 5.86 × (33.3 − 28.2) = 30 W 33.3 °C is room air plus the radiant share of the warm ceiling, which the exposed part of the pad also sees. balance: Apanel qnet(Ts) = Qbody(Tw) + Qpar(Tw), with Tw = Ts + qnet/hplate solved by bisection on Ts: 66 W = 35 W + 30 W The displayed parts don't add exactly because of rounding; the solver's residual is below 0.001 W.

46% of the panel's cooling goes into the mattress, the room air and the pipes instead of the sleeper. Some of it isn't wasted, since the exposed pad cools the air under the sheet, but I count all of it as loss. A pad cut tightly to the torso (0.45 m²) on 20 mm of foam would cut the loss to 13 W and raise body cooling to 38 W. It isn't the base case because people move in their sleep.

3.6 Will it flow without a pump?

Buoyancy from the density difference between the two legs has to overcome friction around the whole loop. Every element is laminar, so each one's pressure drop is a resistance R times the volume flow, and the resistances add:

pipe and fittings: R = 128 μ (Lpipe + Lfit) / (π D⁴), with 20 m of 20 mm pipe plus 4 m for elbows and valves pad, a spacer-fabric gap: R = 12 μ L f / (W h³), flow across the pad (L = 0.6 m, W = 1.2 m), h = 3 mm, spacer penalty f = 3 panel: R = 128 μ l / (π d⁴ N), 250 channels of 5 mm, 1.0 m down the slope Rloop = 6.26 × 10⁶ Pa·s/m³ drive = friction: ρ β ΔT g H = Rloop ṁ/ρ, with ṁ = Q / (cp ΔT) ⇒ ΔT = √[ Rloop Q / (ρ² β g H cp) ] = √[ 6.26×10⁶ × 66 / (997² × 2.57×10⁻⁴ × 9.81 × 3 × 4180) ] = 3.6 K ṁ = 66 / (4180 × 3.6) = 4.3 g/s (16 L/h)  ·  Re = 310 in the pipe, laminar as assumed Δp = 27 Pa: pipe 23.7, pad 2.6, panel 1.0 Using one mean water temperature everywhere is an approximation: in fact the pad sees 26.4 °C in and 30.0 °C out. The spacer penalty f (how much the fabric's threads slow the flow compared with an open gap) and the panel's channel geometry are my assumptions; the bench check in §7 measures the pad's resistance directly.

The pad choice decides whether the loop needs a pump. I first recommended a capillary-tube mat, the kind used in chilled ceilings (2.7 mm tubes at 15 mm pitch). A reviewer pointed out that I'd left its friction out of the budget. It adds 17 Pa with the tubes running across the pad and 44 Pa with them running lengthwise. A thermosyphon still circulates, because it simply finds the temperature difference it needs. But that difference grows to 4.7 K and 7.4 K, and with 1 m of height to 8.2 K and 12.8 K. Both exceed the 8 K I'd accept, past which one end of the pad is uncomfortably colder than the other. With tubes lengthwise, the condensation margin on a humid night also falls to 1.4 K. The spacer-fabric pad costs only 2.6 Pa, so the pipe dominates.

How low can the panel go? The loop temperature difference scales as 1/√H. With the spacer pad it stays within 8 K down to 0.6 m of height; with the capillary mats it needs 1.1 m and 2.6 m. Below that (say, a panel on a balcony at bed level), add a 1–3 W pump. With 15 mm pipe the base case runs at 6.1 K, and at 1 m of height at 6.3 K. Both are fine.

Body weight can't pinch the pad shut. An earlier draft of this page got this backwards. What matters for collapse is the pressure inside the pad, not the loop's driving pressure. The pad sits under an open 3 m water column at about 29 kPa, roughly 13 times the 2.3 kPa mean contact pressure of a sleeping body (70 kg × g ÷ 0.30 m²). The real risk is the opposite. Two welded sheets at 29 kPa balloon into round tubes that are firm, lumpy and touch the skin only along lines. Drop-stitch spacer fabric (the construction inside inflatable paddleboards) holds the sheets flat. What stays delicate is the drive: 27 Pa is 2.8 mm of water head, so a trapped air bubble can still stall the loop. Every pipe must slope up to the header cup, and the pad must be filled bubble-free.

By day, take the panel stagnating at 70 °C in sun. Conduction down the two stratified water columns is 2 × kw × (pipe cross-section) × ΔT / H ≈ 5 mW, plus a similar trickle through the pipe walls. That is negligible. In winter, clear nights will freeze the panel even when the air is above 0 °C, so either drain the loop or fill it with propylene glycol, which is more viscous and needs a larger pipe.

Two results surprised me. First, the system degrades gently under cloud. Even fully overcast, 20 W still flows, because the 31 °C night air is cooler than the pad water and the panel works as a passive air cooler. The clear sky contributes the difference between that and 35 W. Second, humidity matters less than I expected. Warm air hands back half of what the panel radiates, so the panel runs only a few degrees below air whatever the sky does, and the body-side conductance decides how many watts each degree is worth. The physiology numbers swing the result more than the weather does.

That is about 32 times the heat the pad pulls from the sleeper. An air conditioner with COP 3.0 would draw about 375 W, or 3.0 kWh per night if that start-of-night load held for 8 hours (it falls as the ceiling cools). Doing the same job with sky panels would take roughly 34 m² of them at the base-case flux. That is why this design cools one person's contact surface instead of the room: it's the only target small enough for a panel one person can carry up a ladder.

    §5Prior art

    I've only named things I'm confident exist. Every individual part of this idea is old. What I couldn't find is the combination.

    Water-perfused mattress pads with active chillers Certain

    Sleepme (ChiliPad, OOLER, Dock Pro) and the Eight Sleep Pod circulate water through a mattress cover and chill it with a thermoelectric or compressor unit plugged into the wall. They prove that people will sleep on a water-cooled pad. Difference: the chiller is replaced by the roof, so it uses no electricity and the loop switches itself off by day.

    Roof ponds: Harold Hay's Skytherm (1970s) Certain

    Water bags on a flat roof radiate to the night sky and cool the ceiling below. Movable insulation panels cover them by day. This is the closest ancestor. Difference: Skytherm cools a whole building at the kilowatt scale and needs a structural roof and moving insulation. This design moves about 66 W, can be retrofitted, has no moving parts, and targets the body directly.

    Radiative-cooling materials Certain

    Daytime sub-ambient radiative cooling (Raman et al., Nature, 2014), commercial panels from SkyCool Systems, and radiative-cooling textiles (Hsu et al., Science, 2016). Difference: none of it is needed here. At night, ordinary high-emissivity paint is enough. A spectrally selective film would only add value for evening or dawn operation.

    Thermosyphon solar water heaters Certain

    These are the same pumpless loop running the other way: the tank sits above the collector and hot water rises into it. Tens of millions are installed worldwide. The plumbing, the venting and the "keep the loop above the load" rule all carry over.

    Sleeping on the roof Certain

    This is a traditional practice across South Asia and the Middle East and the folk version of this invention: let the sky see your body. This design moves the sky's view indoors, to a bed that frail people can reach.

    Gel and PCM cooling mats; ice-bucket water mats Likely

    Gel mats certainly exist, and they saturate within the hour (§1). I believe mats that pump water from a bucket of ice are sold in several markets, but I could not verify specific products. They need a freezer and a pump.

    A bed plumbed to a sky radiator? Could not verify

    I don't know of a product or paper that couples a night-sky radiator to a sleeper's contact surface with a thermosyphon, but I could not verify that none exists. Radiative cooling is a busy research field, and a hobbyist build or a paper may well be out there. If so, read this dossier as an independent feasibility check of it.

    §6Pre-mortem

    Suppose it's 2028 and this idea is dead. Here are the failure modes I think most likely. For each one, the number is my estimate of the chance that it alone would sink the idea. These are separate judgements, not shares of a single failure, so they don't add up to 100%.

    25%technical

    Your back won't give it up

    Local vasoconstriction under the cool pad throttles the heat flow. Side-sleeping roughly halves the contact area, and a mattress protector or a folded sheet adds fabric resistance. A real 8 W/m²K tissue conductance would leave about 19 W, which isn't worth a roof panel. Experiment 1 tests exactly this.

    15%technical

    The sky is shut where it matters

    Humid tropical heat, urban haze and cloudy monsoon nights reduce the clear-sky benefit. The model degrades gently because the night air also helps (20 W even when fully overcast), but nobody would build this for that. I rate this lower than the body risk because sky radiation is well measured and the target climates are moderately dry on their worst nights.

    10%technical

    Parasitic losses eat the cooling

    46% of the panel's output already goes into the mattress, the room and the pipes. A hotter room, thinner foam or a long uninsulated pipe run could leave little for the body.

    10%technical

    Condensation

    If the pad drops below the bedroom's dew point, the sheets get damp and mould follows. If the panel drops below the outdoor dew point, dew forms on it and its latent heat (not modelled) cuts the cooling. At dew point 24 °C the margin at the pad inlet is only 3.0 K, and a high-friction pad shrinks it further (§3.6). Fix: a throttle valve, or a smaller panel in humid climates.

    15%technical

    Plumbing at 27 Pa: pad friction, air locks, leaks

    Only 2.8 mm of water head drives the flow. A pad with more friction than the spacer-fabric design (whose penalty factor is my guess) pushes the loop past its temperature limit, and one trapped bubble can stop it silently. A spacer pad at 29 kPa must also stay flat and comfortable rather than firm. This puts water inside a frail person's bed, and one leak would get it thrown out. The water sits at 25–30 °C, so the loop needs a biocide, and in winter it has to be drained or filled with glycol.

    35%adoption

    It never reaches the people it's for

    The users are renters and older people. Every install needs roof access, a landlord's permission and a hole or a window gap for two pipes. In practice each one is a small plumbing job, and the people most at risk are the least able to arrange one. Some who do get it may simply dislike a cool back under a hot face. This is the most likely cause of death for the idea, and it has nothing to do with physics.

    25%adoption

    Something cheaper wins

    Small inverter ACs and rooftop PV keep getting cheaper. Cooling centres, fans and wet towels cost almost nothing, and on cloudy or milder nights a fan by an open window does nearly as well. Where the grid is reliable and an AC plus a plumber costs less than this, the AC wins.

    If I treat these as independent (they're not quite), the five technical risks leave a 44% chance that a well-built prototype works as §3 claims, which is the product of one minus each technical risk. Adding the two adoption risks leaves 21% that it becomes something people actually use.

    §7The first experiment: the bucket bed

    The sky side is textbook physics; the body side is not. So test the body first, with no roof involved. Replace the sky with a cooler box of water at the temperature the sky would deliver, and measure how much heat a sleeping person actually puts into it.

    Kit (about $120)

    A 50 L cooler box of water; a small aquarium pump; a drop-stitch spacer-fabric pad on 10 mm foam; two temperature sensors on the pad's inlet and outlet, cross-calibrated in the same bucket to about 0.05 K; a cheap logger; a measuring jug; a bag of ice; a 50 × 60 cm piece (0.30 m², a torso's footprint) of 50 mm foam board.

    Step 0: pad bench check (one afternoon)

    Put a water reservoir about 3 m above the pad (a stairwell works) and raise the pad's outlet tube to 1 cm below the reservoir's water level. The pad then sits at its real ~29 kPa while only 1 cm of head pushes water through it. Lie on it and time one litre. The model's spacer pad passes it in about 6 s. A capillary mat would take about 52 s with tubes across and 209 s with tubes lengthwise. Fail: slower than 58 s, which means the pad alone more than doubles the loop's friction. Then redesign the pad or plan on a pump. Also note whether the pad stays flat or balloons into ridges.

    Step 1: control night, with the body's footprint covered

    Leave the bed empty, but lay the foam board on the pad under the sheet where the torso would lie, so the same 0.42 m² of pad is exposed to the room as on an occupied night. Run the loop and log water, room and pad temperatures. Fitting the heat flow against (room − water) gives the parasitic gains and the pump's heat. Why the board matters: a plain empty bed also exposes the patch the body would cover, which gains about 12 W from the room at 28 °C water. Subtracting it would make a sleeper who performs exactly as modelled read about 24 W instead of 36, and fail the test. The board itself leaks about 1 W; add that back.

    Step 2: three occupied nights

    Remove the board and sleep on the pad in a bedroom at 31 °C or warmer, with the flow at about 5 g/s. Chill the bucket to about 24 °C with ice at bedtime, then leave it alone. It picks up about 76 W in total (body, parasitic gains, pump, box walls), so it warms by roughly 1.3 K per hour and sweeps through the whole 26–30 °C operating range in one night. Body heat = measured flow (ṁ cp ΔT) − the control fit at the same room-to-water difference + the board's leak. Plotted against water temperature, its slope is the body's conductance. A plain empty-bed subtraction would have shown 1.9 W/K instead of 4.00. Note how you slept.

    What the model predicts

    36 W from the sleeper at 28 °C water, which is a 1.7 K rise across the pad at 5 g/s, and a slope of 4.00 W/K. Sensors matched to 0.05 K resolve about ±1.0 W.

    Kill and pass lines

    Read the corrected body heat at 28 °C water. Under 20 W: stop. That means a tissue conductance of about 9 W/m²K or less, which leaves only 21 W in the base case. Even a 6 m² panel would reach just 23 W, so no panel rescues it. 30 W or more (tissue conductance ≥ 15): go on to Experiment 2. In between, repeat the test with a thinner sheet before deciding.

    Then: the sky bucket (about $150, ten clear nights)

    Put a 2 m² pool-heater mat on legs on a roof in a target city, with the same cooler as its thermal load, and log water, air and dew-point temperatures. The rate at which the water cools gives the panel's net flux. The model predicts 51 W/m² with the panel 2 K below air at dew point 16 °C. Kill: under 25 W/m² on clear heat-wave nights. Only after both pass is it worth building the full loop in a real top-floor bedroom.

    §8How I chose this

    I listed candidates and crossed out the clichés, the ones that already exist, and the ones the arithmetic kills.

    • Solar fans to stop hot-car deaths. Keeping a sunlit car cabin within 5 K of ambient means removing about 1.5 kW, which takes about 0.25 m³/s of air, roughly 18× what a small solar window fan moves. A device that half-works here gives false reassurance about a child's life.
    • Micro-turbines in rain downpipes. A 100 m² roof getting 0.8 m of rain a year, with a 6 m drop, yields 1.3 kWh per year before turbine losses.
    • Spring-based regenerative brakes for city bikes. One stop from 20 km/h is 1389 J. Spring steel in bending stores about 153 J/kg, so that's 9 kg of spring per stop, and e-bike regen already exists.
    • Fridge waste heat to dry laundry. The heat already ends up in the room, so the net gain is roughly zero.
    • Seasonal ice pits for summer cooling. Already exists in several forms, from historic ice houses to modern snow-storage cooling plants.
    • Evaporative insulin-cooling wallets. Already sold.
    • Heat pipes that grow lake ice from below for ice roads. Runner-up, with beautiful physics: heat pipes short-circuit the ice's own insulation, so growth stops following √(time). But at one pipe per 4 m², a 400 km road needs about 4 million of them. Pipes can't stick up through a driving surface, and flooding the ice with pumped water already works cheaply.
    • A tap-and-listen ice-thickness gauge. Runner-up: it would read thickness and stiffness from how flexural and plate waves disperse after a hammer tap. Plausible, but it's more a sensor than an invention, and ultrasound and ground-penetrating radar already compete.
    • The sky-cooled bed. It's surprising (the sky is a 16 °C heat sink on a 31 °C night), plausible (every part is off the shelf), useful (it targets the people most at risk in heat waves, in the hot top-floor rooms where they sleep), and cheap to test.

    Revision noteWhat an independent review changed

    After the first version, a reviewer rebuilt the model in separate code. It reproduced every headline number, confirmed the citations, and found real problems. All of them are fixed above:

    • The experiment's control night was biased. I had said that subtracting a plain empty-bed night "slightly over-corrects". In fact it subtracts the room's gain on the body's patch too, about 12 W. A sleeper behaving exactly as modelled would have read about 24 W and failed the 30 W pass line. The control night now covers that patch with a foam board (§7).
    • The pad's friction was missing from the loop budget, and the capillary-tube mat I recommended has a lot of it. The loop now counts pipe, fittings, pad and panel, and the recommended pad is drop-stitch spacer fabric. The base-case loop changed from 2.5 K, 6.0 g/s and 19 Pa to 3.6 K, 4.3 g/s and 27 Pa. The lowest pumpless height is now computed (0.6 m) rather than asserted (0.5 m).
    • I had the pad-collapse argument backwards. The pad sits at about 29 kPa, far above body pressure, so its channels can't be squeezed shut. The real issue is ballooning, which is what spacer fabric fixes.
    • The sleeper's dry heat loss ignored the hot ceiling that §1 makes so much of. With a mean radiant temperature of 33.8 °C, the evaporation needed went from 84 → 31 g/h to 92 → 39 g/h, a cut of 57% rather than 63%. Heat drawn from the sleeper moved from 36 to 35 W, because the pad also sees the warm ceiling.
    • I misexplained why bigger panels give diminishing returns. It is the panel's own floor temperature and the parasitic share, not the body (§3.4). "The sky is not the bottleneck" became "the biggest unknown isn't the sky".
    • Smaller fixes: the pre-mortem now says its percentages are separate judgements, not shares of one failure. Preference and the fan comparison moved under adoption, and the README and the page now agree on the pump threshold.

Nothing here has been measured. Citations are from memory and limited to sources I'm confident exist; where I couldn't verify something, the text says so.
