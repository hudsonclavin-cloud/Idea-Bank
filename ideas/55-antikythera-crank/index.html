What you are turning

In 1901 sponge divers brought up, from a wreck off the Greek island of Antikythera, a lump of corroded bronze that turned out to be gearing: at least thirty gears survive, the largest about 13 cm across, with hand-cut triangular teeth. Its maker built the sky as it was then understood, out of whole-number ratios. One crank turned a four-spoked wheel once a year, and every other pointer was that year multiplied by a fraction of tooth counts.

This page is the reconstruction published by Tony Freeth and colleagues (2006 and 2008 in Nature, 2021 in Scientific Reports), built on Derek de Solla Price's and Michael Wright's earlier work. Each gear below has the tooth count those papers give; where no gear survives and one has been proposed, it is drawn as a ghost. The page proves, with exact fractions, that each train gives the astronomical relation it was cut for; and then it lets you see, against modern theory, how far a bronze sky drifts.

The pin and the slot

The Moon goes fastest near perigee and slowest near apogee. The mechanism makes that happen with two 50-tooth gears, k1 and k2, riding on the slowly turning wheel e3. Their axes are about 1.1 mm apart. A pin on k1, about 9.6 mm from its axis, sits in a radial slot cut in k2. As the pin goes round, its distance from k2's axis changes, so k2 is dragged ahead for half a turn and held back for the other half. This is the eccentric model of the Moon (or, equivalently, the epicycle model) made out of bronze, and it is exact: the angle k2 makes is the angle the eccentric model predicts, not an approximation to it.

The proof: every train, as an exact fraction

A gear train multiplies speeds by driver teeth over driven teeth. Multiply the tooth counts along each train with whole-number arithmetic, reduce the fraction, and compare it with the period relation the ancient astronomers knew. Every line below is computed in your browser from the same table the gears are drawn from, and checked again by the tests.

Three of the hypothetical counts are not free choices. Given the surviving gears, m3 must have 27 teeth for the Saros pointer to make 4 turns in 223 months, n1 must have 53 for the Metonic pointer to make 5 turns in 19 years, and n3 must have 57 for the Games dial to turn once in 4 years. And the awkward prime 53 is there for a reason: the turntable must turn at 477/4237 of a year, and 477 = 9 × 53 (it is 254 × 223 − 239 × 235). So l2 has 53 teeth, and the calendar and eclipse trains, which share it, cancel it with the 53s of n1 and f1.

The drift: bronze against the sky

The ratios are exact; the sky is not. The mechanism's year is whatever one turn of b1 is, so set it by the Sun and ask how the other pointers wander.

The eclipse dial goes stale

Each glyph on the dial says "an eclipse in this month". A Saros brings eclipses back after 223 months, but not quite: the Moon's node is about half a degree further on each time, so a series slowly fades out at one end and a new one begins at the other. This is what happens to the eclipses engraved for 205–187 BC (my reconstruction, below) as the dial is used again and again.

When, and whose

The wreck is dated by its cargo: its newest silver coins were struck between about 76 and 67 BC, so it sank after 76 BC, probably within a decade or two. The mechanism is older than the wreck, but how much older is argued over. The letter shapes of its inscriptions have been placed anywhere from the late third century to the first century BC; the 2006 paper called it "constructed around the end of the second century BC". The dials point earlier: two independent studies in 2014 (Carman & Evans; Freeth) found that the eclipses on the Saros dial fit the cycle that began in April 205 BC, and Iversen (2017) put the start of the Metonic calendar and the Games cycle four months later, in August 205 BC. An epoch is not a date of manufacture: a maker could start a dial at any convenient past cycle. Voulgaris and colleagues have argued for a start in December 178 BC instead. This page uses the 205 BC epochs. The calendar ring on the front is usually read as the Egyptian 365-day year; two statistical studies of its surviving holes (Budiselic et al. 2020; Woan & Bayley 2024) favour 354, a lunar year. I draw the 365-day reading.

Hipparchus. The pin and slot copies the eccentric (or epicycle) lunar model. Freeth et al. called it a realisation of Hipparchus's lunar theory, and Hipparchus is the astronomer we know worked with that model and measured its size from eclipses. But the eccentric and epicycle models are older than him, and if the dial epochs really are 205 BC, the design may predate his work. "Hipparchus's model" is a fair name for the geometry, not a known fact about who designed the gears.
