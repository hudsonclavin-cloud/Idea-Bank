# Smaller Every Cut
> Cut a head and the hydra grows back more of itself. It dies anyway, whatever you do, and the proof counts past infinity.

**Kind:** game
**Run:** open `index.html` in a browser (plain classic scripts, works straight from disk; no build, no network). Tests, from the repo root: `node --test ideas/64-smaller-every-cut/hydra.test.mjs` (the mathematics, about 35 seconds) and `node --test ideas/64-smaller-every-cut/ui.test.mjs` (drives the page and measures flashing pixel by pixel in headless Chromium, about 4½ minutes; skips itself if Playwright is missing).
**Made by:** Iolaus

## What it is

A playable version of Kirby and Paris's hydra (1982), with its proof running beside it.

A hydra is a tree. The bottom is its body, the round ends are heads. You tap a head to cut it. If it grew straight from the body, it is gone. Otherwise the neck it hung from grows back: on your *n*th cut, that neck (without the head) sprouts *n* more copies of itself, with everything on it, from the joint below. The first cut regrows one copy, the tenth cut ten, the thousandth a thousand. The game ends when only the body is left.

It looks as if you should lose. You can't. Every hydra dies, whatever order you cut in. The page lets you try to prove that wrong:

- **Five hydras to fight.** *Sprout* (one neck), *Fork* (two heads on a neck), *Stick* (three necks in a row), *Two sticks*, *Tall stick* (four in a row). The Stick dies in 20 to 37 cuts, whatever you do, and you can finish it by hand.
- **The clock.** Next to the hydra is a number that goes down at every cut and never goes up during a fight: the hydra's reading as an ordinal, like <i>ω</i><sup><i>ω</i></sup>·2 + <i>ω</i> + 3. Under it, the page shows which term got smaller and why. Below the game, every reading so far is listed with “>” between them. A tick box writes each joint's own reading on the hydra.
- **Let Hercules play.** Two strategies: the *fast way* (always cut in the biggest part) and the *slow way* (always the smallest part), at 1 or 2 cuts a second with animation, or 30, 1,000, or as many cuts a second as the browser can manage. On the Tall stick the slow way runs to millions of cuts and millions of heads while the first terms of the clock stay frozen.
- **Grow your own.** Tap any joint to sprout a head (up to 24 joints and heads, 6 deep), watch the clock rise, then fight it. The page works out what it can about how long the fight takes.
- **Facts for each hydra.** The fewest and most cuts possible, when the page or the tests could search every order of cuts; otherwise the fast way's count and the slow way's count or a proved floor under it.

The part that can be proved is in the panels beside the hydra: the clock falls at every cut, the counts are exact, the floors are proved. The part that can only be felt is the hydra itself. You cut and it grows, you cut and it grows again, the heads pass a million and the clock calmly ticks down. Knowing the proof doesn't make the hydra look any less immortal. Then the page tells you that ordinary arithmetic, the Peano axioms, can't prove the general fact you have been watching at work: that every hydra dies, whatever computable strategy Hercules follows.

## Why I made it

I wanted a result that stays strange after you understand it. Most paradoxes come apart once you see the trick. This one doesn't. The proof is short, and I can follow every step. I still can't make the hydra *feel* mortal. The proof works by attaching an infinite ordinal to a finite tree, and Kirby and Paris showed that no proof can stay inside Peano arithmetic, the usual axioms for whole numbers. It is a fact about finite trees and whole numbers, and every proof of it has to reach past them.

What I didn't expect was the cliff between the Stick and the Two sticks. One stick dies in 20 to 37 cuts. Add a second identical stick and the fastest possible fight is 988 cuts, and the slow way is beyond any tower of exponents you could write. The reason is in the clock. A stick reading <i>ω</i><sup><i>ω</i></sup> that is first cut on cut *n* becomes <i>ω</i><sup><i>n</i>+1</sup>. The first stick is cut on cut 1 and becomes a mild <i>ω</i><sup>2</sup>; the second can't be cut before cut 2, so it becomes at least <i>ω</i><sup>3</sup>. I wanted people to hit that cliff with their own thumbs.

## How it works

### The game, exactly

A hydra is a finite rooted tree; the root is the body, leaves other than the root are heads. Cut number *n* (*n* = 1, 2, 3, …) removes one head *h*. If *h*'s parent is the root, nothing else happens. Otherwise let *P* be *h*'s parent and *G* be *P*'s parent: after *h* is removed, *n* copies of the subtree rooted at *P* are added as new children of *G*. Hercules wins when only the root is left. This is the Kirby–Paris rule (the step number is the number of copies). Trees are unordered; the page puts the copies next to the neck they copy so that the picture moves as little as possible.

### The clock

Every node gets an ordinal: a head reads 0, and a node with children reading *a*, *b*, … reads <i>ω</i><sup>*a*</sup> # <i>ω</i><sup>*b*</sup> # …, where # is the natural (Hessenberg) sum: write the terms biggest first and collect equal ones. The hydra's clock is the root's reading. This is Cantor normal form, and it runs both ways: every ordinal below ε<sub>0</sub> is the reading of exactly one hydra, up to reordering necks. So in `hydra.js` an ordinal *is* a hydra: an array of terms `[exponent, coefficient]`, with a hydra as each exponent and a BigInt coefficient counting identical necks.

**Every cut lowers the clock.** Cut a head from a joint *P* that is not the root. *P* read β + 1, now β. At *G* the term <i>ω</i><sup>β+1</sup> = <i>ω</i><sup>β</sup>·<i>ω</i> became <i>ω</i><sup>β</sup>·(*n*+1), which is smaller for every finite *n*. The natural sum is strictly increasing in each term, and so is *x* ↦ <i>ω</i><sup>*x*</sup>, so every joint below *G* reads less too, down to the root. A head on the root just takes 1 off the root's reading. Since the ordinals are well-ordered, the clock can't fall forever, and it reads 0 only when no heads are left. Nothing in this uses how many copies grow back, so the hydra dies under any growth rule at all.

**What Peano arithmetic can't do.** Kirby and Paris proved that every strategy wins, and that the statement “every recursive strategy is a winning strategy”, which can be written in first-order arithmetic, is not provable in Peano arithmetic (PA), assuming PA is consistent. The proof above uses well-ordering of the ordinals below ε<sub>0</sub>. Gentzen (1943) showed that PA proves transfinite induction up to any ordinal below ε<sub>0</sub> but not up to ε<sub>0</sub> itself.

### The fast way and the slow way

- **Slow way:** at every joint, go into the neck with the smallest reading, up to a head. Write α[*k*] for the standard fundamental sequence on Cantor normal form: (γ + 1)[*k*] = γ, (γ + <i>ω</i><sup>β+1</sup>)[*k*] = γ + <i>ω</i><sup>β</sup>·*k*, (γ + <i>ω</i><sup>λ</sup>)[*k*] = γ + <i>ω</i><sup>λ[*k*]</sup> for a limit λ. The slow way's cut number *n* takes the clock from α to exactly α[*n*+1]: the copies make <i>ω</i><sup>β</sup>·(*n*+1) = (<i>ω</i><sup>β+1</sup>)[*n*+1], and a cut deeper inside the smallest term changes only that term. So the slow way on a hydra reading α lasts exactly *H*<sub>α</sub>(2) − 2 cuts, where *H* is the Hardy hierarchy: *H*<sub>0</sub>(*m*) = *m* and *H*<sub>α</sub>(*m*) = *H*<sub>α[*m*]</sub>(*m*+1).
- **Fast way:** at every joint, go into the neck with the biggest reading. While the biggest term is <i>ω</i><sup>*m*</sup>·*c* with *m* finite, the next *c* cuts each turn one copy into *s*+1 copies of <i>ω</i><sup>*m*−1</sup> (at cuts *s* = *n*, …, *n*+*c*−1), which `fastWay` adds up in one step: *c*(*n*+1) + *c*(*c*−1)/2. A biggest term with an infinite exponent is cut one copy at a time. That is how the page counts the Tall stick's fast way exactly: five cuts take it to one neck carrying 15 heads, at cut 6, and the rest is level arithmetic. The answer has 13,597 digits; it starts 265,586,598.

### The numbers

| Hydra | Clock | Fastest possible | Slowest possible | How it was checked |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sprout | <i>ω</i> | 3 | 3 | every order of cuts |
| Fork | <i>ω</i><sup>2</sup> | 10 | 13 | every order of cuts |
| Stick | <i>ω</i><sup><i>ω</i></sup> | 20 | 37 | every order of cuts |
| Two sticks | <i>ω</i><sup><i>ω</i></sup>·2 | 988 | slow way: more than 2↑↑↑76 | branch and bound over every order (`hydra.js`); floor below |
| Tall stick | <i>ω</i><sup><i>ω</i><sup><i>ω</i></sup></sup> | fast way: a 13,597-digit number | slow way: more than 2↑↑↑36 | exact batched count; floor below |

For the first three, the fast way is the fastest and the slow way the slowest. For the Two sticks, the fast way's 988 is also the fastest possible.

**Searching every order.** The exhaustive search memoises on (shape, cut number). For the minimum it is a branch and bound with this lower bound: give each non-root node at depth *d* the weight *n*<sup>*d*−1</sup>, with *n* the next cut number. A cut at depth 1 lowers the total by exactly 1. A cut at depth *d* ≥ 2 removes *n*<sup>*d*−1</sup> but regrows *n* copies of a neck whose top node alone is worth *n*<sup>*d*−2</sup>, so the total doesn't fall, and it can only rise as *n* grows. The game ends at total 0, so at least that many cuts remain.

**Floors under the slow way.** When *H*<sub>α</sub>(2) is too big to compute, the page proves a floor using four facts, all checked numerically where they can be:

1. *H*<sub>α</sub>(*x*) > *x* for α > 0, and *H*<sub>γ+δ</sub> = *H*<sub>γ</sub> ∘ *H*<sub>δ</sub> when γ + δ is in normal form (induction on δ). So *H*<sub>γ+δ</sub>(*x*) ≥ *H*<sub>δ</sub>(*x*), and *k*-fold iterates are at least one application.
2. *H*<sub><i>ω</i><sup>β+1</sup></sub>(*x*) = *H*<sub><i>ω</i><sup>β</sup></sub><sup>*x*</sup>(*x*+1) ≥ *H*<sub><i>ω</i><sup>β</sup></sub>(*x*+1), for *x* ≥ 1.
3. *H*<sub><i>ω</i><sup>λ</sup></sub>(*x*) = *H*<sub><i>ω</i><sup>λ[*x*]</sup></sub>(*x*+1) for a limit λ.
4. *H*<sub><i>ω</i><sup>2</sup></sub>(*x*) = 2<sup>*x*</sup>(*x*+2) − 1 ≥ 2<sup>*x*</sup>; iterating with (2) gives, for *x* ≥ 1, *H*<sub><i>ω</i><sup>3</sup></sub>(*x*) ≥ 2↑↑(*x*+1) and *H*<sub><i>ω</i><sup>4</sup></sub>(*x*) ≥ 2↑↑↑(*x*+1) (Knuth's arrows: 2↑↑*k* is a tower of *k* twos, and 2↑↑↑4 is a tower of 65,536 twos).

(2) and (4) fail at *x* = 0, where the *x*-fold iterate is the identity: *H*<sub><i>ω</i><sup>3</sup></sub>(0) = 1 < 2↑↑1. Every argument the page uses is at least 2.

The page evaluates the smallest terms exactly while it can, then walks the exponent of the first one it can't down to 4. Two sticks: *H*<sub><i>ω</i><sup><i>ω</i></sup>·2</sub>(2) = *H*<sub><i>ω</i><sup><i>ω</i></sup></sub>(39) = *H*<sub><i>ω</i><sup>39</sup></sub>(40) ≥ *H*<sub><i>ω</i><sup>4</sup></sub>(75) ≥ 2↑↑↑76. Tall stick: the exponent goes <i>ω</i><sup><i>ω</i></sup> → <i>ω</i><sup>2</sup> → <i>ω</i>·3 → <i>ω</i>·2+4 → … → <i>ω</i> → 19 → … → 4, reaching <i>ω</i><sup>4</sup> at argument 35, so the slow way takes at least 2↑↑↑36 − 2 cuts, and in fact more than 2↑↑↑36: *H*<sub><i>ω</i><sup>4</sup></sub>(35) = *H*<sub><i>ω</i><sup>3</sup></sub><sup>35</sup>(36) ≥ (2↑↑)<sup>35</sup>(36), while 2↑↑↑36 = (2↑↑)<sup>35</sup>(2), so the 2 subtracted is lost in the slack. The same goes for every “more than” floor on the page. These floors throw away almost everything (every copy after the first, every bigger term). Iterating (2) the same way as in (4) gives *H*<sub><i>ω</i><sup>*k*</sup></sub>(*x*) ≥ 2↑<sup>*k*−1</sup>(*x*+1) for every *k* ≥ 2 and *x* ≥ 1 (with *k* − 1 arrows; the step uses *H*<sub><i>ω</i><sup>2</sup></sub>(*x*) ≥ 2<sup>*x*+1</sup>, which also needs *x* ≥ 1), so the true counts are far above the floors the page shows: at least 2↑<sup>38</sup>41 − 2 for the Two sticks and 2↑<sup>18</sup>21 − 2 for the Tall stick. The page shows the three-arrow floors because they are the ones a reader can picture. None of these numbers rank the hydras against each other. I expect the Tall stick's true count is far bigger than the Two sticks', but proving that needs the monotonicity of *H* in its argument, which I haven't used or checked here.

### The page

`hydra.js` holds all the mathematics as pure functions (ordinals, cuts, layout, counts, searches) and loads both as a classic script and under Node. `app.js` draws the hydra on a canvas: tapered Bézier necks, heads with two eyes, the body at the bottom. Each drawn joint has a key that survives a cut, so the page knows which necks are new. A cut is shown as a 420 ms cross-fade from the picture before to the picture after, with the regrown copies growing out of the joint below them, in amber, along their own curves. (Why not let the necks glide to their new places? See *Safety* below.) When there isn't room, a bundle of identical necks is drawn as a few copies (at most 12) with a “×*m*” badge. When Hercules plays fast, the ordinal engine runs on its own and the picture is rebuilt from the ordinal twice a second. Every change to the picture, whatever asks for it, goes through one gate that lets it change at most twice a second (see *Safety*). Nothing piles up there: at most one request of each kind waits, and a new hydra drops whatever was waiting to act on the old one. Undo goes through the same gate and every click counts: three quick clicks undo three steps (the first at once, the other two together half a second later), and an Undo clicked while a cut is still waiting cancels that cut instead. Keyboard: focus the hydra, arrows pick a head, Enter cuts it (or sprouts, in Grow your own); holding Enter down cuts once, and a held Enter on a button acts once (on Undo it goes on, a step each half second).

- [Certain] The floors 2↑↑↑76 and 2↑↑↑36 follow from facts (1)–(4). The chains are recomputed in the test on plain trees with a separately written fundamental sequence, and (1), (2) and (4) are checked on every case small enough to compute. They are floors only, and weak ones.
- [Likely] The fast way is the fastest possible and the slow way the slowest more generally, but that is only evidence. Over all 84 hydras with up to six necks, the exhaustive search (in a scratch run, with a budget of 3 million positions) finished the fastest-possible question for 38 and the slowest-possible question for 22, and the fast way and the slow way matched every one of them. The page claims it only for the presets and for hydras it searches itself. As Matoušek and Loebl (1991) recall, Hercules has a “short” strategy (a primitive recursive number of moves) and a “long” one; their own result is that against a hidden helper of the hydra making one move after each of his, Hercules can still keep the game short, while a helper making two or more can make it long. I haven't compared my fast and slow ways with their strategies.
- [Certain] For hydras you grow yourself the page gives up honestly: it searches every order only within about 80 ms, stops counting the fast way after 20,000 steps or 60 ms, and for big hydras says the slow way is finite but too long for it to bound, because walking the exponent down to 4 would take more steps than it allows.
- [Certain] The meters round numbers above 7 digits to 3 significant figures (truncated, so a “more than” never overstates). The clock collapses long sums to their first terms, an ellipsis and the last term.
- [Certain] **Safety (WCAG 2.3.1 and the brief).** There is no sound, nothing blinks by design, and the hydra is a cartoon with round heads and dot eyes; a cut head just fades. Nothing is meant to frighten, and nothing here claims any lasting effect on anyone. Whatever the input (taps, clicks, keys pressed fast or held down, the buttons, or Hercules at any speed), the hydra picture changes at most twice a second. Every change goes through one gate in `app.js` (only resizing the window, a switch between the light and dark schemes, which recolours the whole page anyway, and the selection ring below redraw outside it). A change asked for less than half a second after the last one waits its turn, and at most one request of each kind waits: a newer cut replaces one still waiting, a newer hydra button replaces a waiting one and drops the cuts, undos and starts of Hercules that were waiting for the old hydra, and quick Undo clicks add up into one waiting undo of as many steps. So no click is lost and nothing piles up behind the gate: when a burst of clicks stops, the page catches up within a few half-seconds at most, one for each kind of request still waiting (in the tests, a tap worked again within 1.6 seconds, usually within about 1.05). (An Undo clicked while a cut is still waiting cancels that cut instead.) A held Enter or Space cuts once, because key repeats are ignored, and a held Enter on a button acts once too, except on Undo, which keeps going while Enter is held, one step each half second, and stops when the key is let go. Pausing Hercules leaves the picture as it is. Under `prefers-reduced-motion` a cut swaps the picture at once, so no cut can make a pixel change more than three times in any second. Otherwise a cut is a 420 ms cross-fade with the regrown necks growing out. That is not a pure pixel-by-pixel fade: the growing necks are redrawn every frame and the new heads' eyes move outward, so a few edge pixels change direction several times within one cut, and the more frames a second the screen draws, the more of those changes it shows: at 2 cuts a second the worst pixel measured changed 20 times in one second at 60 frames a second, and 34 times at 120, a rate many phones and monitors draw at. Few pixels do it. WCAG counts the flashes that happen within the same second. Measured on a virtual clock that drops no frames, at 60 and at 120 frames a second, the pixels that changed more than 6 times in any one second covered about 500 px² at most on every preset, played either way, and on hydras of up to four sticks 6 necks tall; the most found was about 670 px² (about 3% of WCAG's area limit), on a broom an independent check grew within the page's limits (a stick 5 necks tall with 19 heads on its top joint). These are the largest found, not limits; every one is well inside WCAG's limit. The one thing on the canvas that changes outside the gate is the thin selection ring that follows the mouse or the arrow keys and hides while Hercules plays, and it has a limit of its own: it moves at most 4 times a second, and appearing or disappearing counts as a move. It is drawn at full size from the first frame, even round a head that is still growing. If the ring hasn't moved for a quarter of a second it follows the mouse, the keys or Hercules at once; otherwise it follows when the quarter second is up, to wherever it should be by then. Counting the picture's own changes, a pixel of a ring can change at most 6 times in a second, which is 3 flashes, with or without the cross-fade; the most I measured was 4 under reduced motion and 5 with the cross-fade, with Fast way clicked on and off 15 times a second. Off the canvas, a Hercules button changes its label as often as it is clicked (Fast way or Slow way to Pause and back), inside its own box of about 90 × 40 px, a sixth of WCAG's area limit. Both labels sit in the same box, so the button never changes size and nothing around it moves: a test switches each button at every width from 360 to 430 px and checks that nothing on the page moves, and taps one with real touch taps 15 times a second, by the clock, at 390, 393, 402 and 411 px and counts the browser's own layout shifts (at most 3 in any second, which a cut by Hercules can cause by changing the clock's text). The fast snapshots of auto-play stay under reduced motion, since they are what auto-play is. A note under the auto-play controls states the rule.

## Verification

## Where it could go next

- **Hidden Hydra Helper.** Matoušek and Loebl's variant gives the hydra's friend extra moves after each of Hercules's. A two-player mode, where the friend can make the game long only from two moves per turn up, would let people feel where that line falls.
- **Buchholz hydras**, whose joints carry labels and whose proof of death needs ordinals far beyond ε<sub>0</sub>.
- **Goodstein's clock.** The same ordinals drive Goodstein sequences; a side panel could show a Goodstein sequence and a hydra counting down the same ordinal side by side.
- **Better floors.** With the monotonicity of Hardy functions the floors could keep the inner iterates and would order the hydras correctly.
- **Is the fast way always fastest?** It was on every hydra I could search. A proof, or a counterexample from a wider search, would settle it.

## Revision note

An independent reviewer recomputed every number on the page with separate code, including its own proof that 988 is the Two sticks' minimum, and found one serious problem and four small ones. All are now fixed:

- **The safety rule covered only auto-play.** Taps, clicks and Enter (with key repeat) were not rate-limited. With reduced motion, a held Enter or fast tapping strobed the whole picture, about 143,000 px² flashing more than three times a second at desktop size, while the page said nothing flashed. Every change to the picture now goes through one gate: at most twice a second from any input, and a held key cuts once. The flash test now measures pixel by pixel under held keys, fast taps and mashed buttons as well as auto-play. I also slowed a held arrow key, which could blink the selection ring 15 times a second on the Fork (about 700 px², inside WCAG's limit but needless).
- **The cross-fade claim was wrong at pixel level.** It said each pixel moves once, in one direction, per cut. In fact a few edge pixels shimmer within a cut. The text now says so and gives the measured area.
- **Floor facts (2) and (4) and the *k*-arrow bound need *x* ≥ 1.** They fail at *x* = 0. Every argument the page uses is at least 2, so the floors 2↑↑↑76 and 2↑↑↑36 stand.
- **Matoušek and Loebl** recall the short and long strategies; their own result is about the hidden helper. The attribution now says so.
- **"Whatever Hercules does" overstated what Peano arithmetic can't prove.** The unprovable statement is about every computable strategy, and *What it is* now says that.

A second independent check, of the fixed version, confirmed those five fixes with its own meter and found two things still wrong. Both are now fixed:

- **Undo lost clicks.** The fix sent Undo through the cuts' slot at the gate, where a newer request replaces a waiting one. So three quick Undo clicks undid only two steps, and an Undo clicked while a cut was waiting replaced that cut and then undid the cut before it: tap, tap, Undo left no new cut instead of one. Now each Undo waits its turn, an Undo clicked while a cut or sprout is waiting cancels that instead, and a held Enter on Undo stops undoing when the key is let go. `ui.test.mjs` checks all three; the first two fail on the first fix.
- **The selection ring's bound was false.** The Safety note said the ring was under 400 px². One ring round a head is, but the ring round the body in Grow your own blinked over 536 px² when the mouse flicked on and off it, and when the ring flicks between targets every outline it leaves blinks: 731 px² from arrow presses on the Fork, and about 4,300 px² (a fifth of WCAG's area limit) from sweeping the mouse to and fro across the Tall stick's heads. The ring now moves at most 4 times a second, which takes every one of those cases to 0 px², and the Safety note gives the new rule. While measuring I also found that in Grow your own the arrow keys ringed a head as well as the body or joint they had picked; only the picked one is ringed now.

A third independent check, of that version, confirmed both fixes and the five before them, and found two things still wrong. Both are now fixed:

- **Clicks piled up behind the gate.** Only cuts replaced each other while waiting; every click of a hydra button, Start over or Clear joined the queue and took its own half second. A held Enter on a button repeats its click about 30 times a second, so 2 seconds of a held Enter on Start over, on a hydra button or on Clear left about 55 requests waiting. The page worked through them for 28 seconds, and every tap on the hydra in that time was dropped, because the hydra it was aimed at had been replaced by the time its turn came. Clicking the hydra buttons 10 times a second for 2 seconds did the same for 8 seconds, and so did clicking Undo. Now at most one request of each kind waits: a newer one takes its place, a new hydra drops the cuts, undos and starts of Hercules that were waiting for the old one, and quick Undo clicks add up into one undo of as many steps, so no click is lost. Clear on an empty hydra does nothing. No button but Undo acts on a held Enter's repeats any more (Undo goes on, one step each half second, as before). After any of those inputs, a tap now works within about a second (at most 1.6 seconds in the tests). `ui.test.mjs` checks each case and fails on the second fix (55 requests waiting after a held Enter on Start over).
- **The ring blinked as Hercules went on and off.** The ring hides while Hercules plays, and `draw()` read that straight from whether he was playing, outside the ring's limit. During a cut's cross-fade every frame is redrawn, so switching Hercules on and off blinked the ring at the switching rate: with a head picked, a held Enter on Fast way made a ring pixel change 21 times in a second (about 1,000 px² of ring changed more than 6 times), and clicking it 15 times a second, 9 to 13 times. The area stayed far inside WCAG's limit, but the stated bound of 6 was false. Whether the ring shows is now part of the ring as drawn, under the same limit of 4 moves a second, the ring is drawn at full size even round a growing head, and a held Enter on Fast way switches Hercules on once (its repeats used to flick Hercules, and the button, on and off 30 times a second). The ring's own pixels now change at most 5 times in a second in that test, which fails on the second fix.

A fourth independent check found one more way the page could flash, and the coordinator fixed it by hand:

- **Tapping Fast way or Slow way quickly could make the page jump.** The label change to Pause resized the button, and at phone widths of about 390 to 411 px (including iPhone widths) that re-wrapped the row, moving the speed menu to another line and shifting everything below it on every tap. Both labels now sit in one box that keeps the size of the wider, so nothing moves. Two new tests: switching each button at every width from 360 to 430 px moves nothing on the page, and tapping one 15 times a second at 390, 393, 402 and 411 px gives at most 3 layout shifts in any second. The old page fails both. The cross-fade figure is now "a few per cent" of WCAG's area limit, after an independent measurement of about 2%, not 1%. A keyboard test whose bound had no headroom now takes its bound from the time the presses took.

A fifth independent check, of that version, confirmed the fix with its own whole-viewport meter and found three things still wrong in what the README and a test said. All are now fixed:

- **The layout-shift test tapped about 8 times a second, not 15.** It used `page.tap`, which waits for the button each time (about 54 ms), and then waited 66 ms more. It now taps the button's centre with real touch taps by the clock, one every 1/15 s, and fails unless all 30 taps reach the button at 13 or more a second (they arrive at 14.9 to 15.0). At that rate the page shows at most 1 layout shift in any second at 390, 393, 402 and 411 px, and the page before the fix shows 16 in one second.
- **The cross-fade figure was too low.** The Safety note said the most measured was about 480 px², about 2% of WCAG's area limit, and that the worst pixel changed 13 times in a second. Counting every pixel flagged at any moment of a run, three separate meters measured 500 to 620 px² over 8 seconds on the Tall stick at 2 cuts a second, 670 to 760 px² over 16 seconds and at most about 800 px² (3.7%) over a minute, and a pixel changing up to 18 times in one second. The note now gives those figures. The drawing has not changed. (`ui.test.mjs` counts a pixel's changes only up to 9, which is enough for every limit it checks, so its own report never shows more than 9.)
- **The test list was out of date.** The Verification section still said 9 UI tests taking about 3½ minutes; there are 11, taking about 4½, and the list now names the two new ones. `hydra.test.mjs` now checks that the README's test counts match the test files. While rerunning I also found two measured figures that a run had exceeded, and corrected them: the cross-fade scenarios in `ui.test.mjs` flagged up to 215 px², not 198, and one burst of mixed clicks left the gate busy until a tap worked after 1.53 seconds, not within 1.1 (the test allows 1.6).

A sixth independent check, of that version, confirmed those three fixes and found that the new cross-fade bound was still too low. Measuring it again, I found it too low in two more ways. All three are now stated as measured:

- **The cross-fade bound was the Tall stick's.** The Safety note gave about 800 px² (under 4%) as the most measured over runs of up to a minute, but every run behind it was on the Tall stick. On the Two sticks played the slow way at 2 cuts a second, the check's meter found 1,094 to 1,208 px² over a minute (5.0 to 5.5% of WCAG's area limit), the previous round's meter 1,085, and mine 1,070 to 1,121, and runs of two minutes added almost nothing (at most 1 px²). The Tall stick played the fast way reached 882 to 1,061.
- **Every figure was for 60 frames a second.** Each frame is a fresh sample, so a screen that draws 120 frames a second, as many phones and monitors do, shows more of the shimmer. Headless Chromium draws about 60, so I measured 120 on a virtual clock: the page's own timers and animation frames step exactly 1/120 s at a time, and every frame is read. There a pixel changed up to 33 times in a second on the presets (the note said 18; at 60 frames a second I found 19), and a minute's flagged pixels add up to as much as 2,700 px² on the Tall stick and the Two sticks (12%).
- **Hydras you grow can shimmer more.** The note's figures came from the presets only, but the page lets you grow sticks up to 6 necks tall. One, two or four of them flagged 2,300 to 2,500 px² over a minute at 60 frames a second and 3,600 to 4,200 px² (up to 19%) at 120, with a pixel changing up to 34 times in a second. Measured at real speed, these hydras slowed the meter to about 24 frames a second (the page alone drew about 44), which hid some of it; the virtual clock drops no frames.

None of this comes near WCAG's limit. Those totals add up pixels that flashed at different moments, and WCAG counts the flashes within the same second. Counting only those, the most in any one second was 488 px² (2.2% of the limit), on the stick grown 6 necks tall at 120 frames a second; on the presets it was 287 px². The Safety note and the comments in `app.js` and `ui.test.mjs` now give these figures with the frame rate and the hydra, and `hydra.test.mjs` checks the wording. The drawing has not changed. The selection ring's bound still holds at 120 frames a second: with Fast way clicked on and off 15 times a second, a pixel of the ring changed at most 5 times in a second, as at 60.

A seventh independent check confirmed the button fix with its own whole-viewport meter (nothing on the page moves at any width from 360 to 430 px, and fast tapping stays well inside WCAG's limit) and found that the shimmer figures above, stated as maxima, were beaten by a hydra the page lets you grow: a stick 5 necks tall with 19 heads on its top joint reached about 670 px² in one second (about 3% of the limit) and about 4,700 px² over a minute at 120 frames a second. Each check had answered the last with a tighter maximum and each had an exception, so the Safety note now gives the largest figures found and says they are not limits. None of them comes near WCAG's limit.
