The Shadow of a Feeling

Every emotion word gets two numbers. Put yourself on the map, then tilt it, and watch panic and anger come apart.

An explorable about the valence–arousal circumplex: what it captures, what it flattens, and what the field still argues about. Nothing you tap is recorded anywhere.

1Two numbers

In 1980 James Russell argued that the feeling part of emotion could be drawn on a circle. Two axes: how pleasant, how activated. He placed eight concepts round the rim at 45° intervals — pleasure at 0°, excitement at 45°, arousal at 90°, distress at 135°, displeasure at 180°, depression at 225°, sleepiness at 270°, relaxation at 315° — and then showed that 28 ordinary emotion adjectives fell into that circular order under four different methods: a circular-ordering technique, multidimensional scaling of similarity judgements, unidimensional scaling on the two hypothesised axes, and a principal-components analysis of 343 people's reports of how they currently felt. Russell 1980

This page redraws that map with modern numbers. In 2013, Warriner, Kuperman and Brysbaert had crowd workers rate 13,915 English words on three nine-point scales: valence (unhappy → happy), arousal (calm → excited) and dominance (the power of the word: weak or submissive → strong or dominant). Each word got about 20 ratings per scale. Warriner et al. 2013 The study extended Bradley and Lang's ANEW norms, which had covered 1,034 words. Bradley & Lang 1999 The 254 emotion words on this page carry their exact 2013 means, unchanged; the only editing is the choice of which words to show.

One caution before the pictures. Russell measured how people place feelings; the 2013 norms measured how a word makes a reader feel. Those are cousins, not twins, and the page keeps saying "word" when it means word.

    3The circle is an idea; the cloud is a V

    Russell's circle says where emotion words sit: out near the rim. The whole vocabulary tells a different story. Most of the 13,915 words cluster near the middle of the valence scale, and the middle is calm. Mean arousal is 3.89 for words rated between 5.0 and 5.5 on valence, but 5.41 for words between 1.5 and 2.0, and 5.55 for words between 8.0 and 8.5. Plotted, the vocabulary is a V, and the unpleasant arm is the steeper one.

    Is that true of feelings, or only of words? Kuppens, Tuerlinckx, Russell and Barrett looked at eight datasets of moment-to-moment self-reports and found, on average, "a weak but consistent V-shaped relation of arousal as a function of valence" — with such large differences between individuals that they declined to call it a law. Kuppens et al. 2013 A later study of 8,590 people in 33 societies and 25 languages rejected both the symmetric V and the idea that the two axes are independent: pooled across samples, arousal rose with pleasant valence but not with unpleasant valence, and valence explained anywhere from 5% (Finland) to 43% (Beijing) of the variance in arousal. Yik et al. 2023 So the two numbers are not independent, and the shape of their dependence changes with who you ask, what you ask about, and where.

    4Where the lines go is a choice

    Five years after Russell, Watson and Tellegen looked at the same kind of circle and drew the axes 45° from his, through the densest clusters of words. One axis ran from high positive affect (activated and pleasant) to low; the other from high negative affect (activated and unpleasant) to low. Their case was that these two are the factors that keep turning up in self-rated mood, and that they track different things: negative affect a general factor of distress, positive affect pleasurable engagement with the world. Watson & Tellegen 1985 Drag the slider and watch the axes turn. Not one word moves.

    That sounds like a difference in bookkeeping, and the two camps have spent decades insisting it is not. Watson and Tellegen's axes, and the PANAS scales built on them, treat positive and negative affect as largely separate, each running from "high" to "low" rather than between opposites. Russell and Carroll replied that pleasant and unpleasant are opposite ends of one bipolar axis, and that the apparent independence comes from how the questions are worded and from measurement error. Russell & Carroll 1999 Yik, Russell and Steiger later fitted the major models into a single 12-point circumplex, so that both sets of axes are readings of the same circle. Yik et al. 2011 The words on this page cannot settle the argument; they only show that both sets of lines fit the same cloud.

    5Same spot, different word

    Here is what two numbers cannot do. Panic and anger sit almost on top of each other in the plane: valence 2.56 against 2.50, arousal 6.40 against 5.93. On the third scale they are 2.49 points apart: panic 2.65, anger 5.14. Nobody who has felt both would confuse them, and the map does. The third number is the one that says whether you are the thing being acted on or the one about to act.

The other half is where panic and anger live.

        7What one point cannot hold

        There is a second thing the plane flattens, and adding dimensions does not fix it. A point on a pleasant–unpleasant axis is one value. Larsen, McGraw and Cacioppo asked whether people can feel happy and sad at the same time, and found that in ordinary settings most people report one or the other — but that people leaving the cinema after Life Is Beautiful, moving out of their dormitories, or graduating from college reported both at once. They concluded that experience is usually bipolar but the underlying processes are better described as bivariate: two quantities, not one. Larsen et al. 2001

        8A shadow of what?

        Everyone agrees that the two-number plane leaves things out. The live disagreement is about what kind of thing is missing, and whether it belongs in a map of feeling at all.

        "The plane is the whole of affect"

        In Russell's later account, valence and arousal together are core affect: the simplest, raw, always-present feeling, which need not be about anything. Russell 2003 An emotion like fear or anger is core affect plus everything the mind does with it — attributing it to a cause, judging the situation, reaching for a concept. Barrett's theory of constructed emotion makes the same move with a neural story: interoceptive feeling plus concepts, with the categories learned rather than built in. Barrett 2017 On this reading the third number is not a missing dimension of feeling. "Dominance" is a judgement about your standing in the situation, which is exactly the sort of thing that turns core affect into panic rather than anger. The data on this page are consistent with that: dominance is the least agreed-on scale, half of it is valence in disguise, and Russell's own circumplex kept two axes after his earlier work with Mehrabian had used three.

        "The plane is a shadow of something bigger"

        Osgood, Suci and Tannenbaum found that judgements of almost anything fall on three factors — evaluation, potency, activity — long before anyone drew an emotion circle. Osgood et al. 1957 Mehrabian and Russell turned those into pleasure, arousal and dominance, Mehrabian & Russell 1974 and, as the paper is usually cited, argued that the third was needed to tell apart terms with the same pleasure and arousal. Russell & Mehrabian 1977 Fontaine, Scherer, Roesch and Ellsworth went further: rating emotion terms in three languages on 144 features drawn from six components of emotion (appraisals, bodily changes, expressions, action tendencies, feelings, regulation), they found four dimensions — evaluation, potency–control, activation, unpredictability — with potency second in importance, ahead of arousal. Fontaine et al. 2007 On this reading, the circumplex looks two-dimensional because it only ever asked about the feeling. Ask about the whole episode and the shadow stands up. The data on this page are consistent with this too: the half of dominance that valence does not predict is not noise. Twelve fear words in the lexicon (afraid, scared, terrified, frightened, fearful, fear, terror, panic, petrified, dread, fright, horror) and thirteen anger words (angry, furious, irate, enraged, hostile, outraged, indignant, livid, anger, rage, fury, wrath, hostility) have the same average valence (2.8 against 2.8) and the same average arousal (5.7 against 5.7), and sit 1.1 points apart on dominance (3.3 against 4.5). Whatever the third number is, it knows the difference.

        There is a third position, which is that points are the wrong idea. Cowen and Keltner collected self-reports to 2,185 short videos and argued that 27 distinct categories of experience — amusement, awe, nostalgia and the rest — explained the reports better than any small set of dimensions, while still being bridged by smooth gradients. Cowen & Keltner 2017 Barrett and colleagues answered with a critique of the design and asked for studies that look directly at variation within each category and at the similarities between categories; Barrett et al. 2018 Cowen and Keltner replied that this framed their work as one more round of the discrete-versus-dimensional debate when it was not meant as either. Cowen & Keltner 2018

        Note what the map does and does not decide. Basic-emotion theory holds that a handful of emotions each come with their own signal, physiology and triggers, built by evolution. Ekman 1992 Constructionism holds that the biological givens are the two axes and the categories are learned. Both camps can read the figures above without flinching. They disagree about what the words are the names of, and a scatter plot of words cannot adjudicate that.

        10What this page is not

        It is not a mood tracker: nothing you tap is stored, sent or kept after you close the tab. It is not advice, and it does not tell you what any feeling means or what to do about it. It is a map of how English words have been rated, with the arguments about the map attached. Where you put your dot is your business.
