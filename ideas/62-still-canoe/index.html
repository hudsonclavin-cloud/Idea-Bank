Borrowing a mind · Carolinian wayfinding

The Still Canoe

Sail from Puluwat to Chuuk the way Carolinian navigators reckon it: the canoe stays put, the stars stay put, and an island you never see slides backward under them until you arrive.

Introduction

Out of sight of land, the navigators of Puluwat, an atoll in the central Caroline Islands of Micronesia, reckon a voyage in a way that turns the usual picture inside out. The canoe stays where it is. The stars stay where they are, rising and setting at the same places on the horizon. What moves are the islands: the one ahead comes toward you, the one astern falls away, and a third island, off to one side and far below the horizon, slides backward from one star to the next. Each time it reaches the next star, one etak, one stage of the voyage, is done.

That is the system the anthropologist Thomas Gladwin learned on Puluwat in the 1960s from the navigator Hipour Gladwin 1970, and that the cognitive scientist Edwin Hutchins later worked through as a piece of computation Hutchins 1983 Hutchins 1995. Neither treats it as a mistake about geography. Gladwin presents etak as a way of organising what the navigator already knows about speed, time, stars and islands into a judgement of distance. Hutchins goes further: the Western navigator's picture, a ship crossing a fixed chart, is also a choice of frame, and from a canoe the frame that holds the canoe still is a sound one Hutchins & Hinton 1984. (The chart, I would add, is a view from above that nobody at sea ever has.)

This page lets you use that frame on one real, documented voyage: from Puluwat east to Chuuk Lagoon (Truk), with the island of Pisaras as the reference, as Ward Goodenough and Stephen Thomas recorded it Goodenough & Thomas 1987. Part of it can be proven, and the page proves it. The rest you have to feel, and the page can only ask you to try.

Whose knowledge this is

Carolinian navigation is living knowledge, held by navigators of Puluwat (Polowat), Satawal, Pulap, Lamotrek and neighbouring islands and passed on in navigation schools such as Weriyeng. In 1976 Mau Piailug of Satawal navigated the Hawaiian voyaging canoe Hōkūleʻa from Hawaiʻi to Tahiti without instruments Finney 1979. In March 2007 he presided on Satawal over a pwo ceremony that initiated sixteen navigators, eleven Micronesians and five Native Hawaiians Polynesian Voyaging Society 2007.

Everything here comes from published accounts, almost all by outsiders, plus one compass transcribed under the supervision of a Polowat man. It is an outsider's model of one technique, simplified, and it is not how the knowledge is taught. Where the sources disagree, or where I could not check something, the page says so.

1 The ring that does not move

Directions here are named by stars. A star always rises at the same point on the horizon, night after night and year after year, so that point can carry its name: rising Vega, setting Vega. Goodenough recorded 32 such points for the central Carolines Goodenough 1953. Altair is the base star: its rising is east and its setting is west. Polaris, just above the northern horizon at these latitudes, is north. The Southern Cross gives five points in the south, as it rises, leans, stands upright, leans and sets. Where the stars physically rise, the points are not evenly spaced, because the stars are not.

Below, each point is drawn where its star really rises or sets at Puluwat's latitude, about 7.4° north, computed from the star's catalogued position. That is my astronomical rendering of the compass, not the navigators' diagram. The Weriyeng compass this page draws on, as McDonald renders it, sets its 32 named points evenly round the ring, 11.25° apart, and marks separately where each star actually rises McDonald, n.d.; and a summary of Goodenough's account (I could not read it in full) has the stars naming the points rather than physically marking them Goodenough 1953. East is at the top, as on the Weriyeng compass, because the compass is built on Altair's rising Holton et al. 2015. Tap a point to see its partner on the other side.

What can be proven

  • A star of declination δ seen from latitude φ rises at azimuth A, measured from true north, where cos A = sin δ / cos φ. It sets at 360° − A. So the compass is its own mirror: every star sets as far west of north as it rose east of it. The two leaning-Cross points are the exception, because no star rises or sets there: the cross's long axis is not exactly north–south on the sky, so it reaches a 45° lean at different times on its two sides, and the pair misses being mirror images by 4.0°. The test file checks the formula against a separate calculation that turns the sky about the pole and finds where each star crosses the horizon.
  • Altair rises at 81.1° here, 8.9° north of true east. The compass's east is a star, not a number.
  • The gaps are uneven: rising Schedar and rising Dubhe are 5.4° apart, rising Vega and rising Schedar 18.1°. Near east, three stars of Aquila rise within 4.2° of each other.

Gladwin's book title, East Is a Big Bird, rests on reading Altair's name, Mailap, as "big bird". Holton and colleagues argue that this was a mistake: the Big Bird, Mannap, is a different figure centred on Sirius, and is not a compass star Holton et al. 2015.

2 One voyage, two pictures

Chuuk Lagoon lies about 151 nautical miles east of Puluwat. The navigator's reference for this crossing is Pisaras, a small island about 120 miles to the north-east, under rising Vega from Puluwat and under setting Vega from Chuuk Penn Museum, "Keeping Track". You never see it. As the voyage goes on it is "dragged", in Goodenough and Thomas's word, back through the risings of Vega, Cassiopeia, the main star of the Great Bear, Kochab, and Polaris, and on round to setting Vega at the end Goodenough & Thomas 1987.

Here is the voyage drawn twice. On the left, a chart: north up, islands fixed, the canoe crossing. On the right, the canoe: bow up, stars fixed, everything else moving. Drag sideways across either picture, or press Sail.

What can be proven

  • The two pictures are one set of numbers. To get the right-hand picture from the left, subtract the canoe's position and turn by its course. The test file takes every island at every moment of the voyage from the canoe's frame back to the chart and lands within a metre of where it started.
  • From Puluwat, Pisaras bears 44.8° at 103 nautical miles (118 statute miles); rising Vega is at 50.8°. From Chuuk Lagoon it bears 311.6°; setting Vega is at 309.2°. So Pisaras starts 6.0° from rising Vega and ends 2.5° from setting Vega, and at each end Vega's is the nearest point of the compass, as the record says. This is the real check, and it could have failed: at the start there are only 3.0° to spare before rising Schedar would be nearer. Nothing was tuned; it comes from the published positions of three islands and the catalogued positions of the stars.
  • Followed along the track, Pisaras's bearing sweeps 93°, always the same way, and passes, in order, rising Schedar, rising Dubhe, rising Kochab, Polaris, setting Kochab, setting Dubhe and setting Schedar: the sequence in the record, mirrored on the far side of north. That order is not a second check. Seen from a straight track, a point off to one side only ever turns one way, and the compass points are in order round the horizon, so any island that starts nearest rising Vega and ends nearest setting Vega, passing north, must go through these seven in this order. The test file confirms it for islands scattered at random. Likewise the count of 8 etak is the number of gaps between star points from rising Vega round to setting Vega. It comes out at 8 because Pisaras starts and ends inside the first and last gaps, which are counted as partial etak; an island starting just beyond rising Vega would make 9.
  • Pisaras never comes nearer than 71 nautical miles. Even palms 30 metres tall, seen from 3 metres up, clear the horizon only within about 15 nautical miles Bowditch. The island you reckon by is never seen.

Why the stages are uneven

The etak are not equal stretches of sea. The shortest full one here is 8.7 nautical miles and the longest 19.4. Two things make them uneven, and the curve below shows both: the star points are unevenly spaced, and the island swings fastest when it is abeam, nearest the canoe. At departure Pisaras moves 0.38° for each mile sailed; abeam, 0.80°.

Riesenberg, describing etak after Gladwin, puts the reference island "about fifty or so miles to one side" of the course and says an etak averages about ten miles Riesenberg 1975. Pisaras is farther off than that, so its etak are longer. How navigators weigh a short etak against a long one I could not find in anything I could read; the sources I reached say only that segments differ.

3 Sail it in the dark

Now the islands are gone, as they really are once you are out of sight of land. You get a little of what a navigator gets: the stars, and a sense of how long you have been sailing and how fast. Your job is to keep Pisaras moving in your head. After each stretch, tap the ring where you think it now lies. The page then shows where it is.

Speeds are invented for the exercise, roughly 3 to 7 knots. The voyage is 151 nautical miles. Pisaras starts under rising Vega.

4 The island does not have to be there

Nothing in the reckoning uses Pisaras's sand or trees. It uses two facts: the star it lies under from Puluwat, and the star it lies under from Chuuk. Two bearings from two known places fix a point, whether or not there is land at it. Drag the reference island anywhere and the voyage is re-cut. The two gold lines are the star it lies under from each end.

Puluwat navigators learn, island by island, which other islands lie under each star Riesenberg 1972, and that lore includes islands that are on no modern map. Riesenberg followed one of them, Kafeŕoor (Kaafiror on Puluwat), said to lie north-west of Gaferut, through a chain of errors on European maps that moved island names about Riesenberg 1975. Gunn argued that these "ghost islands" may answer a navigational need, bridging gaps in the islands navigators reckon by Gunn 1980. That is an interpretation, not something navigators are recorded as saying. Real islands that are never seen certainly serve: Gunn notes that on the leg from Saipan to Pikelot of David Lewis's voyage with Hipour, Gaferut was the etak island Lewis 1971.

5 What does not slide

In the canoe's frame one island does not slide against the stars: the one you are sailing to. Its bearing against the star ring stays put, to within 0.3° over the whole voyage, while it grows nearer. That bearing need not fall on a star point; on this ring Chuuk's does not (section 6). The same holds for anything else that is going to meet you. Western seamanship states it as a rule: risk of collision "shall be deemed to exist if the compass bearing of an approaching vessel does not appreciably change" COLREGs, Rule 7. Hutchins notes that Western navigators prefer to think in absolute motion, but that there are tasks for which a relative-motion frame is the better one Hutchins, n.d.. Watching for collisions, I would say, is one.

Watch the other canoe for a few seconds against the stars, then say what happens.

What can be proven

  • For an approaching vessel on a steady course, the bearing stays fixed exactly when its motion relative to you points straight at you; then the gap closes to zero. The bearing changes at a rate (r × v)/r², where r is the other vessel's position relative to you and v its relative velocity. That rate is zero whenever the relative motion lies along the line of sight: straight toward you, but also straight away, or no relative motion at all (same course and speed: a fixed bearing at a fixed distance, and no meeting). That is why the rule says "approaching". The test file checks the closest-approach formula against a step-by-step simulation for random encounters, and checks the two constant-bearing cases that never meet.

6 What this page simplifies, and what is argued

  • The sea is still. No current, no leeway, no tacking. Real navigators have to allow for all three; the model sails a straight line at the speed you are told.
  • Chuuk is "under rising Altair". The record says the course from Puluwat to Chuuk is almost due east, on Altair's rising Goodenough & Thomas 1987. By geodesy the lagoon's centre bears 88.5°, and Altair rises at 81.1°. On this ring that is not a near miss. The lagoon's centre is nearer rising Orion's Belt (2.7° away) than rising Altair (7.5°), with rising Alshain between them: a two-point disagreement, larger than at either end of the Pisaras sequence. A course held on Altair's rising all the way would pass about 20 nautical miles north of the lagoon's centre. The lagoon is about 79 by 50 km (43 by 27 nautical miles) inside its reef Wikipedia, so that course passes near its northern edge, not through its middle. On an evenly spaced 32-point ring set by Polaris, where rising Altair's point is due east, the lagoon's centre is 1.5° from it. I do not know how navigators reconcile the two. Current, the size of the target and the compass's being a set of named directions rather than a protractor are all candidates; I could not find out which.
  • The ends are a few degrees off. In this geometry Pisaras starts 6.0° past rising Vega and ends 2.5° short of setting Vega. That is within the spacing of the compass, and it is why the first and last etak on the strip are drawn partial.
  • The names are one school's. All 32 points are in the published compass Goodenough 1953, though I saw Goodenough's and Halpern's lists only in extracts. The local names, their spellings and the east-up layout come from the Weriyeng list. So does the placing of the two "leaning" Southern Cross points: the Weriyeng compass defines them as where Acrux stands when the cross's long axis leans 45° McDonald, n.d.. I adopt that definition and could not check it against how navigators judge the lean.
  • Do the islands "really" move? I suspect it is mostly outsiders who ask. Gladwin and Hutchins both treat etak as a way of organising and computing, not a belief; Hutchins and Hinton argue that the canoe-still frame is a sound choice from a canoe, where the star points stay fixed while everything near moves Hutchins & Hinton 1984. Hutchins also says plainly that, because he was not able to sail with these navigators, he cannot be sure his explanation is adequate Hutchins, n.d.. I have not sailed with them either.
  • The units. The Penn Museum account gives distances in "miles"; its 120 miles to Pisaras matches statute miles (the geometry gives 118). The page works in nautical miles throughout.

7 Take it with you

The proof stops here. Next time you travel in a straight line with a window beside you, a train, a bus, a car you are not driving, try it.

  1. Find something very far away: a ridge, a tower on the skyline, the Moon. It is your star. Watch it for a minute: it barely slides, and the Moon not at all.
  2. Find something in the middle distance off to one side: a farm, a hill, a chimney. It is your Pisaras. Hold yourself still and let it slide backward against the far thing.
  3. Notice when it moves fastest: when it is beside you. Notice that you can tell how far you have come by where it has got to.
  4. Now find what does not slide. Whatever stays fixed against the far things and grows is where you are going, or what is coming to meet you.

If it works, the vehicle stops being the thing that moves. That part I cannot prove.
