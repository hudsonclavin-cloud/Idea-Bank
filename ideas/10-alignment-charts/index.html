How a straight line does arithmetic

Collinearity is an equation

Three points lie on one line exactly when a 3×3 determinant of their coordinates is zero. Rewrite a three-variable formula so that each row of that determinant depends on only one variable, and each variable becomes a curve on paper. The formula has turned into the sentence “these three points are in line”, which a ruler can check. Maurice d’Ocagne made a discipline of this in the 1880s and 90s and named it nomography.

Sums become parallel lines

For f(u) + g(v) = h(w), put u and v on two parallel lines with spacings m1 and m2 per unit. The w scale sits between them, m1/(m1+m2) of the way across, with spacing m1m2/(m1+m2). Logarithms turn products into sums, and log-odds turn Bayes’ theorem into one. When a formula will not become a sum, a scale can bend (the quadratic) or the lines can meet at an angle (the harmonic sum).

Warping changes nothing

A projective transformation, which is what a camera does to a flat page seen at a slant, sends straight lines to straight lines. So a warped working chart is still a working chart. Nomographers used this to spread out the part of a scale they cared about. Press Warp the paper and drag a corner: the ticks crowd and spread and re-thin themselves, and the reading stays exactly where it was.

Nothing behind the curtain

The reading on this page is found the way your ruler finds it: the program intersects the line through your two pins with the drawn third scale. The formula is shown only as a cross-check underneath. If the two ever disagreed, the chart would be wrong; the tests that ship with this page check that they agree on more than 1,700 random readings, flat and warped.
