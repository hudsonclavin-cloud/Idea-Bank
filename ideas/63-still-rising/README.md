# Still Rising
> The inflow is falling and the tub is still filling. A practice ladder for one question you can ask of almost anything: what is piling up here, and right now, is more coming in than going out?

**Kind:** trainer
**Run:** Open `ideas/63-still-rising/index.html` in a browser. Plain classic scripts, so double-clicking the file works; no server, build, network or install. Tests, from the repo root: `node --test ideas/63-still-rising/still-rising.test.mjs` (pure Node, about 25 seconds), and `node --test ideas/63-still-rising/ui.test.mjs` (draws in the real page with Playwright, when it is installed).
**Made by:** Tidemark

## What it is

A trainer for one mental tool: **stock-and-flow thinking**, sometimes called bathtub thinking.

Almost any quantity you care about is either a *stock*, an amount you could count at an instant (people in a shop, water in a reservoir, patients on a ward, money in an account, a country's debt, carbon in the air), or a *flow*, a rate that only makes sense per unit of time (arrivals per minute, rainfall, a deficit, emissions, inflation). The tool is one fact and one habit:

- **The fact:** a stock changes only by inflow minus outflow. `level(t) = level(start) + ∫ (in − out) dt`. It is an identity, so it cannot be wrong.
- **The habit:** when you hear that something is rising or falling, ask *is that a stock or a flow?*, find the tub it fills, and compare what is coming in with what is going out *right now*.

Most of what follows from it is the same fact seen from different sides, and each is a rung of the ladder:

1. **Which way?** (8) A stock rises whenever more comes in than goes out, *whatever either flow is doing*. A falling inflow can still be filling the tub.
2. **Fullest and emptiest** (8) A stock can only turn where the two flows cross, or at the ends of the period. So it usually peaks after the inflow peaks, sometimes long after (though if more goes out than comes in the whole time, it is fullest at the very start), and when the lines cross more than once you have to compare the areas between them.
3. **Draw it** (5) Sketch the level from the flows with your finger. What is marked is where your line turns (where the lines cross) and which way it goes in between: up while the in-line is on top, down while it is underneath. How high it goes is not marked.
4. **How much is in it?** (5) The level at a time is where you started plus the area under the in-line minus the area under the out-line.
5. **Spot the tub** (11) No graph. Headlines and short stories in which you have to find the stock yourself: inflation and prices, deficit and debt, births and population, emissions and CO₂, a record number of fixes and a growing waiting list.
6. **Time in the tub** (7) When a tub has settled, level = throughput × time inside (Little's law). Count the queue ahead of you and time one service, and you know your wait; know how many red blood cells you have and how long one lives, and you know how many your body makes each second.
7. **Where it lies** (7) The tool's real limits, as exercises: a snapshot over-represents long stays; level ÷ flow is not the average stay while a tub is still filling (if the inflow has only ever grown, and newcomers stay about as long as earlier arrivals did, it is a lower bound; if the inflow has already peaked, it can overstate the stay); the flows may respond to the level or to what you do; a record flow can sit beside a shrinking stock; when a stock and its flows disagree there is a flow you have not counted; and when things pass through quickly, the stock really does follow the inflow, so the shortcut the ladder trains you out of is sometimes right.

That is 51 exercises on the rungs, plus a first question asked cold, before any teaching (a shop), and a last one that is its twin in a different setting (a flu ward): 53 hand-made exercises in all. All four graph rungs also draw fresh exercises from a seeded generator, in five more settings, once their hand-made ones have been used, on the rung itself or in a review.

How a question goes: you read a short situation, look at a chart of the two flows, and answer by tapping the moment you choose, drawing, or picking. Then the hidden level panel sweeps in from left to right while a little tub beside it fills and empties and leaves a tidemark at its highest; the gap between the two flow lines is shaded teal where the tub is filling and orange where it is draining; and a sentence built from the exact numbers says what really happened. A wrong answer is diagnosed by the mistake it most resembles: *"Minute 5 is about when the 'coming in' line is highest. But the number of people in the shop keeps rising for as long as more comes in than goes out"*, or *"that is a turning point, but not the highest one"*, or *"that adds what came in but forgets what went out"*.

Three right in a row, first try, clears a rung. Once a rung is cleared, every fourth question is a review from the rungs behind you, and anything you missed comes back first: a graph question as a fresh one of the same kind in another setting, so you cannot pass it by remembering the chart, and a word problem as itself. After the seventh rung comes the twin of the first question, a page that sets your first answer beside your last, and then "open water": everything mixed, until every exercise has been seen.

## Why I made it

The finding that started this is one of the strangest I know in the psychology of reasoning. Cronin, Gonzalez and Sterman (2009) showed MIT graduate students a graph of people entering and leaving a department store, minute by minute. Nearly all of them could say when the most people entered and when the most left. Far fewer could say when the most people were *in the store*, which needs nothing but the observation that the crowd grows while more come in than go out. A common wrong answer reads the stock straight off the inflow: the store is fullest when the most people are arriving. The researchers call it the correlation heuristic: assume the output looks like the input.

That would be a curiosity if headlines were not almost all flows. *Inflation falls. The deficit shrinks. New cases are down. Housebuilding hits a ten-year low. Emissions have peaked.* The thing you actually care about is nearly always the stock: prices, debt, the number of people ill, the number of homes, the CO₂ in the air. Every one of those headlines is compatible with the stock still rising. Sterman and Booth Sweeney (2007) found the same error in thinking about climate: asked what it would take to stabilise atmospheric CO₂, most of their highly educated participants, graduate students with strong technical backgrounds among them, drew emissions that stopped growing but stayed above what is removed, and so a level that kept rising.

So I wanted a tool that would make one question automatic, and I wanted to train it the way you learn to read a clock: not by being told, but by being wrong in front of a chart several times in a row, in different places, until the right reading becomes the first one. The brief I was working to asked for something with a part that can be proven and a part that can only be felt. Here the proven part is the bathtub equation itself, and every answer in the bank follows from it exactly. The felt part is the moment the level panel sweeps in and the shop keeps filling for seven more minutes after the arrivals peaked, which an explanation does not give you.

Before choosing I listed: dimensional analysis, inversion ("how would I make this fail?"), finding the bottleneck, the outside view, thinking at the margin, scaling (what happens at 10× and 0.1×), regression to the mean, base rates, Little's law on its own, and stocks and flows. I picked stocks and flows because it is the one I see reasonable people get wrong most often in public argument, because it is exact (every exercise has one checkable answer, so the feedback can be immediate and honest), and because the answer can be *drawn*, which made it the most fun to practise on a phone. Little's law turned out to be the same tool seen from the steady state, so it became rung 6 instead of a separate idea.

## How it works

**The arithmetic (`tub.js`).** Every graph exercise gives its inflow and outflow as piecewise-linear curves. Their difference is linear between the union of the knots, so the level is a sum of exact trapezoids. The level's highest and lowest points can only be at the ends of the period or where in − out is zero, so those are the only candidates, and the extremes are exact too. The same file holds the grading rules, the misconception diagnostics, and the seeded generator.

**Grading, kind by kind.** Each rule grades the thing the tool claims to tell you, and nothing else.

- *Which way?* Right if you name the sign of in − out at the marked moment. Seven of the eight are asked where a line is heading the misleading way (the inflow falling while still on top, the inflow climbing while still underneath, or the outflow climbing while still underneath); the eighth asks about a moment when arrivals, after falling all morning, exactly match departures. Answering from the trend of a line gets seven of the eight wrong (tested). At the eighth both lines are flat and equal, so someone reading the lines as they are at that moment says steady and is right; someone who goes by the morning's fall says falling.
- *Fullest and emptiest.* You are graded on how full the tub really was at the moment you chose: right if the level there is within 3% (of the level's whole range over the period) of the true highest or lowest level. A broad flat top forgives more than a sharp peak, which is how it should be. For every one of these, the answer you get by reading the extreme off a single flow (the inflow's peak for a highest, the outflow's peak for a lowest) is graded wrong (tested).
- *Draw it.* What a sketch should show is where the level turns, so that is what is graded. Your line is resampled at 121 times and smoothed (a moving average over 4% of the period either side), and its turning points are found: a top counts once your line has come back down by 5% of its own height range (a bottom, the same upside down). Each place where the level really turns, a crossing of the flows, must be matched, in order, by a turn of the same kind in your line, inside a band of 5% of the period either side of the crossing (wider where the true level stays within 3% of its range of the turning level, the rule rung 2 uses for taps). No other turn in your line may sit outside those bands once it comes back by a tenth of its height range. Those are looked for on a copy of your line smoothed only over 1.5% of the period either side, so a short extra wiggle is not averaged away, though it is cut down: a sharp V 8% of the period wide keeps about three quarters of how far it comes back on a level stretch, but only about half between turns, where the line is climbing or falling and where an extra wiggle sits (a wider V keeps more, a narrower spike less). So a wiggle drawn well over a tenth of the height can pass; the honest assessment below gives the sizes. A turn outside the bands that comes back by less than a tenth on that light copy (down to 5% of its height) is not marked either way, but on a pass it is circled faintly and named in the feedback, so a pass does not hide a visible extra turn. Between the bands, wherever the true level clearly moves, your line must go the same way at least 80% of the time, judged against your line's own height range, so a gentle slope counts as long as it visibly moves. A line whose whole range is under 4% of the panel counts as flat, and is marked wrong with a sentence saying so. Height and scale are not graded. After you answer, the bands are shaded, your turns are circled (green if they landed, red if not, faint and dashed if they were too small to mark), and the feedback says which turn was missed, early, late or extra, or where your line was too flat. On all five hand-made sketches these fail: a sketch shaped like the inflow (also with the inflow delayed by 5% or 10% of the period), a straight rising line, a flat line, a line that stays level where the level falls, one that skips a turn, and one with an extra turn. On generated sketches the inflow shape, the straight and flat lines, a skipped turn and an extra turn fail too; a delayed inflow is not always wrong there, because on a few of them the level does turn soon after the inflow does (at a 10% delay it passes on 41 of 600), the case the sandwich-shop exercise is about. For extra turns (in the tests, a triangular bump 10% to 24% of the period wide added to the true curve of 30 sketches, in 894 places where it gives the line an extra turn of a fifth of its height or more, fails every time; in a wider sweep over 35 sketches with bumps 8% to 24% of the period wide, all but 1 of 5,134 such bumps failed, the one that passed a spike 8% of the period wide; narrower spikes were not swept). A sketch with every turn moved 15% of the period early or late always fails when drawn cleanly. With a 1.5% wobble added it fails about 97% of the time on the debt moved early, every time on the rest of the hand-made five (10,000 tries each), and 99.9% of the time across 600 generated sketches (200 tries each way). The exceptions are broad bands: the wobble can carry the drawn turn back inside one, where the level really is within 3% of its turning level, so the band is doing its job. On the worst of the 29 generated sketches with a band over a fifth of the period wide, the moved sketch passes about one time in four; where every band is narrower, at most about 6% of the time. The true curve passes; so do straight-stroke versions with every slope after the first cut to a half (or, on the hand-made five, a third), the true curve at half (or a quarter of) its height, and the true curve with a 1% wobble. With a 2% wobble (smooth, or jitter on every point), at least 94% pass on every hand-made sketch over 10,000 tries each: the reservoir with jitter is lowest (94.7%), then the debt (about 95%), whose turns sit on the flattest stretches; the bath, the theme park and the savings pass over 99% of the time.
- *How much?* Four options: the right one and three distractors, each a named mistake (forgot the outflow, forgot the inflow, forgot the starting level, counted the start twice, subtracted the change, added the outflow, or took the gap between the lines at that moment as if it had held all along), so a wrong pick gets the sentence for that mistake. The distractors are chosen so that the right answer's size gives nothing away. Each exercise says how many of its three wrong options are smaller than the right one. On the five hand-made ones, in the order you meet them, the right answer is the largest of the four (the bath), the third (the shop), the second (the ward), and then the smallest (the reservoir and the market stall). On generated ones the count steps through 0 to 3 with the exercise's number, so any four numbered one after another cover every rank. Of the *How much?* questions that 400 simulated learners, right four times in five, are shown on the way to the twin, the right answer is the smallest 26%, second 24%, third 25% and largest 25% (tested).
- *Word problems.* Every wrong option has its own feedback sentence explaining why it is wrong, and every number in them is recomputed in the tests.

**The progression (`trainer.js`).** The cold first question is a baseline. Within a rung the hand-made exercises come in order, each in a different setting (shops, baths, reservoirs, wards, start-ups, car parks, snow, bug trackers, the air, festivals, warehouses, inboxes, populations). A rung is cleared by three right answers in a row, first attempt. Every fourth question after the first cleared rung is a review from the cleared rungs: missed exercises first (each waits at least three questions), then unseen ones, then fresh generated ones. A missed graph exercise comes back as a freshly generated one of the same kind in another setting, and getting that right counts for the one you missed; a missed word problem comes back as itself. Reviews do not name their rung. Each exercise carries a Leitner box (0 to 3) that a miss resets.

The design borrows three well-established findings from the learning literature, without claiming they transfer to this exact case, and applies two of them only lightly. Practising retrieval with feedback is the core of it. Spacing (Cepeda et al. 2006) is here only as gaps of a few questions within one sitting, far shorter than the gaps of days that work best for lasting retention. Interleaving (Rohrer and Taylor 2007; Kornell and Bjork 2008) is a light dose: practice is mostly blocked by rung, with one question in four a review from earlier rungs once one is cleared, and everything mixed only in open water after the ladder. A learner who never errs climbs in 29 questions: 21 on the rung they are on, 6 reviews, and the first and last questions.

Three in a row is a light gate, and I want to be clear about how light. A guesser has a 1 in 27 chance of three in a row on a three-way question, but chances accumulate over a rung: within the eight hand-made questions of rung 1, someone pressing the three buttons at random clears it 16% of the time, and someone guessing only between "rising" and "falling" 29% of the time (one of the eight is "steady", which that guesser always misses); both computed exactly in the tests. The gate is aimed at the systematic mistake, not at a guesser: someone who answers from the trend of a line gets seven of the eight hand-made rung-1 questions wrong and every generated one, so never gets three in a row and never clears it. For a self-training tool that seemed the right trade; a test would need more questions.

**The generator.** For extra practice on the graph rungs, `Tub.generate` builds an exercise from one of five templates (a museum, a parcel depot, a clinic waiting room, a lake, a help-desk queue) and a seed, by sampling two humped flows on fixed knots, starting the tub high enough never to go negative, and retrying until the exercise is fair: the level moves visibly, the answer window is a single stretch between 2% and 25% of the period, a *Which way?* question is asked where the inflow's trend points the wrong way and the flows differ by at least 15% of the largest flow, a *How much?* answer is a whole number with four distinct options (with the number of wrong ones below the right one stepping through 0 to 3 with the seed), and for peaks and troughs the trend-reading answer is wrong. A generated sketch must have turns that are plain to see and to draw: the level moves at least 15% of its range between turns (and to and from the ends), turns sit at least 8% of the period from the ends and 15% from each other, the true curve passes the sketch grader, and a sketch shaped like the inflow fails. The tests generate 1,000 exercises and recompute each independently, and check that 4,000 more seeds all give fair ones.

**Where the tool fails, and what the ladder says about it.** The equation is an identity, so the failures are all in how you use it:

- *It gives direction, not destination.* Given the flows, it tells you which way the level moves and where it turns. It does not tell you how the flows will respond to the level, or to what you do. A fuller tub often drains faster; a wider road often brings more traffic. Rung 7 has one exercise on each.
- *Little's law needs a settled tub.* Level ÷ flow is the average time in the tub only when inflow and outflow have balanced for a while. In a tub whose inflow has only ever grown, it is a lower bound on the average stay, as long as newcomers stay about as long as earlier arrivals did. Still filling is not enough: a tub can keep filling after its inflow has peaked, and then level ÷ today's inflow can overstate the stay.
- *A snapshot is not the flow.* Walk a ward and ask the patients how long they will stay and you get the length-biased average (8.2 days in the exercise), not the average per admission (5 days).
- *You have to pick the right tub.* A record flow (loans, admissions, fixes) can sit beside a shrinking stock.
- *Sometimes the shortcut is right.* When things pass through quickly compared with how fast the flows change, the level is roughly inflow × time inside and does follow the inflow; the sandwich-shop exercise is there so the ladder does not overclaim. The tool matters most where things stay long: debts, carbon, populations, backlogs.
- *Missing flows are a feature.* When a stock and its known flows disagree, the identity does not break: it tells you there is a flow you have not counted (the audit exercise).

**Files.** `tub.js` (arithmetic, grading, generator), `bank.js` (every exercise as data), `words.js` (every feedback sentence, built from the computed numbers), `trainer.js` (progression and stored-state cleaning) are pure and load both in the page and under Node. `app.js` draws the SVG charts and handles touch, mouse and keys; `style.css` has light and dark schemes and honours reduced motion (the sweep becomes an instant reveal). Drawing happens on an HTML overlay with `touch-action: none` over the level panel only, so the rest of the page still scrolls under a finger. Progress is kept in `localStorage` inside `try/catch`, and everything read back is validated; the page works without it.

## Honest assessment

- [Certain] `still-rising.test.mjs` passes 28 tests. It integrates every graph exercise two independent ways (each flow's own trapezoids, and a 20,000-step midpoint sum) and compares them with the engine; finds every extreme and turning point by brute-force scan; checks every stated answer, every amount option against the mistake it names, the numbers in the stories against the curves, and every number in the word problems from first principles (1,000 + 120 + 90 + 60 = 1,270; 12 × 40 s = 8 min; 90 an hour × ⅓ hour = 30; 25 × 10¹² ÷ (120 × 86,400 s) ≈ 2.41 million a second; (5 × 1 + 45 × 9) ÷ 50 = 8.2 days; and so on); generates 1,000 exercises and recomputes them; attacks the sketch grader from both sides (turns moved early or late, skipped, doubled by an extra wiggle, or replaced by a level stretch must fail; gentle, shrunken and wobbly versions of the right shape must pass), and measures how much of an extra wiggle the light smoothing takes off and what size of one passes; and simulates learners to show that every exercise is reachable, that the cold question comes first and its twin after the ladder, that a missed graph exercise comes back as a fresh one of the same kind, that the progression rules behave as described, and that the right *How much?* answer is spread over all four ranks in what learners are shown. `ui.test.mjs` passes 4 more, which draw sketches in the real page in headless Chromium, with a mouse at 1280 px and with touch at 390 px (it skips itself where Playwright is not installed). `node tools/check.mjs ideas/63-still-rising` passes, including the smoke test at desktop and phone sizes.
- [Certain] A headless Chromium run played the whole ladder through the real page at 390 px with touch input (taps, and finger-drawn sketches sent as touch events, in two strokes), in light and dark schemes, with and without reduced motion; after the revision below it was run again: 37 questions from the first look to the summary, with five deliberate wrong answers, one sketch drawn with its peak too early (marked wrong, with the right reason) and one drawn with gentle, wobbly slopes (marked right), and missed graph questions coming back as fresh ones. - [Likely] The research I lean on is as I describe it. I could not reach the full texts from where I built this and worked from abstracts and summaries: Cronin, Gonzalez and Sterman (2009, *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* 108:116–130), reported as 96% and 95% of 173 MIT graduate students reading the flow questions correctly, with the stock questions answered far less well; Sterman and Booth Sweeney (2007, *Climatic Change* 80:213–238) on the CO₂ error; Sterman (2010, *System Dynamics Review*) finding that a system dynamics course reduced the correlation heuristic without removing it. The learning-science references in How it works (Cepeda et al. 2006 on spacing; Rohrer and Taylor 2007 and Kornell and Bjork 2008 on interleaving) are cited from memory of that literature, not re-read for this. The exercise numbers that come from the world are few and sourced in the page: the 2012–2021 averages of the Global Carbon Budget 2022 (10.8 billion tonnes of carbon emitted a year, about 6.0 taken up by ocean and land) and Sender, Fuchs and Milo's (2016) estimate of about 25 trillion red blood cells in a 70 kg adult. Every other number is invented to make a clean exercise and says so where it matters.
- [Speculative] **That anyone will keep using this.** No human has trained with it. Everything about pacing, difficulty, whether the climb is too long or too short, and whether the habit transfers from these charts to the next headline you read, is untested. The length is only simulated: 29 questions for a learner who never errs, a median of about 45 for one right four times in five, and about 58 for one right seven times in ten (the people this is for, who start out making the mistake, will be at the long end). The literature is not encouraging about quick fixes: Sterman's course helped but did not cure, and Xie, Hurlstone and Walker (2018) note that earlier interventions to correct the CO₂ error had not produced robust improvements, though in their own study, discussing the task in a group did help. My bet is that varied, immediate, slightly embarrassing practice does better than explanation, but it is a bet.
- [Speculative] The cold-then-twin comparison is a pleasing before and after, not evidence: it is one question each, the twin comes after at least 27 questions about exactly this, and the twin's setting differs. Nothing measures retention after a break.
- [Certain] The charts are stylised: smooth piecewise-linear flows, no noise, no measurement error. Real data is jagged, and deciding when two noisy lines have "crossed" is a judgement the ladder does not train.
- [Certain] The sketch grader checks where the line turns and which way it goes, not heights: a sketch with its turns in the right places and absurd heights passes. That is deliberate (the tool tells you direction and turning points), but it means rung 3 does not train magnitude; rung 4 does. The tolerances are a judgement: a turn may land within 5% of the period of the crossing (more on a broad, flat top), a hand that wobbles by 2% of the panel fails a correct sketch about 1 time in 20 on the two hardest exercises, and an extra wiggle passes (named in the feedback, but not marked) if, after the light smoothing, it comes back by less than a tenth of the line's height. That smoothing takes off about a quarter of a sharp wiggle 8% of the period wide on a level stretch, but about half between turns, where the line is climbing or falling and where an extra wiggle sits, and more of a narrower one. So a wiggle drawn well over a tenth of the height can pass, and how far over depends on its width, its shape and where it sits. On the hand-made five, a symmetric V 8% of the period wide passes in some places at a fifth of the height, and a spike 4% wide usually passes at a quarter. The largest found to pass in the independent checks were a symmetric 8% V coming back by about a quarter of the height (on the theme park), an off-centre V of the same width by about 31%, and a 4% spike by about 45%. Those are the largest found, not limits.
- [Likely] The practice is mostly blocked by rung, labelled on screen, with every graph question showing both flows on one labelled axis; the learner never has to find the tub in a chart until rung 5. That makes the ladder easier than the real thing, and the twin at the end is near transfer in the same format.
- [Likely] The "fresh" generated exercises are fair but generic: they reuse five settings, so they practise the skill, not the transfer. The hand-made bank is where the variety is.
- There is no sound. Nothing on the page needed it.

## Where it could go next

- **Real data.** A rung of noisy, real series (a reservoir's daily releases, a hospital's admissions and discharges, a country's births and deaths) where you also have to decide whether the lines have really crossed.
- **Feedback loops.** A rung where the outflow depends on the level, so you predict where the tub settles and how fast: the first step from bathtubs to system dynamics.
- **Headline mode.** Paste a news sentence; it asks you to name the stock, the flows, and what the sentence does and does not tell you about the stock.
- **Measure it.** Keep, on the device, how often the first answer on each rung is right over weeks, and whether the cold-style mistakes come back after a break. That would turn the [Speculative] line above into a number for one person at least.
- **Two tubs in a row.** Infections feeding hospital admissions feeding beds: a chain of stocks, each lagging the last, which is where the intuition fails most expensively.

## Revision note

An independent reviewer rebuilt the arithmetic in separate code and confirmed every answer, amount, turning point, word-problem number and guessing chance. It found real problems in the sketch grader and in a few general claims; these changes come from that review:

- **The sketch grader was lenient on timing and harsh on gentle slopes.** It passed a theme-park peak 2.5 hours early and a debt peak 6 years late, yet failed correct sketches with a gentle slope while saying only direction was marked. It now grades turning points: each crossing must be matched by a drawn turn of the same kind within 5% of the period (wider on a broad, flat top, by rung 2's rule), and no big drawn turn may sit anywhere else. It smooths the line first and judges flatness against the line's own height, not the panel's. The chart shades each band and circles your turns, and the feedback names the real reason: a turn early, late, missed or extra, or a stretch too flat. New tests pin both sides (turns moved 15% of the period fail; gentle, shrunken and wobbly right shapes pass; inflow-shaped, straight and flat sketches still fail), and generated sketches now need turns that are plain to see.
- **The level ÷ flow rule had lost its condition.** "At best a lower bound while still filling" is false in general. It is a lower bound only if the inflow has only ever grown and newcomers stay about as long as earlier arrivals did. A tub still filling after its inflow has peaked can make level ÷ flow overstate the stay. The rung-7 summary, the growing-app answer and its explanation now say so.
- **"Interleaving rather than blocking" overstated the design.** The ladder is mostly blocked by rung. The README now says so, with the numbers. Reviews no longer name their rung, and a missed graph question now comes back as a freshly generated one of the same kind, not the same chart.
- **Smaller fixes:**
  - Trend-reading gets seven of the eight rung-1 questions wrong, not all eight.
  - The intro gives the simulated question counts, not "about thirty".
  - All four graph rungs draw generated exercises, not two.
  - A stock does not always peak after its inflow.
  - The Xie, Hurlstone and Walker citation now gives that paper's own positive finding.
  - On *How much?* the right answer used to be never the largest option. Generated exercises now put it at each rank equally often; the hand-made ones were missed at first (see the third check below).

A second independent check of the revised entry confirmed those fixes and found two claims about the new sketch grader that did not hold; re-measuring turned up a third:

- **The rule against extra turns was much weaker than stated.** It looked for them on the heavily smoothed line, which roughly halves a wiggle 8–10% of the period wide on a level stretch, and takes off most of one between turns. So a reservoir sketch that rises again from month 9 to 11 by about a quarter of its height was marked Right, with "Your line turns where the water in the reservoir turns". That rise follows the inflow's trough while the inflow is still underneath, which is the very mistake the ladder trains against. About two in five sketches given an extra turn of a fifth of their height passed. Extra turns are now looked for on the lightly smoothed line, at a tenth of its height range, and are circled red. A smaller turn is circled faintly and named in the feedback instead of passing in silence. A new test pins the reservoir and savings sketches the check found, 894 bumped sketches, and the note on small wiggles; it fails on the previous code. In the real page (390 px with touch and 1280 px with a mouse, light and dark), both sketches are now marked wrong with their extra turns circled red, and a debt drawn with a small dip passes with the dip circled faintly and named.
- **The figure for moved turns drawn with a wobble was too high.** The README put their failure rate at 98% or more, with the debt the only exception. The debt moved early fails about 97% of the time (10,000 tries), and on generated sketches with a broad band the wobble can carry the turn back into the band, up to about one time in four on the worst of 600. The README now gives those numbers and why. A third check confirmed the fixes above and found one bug they had brought in and one fix that was only half done:

- **A flat sketch could not be marked in the page.** The grader now calls a line flat when its whole range is under 4% of the panel, but the page treated every result other than a graded one as a line too short. So a flat or nearly flat line drawn right across the chart got "Draw across more of the chart first." under "Ready to check.", and was never marked. The page now sends back only a line that does not reach far enough; a flat one is marked wrong with "Your line is nearly flat", and the bands where the level turns are shaded. A new `ui.test.mjs` draws flat lines and a low hump in the real page, with a mouse and with touch, and fails on the previous code.
- **The *How much?* fix covered only generated exercises.** On the five hand-made ones, which every learner meets first, the right answer was still never the largest. For that, the shop starts with 60 people inside rather than 12, so that two of the named mistakes come out smaller than the answer. Of the *How much?* questions shown to 400 simulated learners right four times in five, the right answer had been the largest 10% of the time; it is now 25%. The test checks the hand-made ranks, that any four generated exercises in a row cover every rank, that each rank gets between 15% and 35% of what learners are shown, and that this README quotes the figures.

A fourth check confirmed those and found two sentences that overstated, which the coordinator fixed by hand:

- **"Fullest at the very start" needed its condition.** Rung 2 said a tub is fullest at the start "if more goes out than comes in from the start". A tub that drains at first can refill above where it began, as some of the rung's own exercises do. It now says "the whole time".
- **The extra-turn tolerance was stated on the wrong line.** The tenth of the line's height is measured after the light smoothing, which takes off part of a short wiggle, so a wiggle drawn well over a tenth can pass. The honest assessment now says so, the feedback on such a wiggle no longer calls it "small next to your line's whole height", and the bump sweep's figure now gives the bump widths it covered.
- **A line drawn starting after an early turn was told it "stops before" that turn.** It is now told it starts after it. A test searches 400 seeds of every template for such lines and checks the wording.

A fifth check confirmed those and found that the new sentence on the light smoothing still understated what passes:

- **The light smoothing cuts an extra wiggle by about half, not a quarter.** A quarter is right only for a V on a level stretch. An extra wiggle sits between turns, where the line is climbing or falling, and there the smoothing rounds off both of its corners, taking off about half of how far it comes back. So on the hand-made five a sharp V 8% of the period wide drawn at a fifth of the height passes in some places, and a spike 4% wide usually passes at a quarter. The Draw-it bullet, which said such a V keeps three quarters of its depth, and the honest assessment now give both figures and those sizes; the Draw-it bullet also gives the widths of the bumps its 894-bump test uses, and says that the faint, unmarked turns are those that come back by less than a tenth on the light copy. The second check's note on the heavy smoothing had the same gap: it roughly halves such a wiggle only on a level stretch, and between turns takes off most of it; that note now says so. A new test (the suite now has 28) measures the figures on the hand-made five and checks that this README quotes them; it fails on the previous README.

A sixth check confirmed that and found one sentence it had added that was false:

- **An 8%-wide V can pass at a quarter, just.** The honest assessment said such a V passes on the hand-made five "nowhere at a quarter". That came from trying places a fortieth of the period apart, which miss the few where it does: on the theme park, a V from 10:04 to 11:02 that comes back by exactly a quarter of the line's height is marked Right, in the grader and in the real page (1280 px with a mouse and 390 px with touch, light and dark), and fails at 26%. Trying the V every five-thousandth of the period, clear of the turns, both dipping against the level and overshooting it, 11 of about 4,200 tries on the theme park pass at a quarter, none on the other four (where the most that passes is 20–24%), and none anywhere at 25.25% or more. The sentence now says it passes in a very few places on the theme park at a quarter and none beyond about 25%. The test now tries places a thousandth of the period apart, checks the theme-park V at a quarter and at 26%, and checks that nothing passes at 26%; it fails on the previous README.

A seventh check found that the sizes just added were still read as limits, and they were not: an 8%-wide V whose point sits off-centre passes at up to about 31% of the height, and a 4%-wide spike at up to about 45%. Each check so far had answered the last one with a tighter bound, and each bound had an exception, so the coordinator stopped giving bounds: the honest assessment now gives the typical sizes the test measures and the largest sizes the independent checks found, and says those are not limits.
