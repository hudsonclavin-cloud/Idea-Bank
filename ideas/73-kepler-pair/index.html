The Kepler Pair

Two printable gears whose shapes are Kepler's second law. Turn one steadily, as time goes, and the other carries Mercury round its orbit, 1.55 times its average speed at perihelion and 0.67 at aphelion. Kepler's equation has no closed-form answer; the wheels give it by their shape.

What the gears are doing

A planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times, so it speeds up near the Sun. Its angle round from perihelion, θ, is tied to time only through Kepler's equation, M = E − e sin E, which has no closed-form solution for θ. A pair of gears can turn one into the other anyway. Where the two wheels touch, the ratio of their radii is the ratio of their speeds. Cut the wheels so that, at every point of the turn, that ratio is the planet's speed relative to its average, (1 + e cos θ)² / (1 − e²)3/2, and keep the two radii adding up to the distance between the pegs. Then, while the time wheel turns evenly, the other wheel's angle is Kepler's: exactly, for the ideal curves, and to within the play of the printed teeth and pegs for the real ones. No series, no iteration: the solution is the shape.

The dots on the plate are equal steps of time. Round the knob they are evenly spaced; round the Sun they lie on the orbit, bunched at aphelion and spread at perihelion, and each neighbouring pair encloses the same area with the Sun. Turn the knob until a time dot shows in its window; the planet's window then shows the matching orbit dot.

The trick is old. Since 1732, cometaria have shown a planet's uneven speed with two elliptical wheels joined by a cord, which does not follow Kepler's law exactly; Carl Zeiss patented non-circular wheels for Kepler's motion in planetarium projectors in 1965. What this page adds is a pair you can print, with its clearances taken from a fit table and its reading error estimated in code.

Against the sky

The law the wheels are cut to is the two-body ellipse, with each orbit's eccentricity and period. JPL's DE441 ephemeris, which includes every planet's pull, gives where each body really was at the times of the dots, seen from the Sun. The two agree to within:

Printing and putting it together

  1. Print the coupon first (PLA, 0.2 mm layers, no supports). Drop each ring on the peg: one notch is 0.5 mm, two 0.6, three 0.7. Choose the smallest that spins freely with a flick, and set the bore above to match.
  2. Print the base plate and both wheels flat, as they lie in the files: 0.2 mm layers, 3 perimeters, no supports, no brim, and no skirt: on a 180 mm bed a slicer's usual skirt, 6 mm out, runs off the edge with the larger plates, the default's among them. Use 15–20% infill for the plate and the time wheel, and print the Kepler wheel at 100% infill: its counterweight only balances it if it is solid. For dark dots, change filament at the layer that starts at 2.4 mm: the dot floors stay the first colour.
  3. Turn the Kepler wheel (the one with the collar) so its spoke points at the doubled orbit dot, and drop it on the Sun's peg. Turn the time wheel so its spoke points at the doubled time dot and lower it onto the other peg, nudging the knob until the teeth slip in. If the spokes are not both on their doubled dots, lift the time wheel and seat it again: one tooth out, the pair jams or jumps a tooth within a turn.
  4. Turn the knob clockwise. Each dot under the knob's window is a step of time; the planet's window shows where the planet is.

This design has not been printed yet. The tests prove the geometry; only a printer can prove the fit.

The part you have to turn

For Mercury, the arithmetic says the planet wheel runs 1.55 times as fast as the knob at perihelion. Turning the knob at an even pace, I expect what you notice to be something else: the other wheel suddenly hurrying away round perihelion while your hand keeps its pace, then dawdling round the far side. You will see it more than feel it, since the planet wheel is light and pushes back on the knob hardly at all. Kepler found that law by years of arithmetic on Tycho Brahe's observations of Mars. If the wheels work as drawn, they show it in a few seconds; no one has turned a printed pair yet.
