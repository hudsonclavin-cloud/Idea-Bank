# The Kepler Pair
> Two printable gears whose shapes are Kepler's second law. Turn one steadily, as time goes, and the other carries Mercury round its orbit, at 1.55 times its average angular speed at perihelion and 0.67 times at aphelion. Kepler's equation has no closed-form solution; these wheels solve it with their outline, exactly on paper and to within several degrees once printed.

**Kind:** printable mechanism
**Run:** open `ideas/73-kepler-pair/index.html` in a browser (classic scripts, works from `file://`, no network). Drag either wheel, or use the buttons and keys; *Print one* makes the STLs for any orbit, size and fit it offers. `coupon.stl`, `default.stl` (the base plate), `default-time-wheel.stl` and `default-kepler-wheel.stl` are the default Mercury set, ready to slice. Settings can come from the URL: `?body=mars&C=66&bore=0.6`. Tests: `node --test ideas/73-kepler-pair/*.test.mjs` from the repo root (Node built-ins only, about a minute). With Playwright installed globally, `node ideas/73-kepler-pair/ui-check.mjs` drives the page in headless Chromium; `node ideas/73-kepler-pair/sweep.mjs` runs `tools/printcheck.py` on every part at every extreme setting.
**Made by:** Mean Anomaly

## What it is

Three printed parts and a calibration coupon, with no glue and no screws: a base plate with two 8 mm pegs, and two 16-tooth gears that drop onto them. The gears are not round. Their outlines are cut, in code, so that wherever the two wheels touch, the ratio of their radii is how fast Mercury is moving round the Sun at that point of its orbit, compared with its average. Turn the knob on one wheel at an even pace (it stands for time) and the other wheel turns the way Mercury does: 1.55 times as fast as the knob at perihelion, 0.67 times at aphelion.

Under the wheels the plate has two dials of engraved dots, 22 each, for 22 equal steps of Mercury's 88-day year: one dot is 4.0 days. Round the knob's peg they are evenly spaced. Round the other peg, which is the Sun, they lie on Mercury's orbit drawn to scale, bunched at aphelion and spread at perihelion, and any two neighbours enclose the same area with the Sun. Each wheel is a toothed ring with one spoke, and each spoke has a window. The planet wheel is lopsided, so half of it is solid out to the edge of the dial and, where that is not enough, it carries a raised counterweight: that keeps it level on its peg. Turn the knob until a time dot shows in the time wheel's window; the planet wheel's window then shows the orbit dot for the same moment. The doubled dot on each dial is perihelion.

The page draws the machine and turns it by drag, keys or buttons. Thin lines from the Sun show where a planet moving evenly round a circle would be, and where two identical elliptical gears (the old shortcut) would put it; a shaded wedge shows the area swept in the last dot of time, which never changes. *Set to today* turns the machine to today's date from DE441's perihelion times. A chart compares all three with Kepler over an orbit, with the printed pair's error budget drawn in. The page makes the STLs for four orbits:

## Why I made it

The brief was a mechanism, printable at home, whose gears, cams or linkages encode real numbers about space, with their error against the sky computed. I sketched three:

1. **A retrograde pointer.** Two cranks geared for Earth and Mars, and an arm between them that swings backwards for about ten weeks in every 780 days. It needs a rounded ratio (15:8 = 1.875 against 687.0 / 365.26 = 1.881), so it drifts, and it shows what any orrery shows.
2. **A sidereal-day differential,** turning once a solar day and once a sidereal day, to show the extra turn a year. The ratio 366.24 : 365.24 needs hundreds of teeth or another rounding, and there is little to feel.
3. **The Kepler pair.** Non-circular gears whose shape is the law. No ratio is rounded: the wheels turn one to one on average, and within each turn the ratio is Kepler's everywhere. It can be checked against JPL's ephemeris, and it has something to feel.

The third won. I did not start from nothing: an earlier session for this slot began the Kepler pair and stopped after a first version of the tooth cutting and the meshing check, with no tests. I kept the idea and treated that code as a stranger's; what I changed is in the honest assessment.

**The part that can be proven** is the outline. Where the wheels touch, their radii must add up to the distance between the pegs and their ratio must be the planet's relative speed. The tests check that the two outlines roll on each other without slipping, touch on the line between the pegs and are tangent there, and that integrating the ratio of their radii over a turn gives Kepler's equation back to 1e-9 radians; that neighbouring orbit dots enclose equal areas; that the teeth always leave room for Kepler's angle; and that the law agrees with DE441 to arcminutes.

**The part that can only be felt** is the hurry. Kepler found the area law by years of arithmetic on Tycho Brahe's observations of Mars. Turning the knob at an even pace, I expect what you notice is not a ratio but a change of mood: the other wheel suddenly hurrying away from you round perihelion, then dawdling round the far side, as if it had somewhere to be and then didn't. The gears were cut from an equation, but the hurrying is what Mercury does, and turning them is the way to feel it. It will be seen more than felt, I think: the planet wheel weighs 13 g, and by my estimate, with the friction guessed, what it pushes back on the knob changes by a few tenths of a newton-millimetre over an orbit, too little for fingers to notice.

**Not new.** Cometaria have shown this since 1732, when J. T. Desaguliers built one to show the change in Mercury's speed between perihelion and aphelion; most used two elliptical wheels joined by a crossed cord, and Beech (2002) showed by calculation that they do not reproduce Kepler's second law exactly. Carl Zeiss patented non-circular wheels for Kepler's motion in planetarium projectors (US 3,178,959, 1965). Entry 65 in this bank, *The Antikythera Crank*, shows the Greek answer for the Moon: a pin in a slot. A short search found no printable, exact Kepler pair, which is not the same as there being none.

## How it works

**The law in the outline.** A planet's angular speed, in units of its average, is *ρ*(*θ*) = (1 + *e* cos *θ*)² / (1 − *e*²)<sup>3/2</sup>, where *θ* is its angle from perihelion (the true anomaly) and *e* the eccentricity. Two gears on pegs a distance *C* apart touch on the line between the pegs, and their speeds are in the inverse ratio of their radii there. So the planet ("Kepler") wheel has radius *r*<sub>K</sub> = *C* / (1 + *ρ*(*θ*)) in the direction that touches at *θ*, and the time wheel *r*<sub>T</sub> = *C* − *r*<sub>K</sub> in the direction that touches at the matching time, *M* = *E* − *e* sin *E*, with *E* from *θ* in closed form. Written as functions of *θ*, neither outline needs Kepler's equation solved: the hard direction, *θ* from *M*, is what the gears do. Both outlines close after one turn with the same perimeter, so the wheels turn one to one on average, and both are convex (tested). They are nearly circles for Earth (radii 34.4–35.6 mm at *C* = 70 mm) and lopsided for Mercury (27.4–41.8 mm) and Pluto (25.8–43.2 mm). `kepler.js` does solve Kepler's equation, by Newton's method, but only to place the orbit dots (where each step of time puts the planet), for the page's readouts and for the tests: the wheels' outlines never need it.

**The teeth.** A straight rack with a 25° pressure angle is rolled without slipping along each pitch outline, 90 positions per tooth, and each wheel is whatever the rack never touches: the nearest point of the rack in each of 7,200 directions round the wheel's centre. Both wheels are cut by the same rack, so their teeth are conjugate. The tooth count is the whole number that makes the module nearest 4.2 mm (16 for Mercury and Pluto, 17 for Mars and Earth, at 70 mm), and the cut outline is reduced to a polygon within 0.1 mm of it (215 corners on the default Kepler wheel). The meshing check places the two polygons, densified every 0.25 mm, at any angles and peg positions and reports whether either pokes more than 0.001 mm into the other; `freeRange` finds how far the Kepler wheel can turn each way while the time wheel is held. With both wheels centred, pegs 70 mm apart and a 0.5 mm bore, the free range contains Kepler's angle at each of 3600 positions tried, and the most it reaches either side of it is 1.43° for Mercury and 1.52° for Pluto. These are the largest found, not bounds: 360 positions found 1.38° and 1.50°. Smaller wheels and looser bores, whose flanks are wider, have more: 1.65° for Mercury at 62 mm, 2.09° there with a 0.7 mm bore. The wheel is held both ways at every position tried.

**The dials.** The drawn orbit is as large as the Kepler wheel's open middle allows: its aphelion dot and the twin of its perihelion dot sit 1 mm inside the rim where the rim comes nearest the Sun. For Mercury at 70 mm that is 11.4 mm from the Sun's peg at perihelion and 17.3 mm at aphelion; the time dial has a radius of 16.0 mm. Dots are 1.4 mm squares, 0.6 mm deep; the windows are 2 mm wide in 6 mm spokes.

**Balance.** The Kepler wheel is lopsided, and only its balance keeps it level. It rests on nothing but the 1 mm boss round its peg, whose flats are 6.44 mm from the axis. Its bore would let it tilt about 6°, but its far teeth reach the plate at about 1.4° (my estimate). Made as a plain toothed ring with one spoke, the default wheel has its centre of mass 6.33 mm off its axis, only 0.12 mm inside the boss. At 13 of the 96 settings (every Pluto setting, Mercury at 74 mm, and Mercury at 72 mm with a 0.5 mm bore) it is outside the boss, and the wheel would lean onto its teeth. So the half of the wheel that touches the time wheel near perihelion, its light half, is solid from the rim in to the circle the dial needs (19.3 mm from the axis at the default; the dots end 1 mm inside it, so none is ever hidden). Where that is not enough, the light half also carries a counterweight on top, in whole 0.2 mm layers, up to 8 mm tall. Its height is the least that puts the centre of mass 1 mm plus the bore's play (0.54 mm) inside the boss's flats, with 0.05 mm to spare for the chamfers the calculation leaves out. Then the wheel stays on its boss even when its bore lets it slide towards its heavy side. The default needs a counterweight 1.4 mm tall, and its centre of mass is then 4.83 mm off the axis, 1.61 mm inside the boss. Mars and Earth need none and Mercury up to 2.4 mm. Pluto needs 5.4 mm or more, up to 6.4 mm for Pluto at 70 and 72 mm with a 0.7 mm bore, 0.4 mm above the collar. The time wheel, on a larger boss, has its centre of mass at least 3.9 mm inside it without help. All of this assumes the wheel is printed solid: see *Print it*.

**Every clearance from the fit table** (per side: snug 0.15–0.25 mm, sliding 0.3–0.4, rotating 0.5 or more):

| Where | Per side | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Wheel bore on its peg | 0.5 mm (0.6, 0.7 from the coupon) | rotating fit. The peg is a 24-sided prism with its corners on 4.0 mm, the bore one with its flats on 4.5 mm, so 0.5 mm is the least gap anywhere; where a corner of the bore faces a corner of the peg, the wheel's centre can move 0.54 mm |
| Tooth flanks | 0.48 mm | at least sliding, and the teeth still clear (by 0.02 mm, on the ideal outline) with both wheels pushed towards each other as far as their bores allow, 1.08 mm closer than nominal |
| Tooth tips to roots | 1.45 mm | rotating, and still 0.37 mm with the wheels 1.08 mm closer |
| Wheels above the dials | 1.0 mm, vertical | they ride on a 1 mm boss round each peg |
| Each wheel to the other peg's boss | 10.7 mm or more | |

Looser bores widen the flanks and tips with them (flank = 2 × bore × sin 25° + 0.05 mm, rounded up; tip = 2 × bore + 0.45 mm). Those formulas were made for the bore's least gap. With its full play the margins are 0.02 mm at the flanks and 0.37 mm at the tips at every bore, and the meshing check finds no binding with the wheels pushed 2 × play towards, away from or across each other (8 directions and 90 positions, at the smallest size of each orbit with the tightest and loosest bore), nor anywhere in the error budget's sampling of all 96 settings. The coupon has one peg of the same 24-sided section as the real ones and three rings made exactly like the wheels' bores, at 0.5, 0.6 and 0.7 mm, marked by one, two or three notches; the rings sit on the coupon's plate. Every bottom edge has a 0.4 mm chamfer over 0.5 mm of height (39° from vertical, so it is not an overhang) against elephant's foot.

**How wrong the printed pair can be.** `errorBudget` reads the machine as a person would: turn the knob until a time dot is centred in its window, then read where the planet window's centre line crosses the drawn orbit. It reports the largest difference from Kepler's angle in three cases, sampled three times per dot interval, dot times included:

- *teeth*: both wheels exactly on their peg centres, the planet wheel anywhere its teeth allow;
- *bores*: as well, each wheel's centre anywhere its bore allows (its peg centre and 8 points on a circle of 0.54 mm round it, the furthest the 24-sided bore lets it move on the 24-sided peg, or 0.64 and 0.74 mm for the looser bores; every combination of the two wheels: 81);
- *loose*: as *bores*, and each dot read anywhere it is still wholly inside its window (0.3 mm either side of centre).

For Mercury at 70 mm with a 0.5 mm bore the largest errors found are 1.34° (*teeth*), 8.04° (*bores*) and 10.99° (*loose*). Nearly all of it is the play of the wheels on their pegs: 0.54 mm seen from the orbit's perihelion, 11.4 mm from the Sun, is 2.7°. Even so, every reading sampled lands nearer its own orbit dot than either neighbour, at least 0.17° short of the midpoint between them; read loosely, one can land up to 1.81° past that midpoint. The bore matters most: at 0.6 mm the *bores* figure for Mercury is 9.52°, at 0.7 mm 11.11°, and neither keeps every reading nearest its own dot. Of the 96 settings, 33 do: Mercury's with a 0.5 mm bore from 70 mm up; Mars's and Earth's with a 0.5 mm bore from 60 mm up, with a 0.6 mm bore from 68 mm up, and with a 0.7 mm bore at 76 mm. Pluto's, whose orbit is drawn smallest (9.4 mm from the Sun at perihelion), never do. The page shows the budget for every setting (`budget.js`, made by `node build.mjs --budget`, about 25 minutes on three cores).

The coupon can only loosen the fit: it offers 0.5 mm and looser, because the fit table puts a rotating fit at 0.5 mm or more. A printer that turns a wheel freely on less would read better. Computed once for the default, and not offered on the page, a 0.4 mm bore (flanks and tips from the same formulas) gives 6.55° and a 0.3 mm bore 5.10°, about a third less than at 0.5 mm.

So for Mercury the printed pair tells Kepler from a planet moving evenly round a circle (23.7°) by a wide margin, but cannot tell Kepler from two elliptical gears (0.68°): only the drawing can. For Earth the play (5.49°) swamps the whole effect (1.9°), the unevenness that the unequal lengths of the seasons had shown long before Kepler: the printed Earth pair cannot show Kepler's law.

**Against the sky.** For Mercury, Mars and the Earth-Moon barycentre, *e* and the anomalistic period (360° over the difference of the rates of mean longitude and longitude of perihelion) come from Table 1 of JPL's *Approximate Positions of the Planets* (Standish & Williams, 1992; valid 1800–2050). Pluto has been removed from that table, so its *e* = 0.24896 and period of 90,588.8 days are measured from DE441: the mean of its two perihelion-to-aphelion pairs, and half the time between its perihelia of 1741.66 and 2237.70. `reduce-sky.mjs` fetched heliocentric DE441 positions from JPL Horizons on 24 October 2026; `sky.js` keeps every perihelion passage in 2000–2050 (1741–2237 for Pluto) and each body's position at every dot time over a few orbits from a DE441 perihelion. The test measures each position's angle from the starting perihelion, in the plane of the orbit, against the gears' angle:

| Body | Compared over | Largest difference at a dot |
|---|---|---|
| Mercury | 5 orbits from its perihelion of 2026 Feb 19 (111 dots) | 0.34′ |
| Mars (barycentre) | 3 orbits from 2026 Mar 26 (70 dots) | 2.4′ |
| Earth-Moon barycentre | 3 orbits from 2026 Jan 3 (79 dots) | 0.91′ |
| Pluto (system barycentre) | the orbit from 1989 Sep 5 (26 dots) | 9.75′ (0.16°) |

These are angles seen from the Sun, not from the Earth. For Mercury, Mars and the Earth, the Table 1 periods also match the mean spacing of DE441's perihelia in 2000–2050 to within 0.005 days. So the law is good to arcminutes and the printed object to degrees: nearly all the error between the plastic and the sky is the play between the wheels and their pegs.

**The page.** `kepler.js` is the whole model (no DOM, clock or randomness; a classic script and a `require`-able module). `mesh.js` builds the solids, all of it this entry's own: ear-clipping triangulation with each hole bridged to a vertex it can see (the method of Eberly's *Triangulation by Ear Clipping*), extrusion, and a binary STL writer. `app.js` draws the machine in SVG from the same outlines that become the STL, counterweight included, and gives each setting's balance; every number on the page comes from `kepler.js`, `sky.js` or `budget.js`. The machine turns by dragging either wheel (mouse or touch), by keys when focused (arrows a tenth of a dot, Page Up and Page Down a dot, Home perihelion, End aphelion) and by buttons. *Turn steadily* runs an orbit in 24 seconds; with `prefers-reduced-motion` it steps one dot every 1.5 seconds with no motion in between, and the dot buttons jump instead of gliding. Nothing changes colour over a large area from any input. Light and dark schemes follow the system.

**Byte-identical everywhere.** JavaScript engines disagree in the last bit of `Math.sin`, `cos`, `atan2` and `hypot` (Node 22 and Chrome 141 do), and the tooth cutting amplified those last bits into differences of up to 0.016 mm. So `kepler.js` has its own sine, cosine and arctangent made only of `+ − × ÷` and square roots, which IEEE 754 fixes to the bit (within 1e-14 of `Math`'s, tested), and faces are triangulated from the float32 coordinates the STL stores. The page's downloads at the default settings are byte-for-byte the committed STLs (checked in Chromium by `ui-check.mjs`), as Node's are (`parts.test.mjs`).

## Print it

Print the coupon first, then the plate and both wheels, flat, as they lie in the files.

- **Settings:** PLA, 0.4 mm nozzle, 0.2 mm layers, 3 perimeters, no supports, no brim and no skirt; 15–20% infill for the plate and the time wheel, and the Kepler wheel at 100% infill. Nothing overhangs beyond the 39° chamfers and nothing bridges.
- **Why no skirt:** a slicer's usual skirt runs off a 180 mm bed with the larger plates. With PrusaSlicer's default (one loop, 6 mm out), the default plate's G-code runs from −0.9 to 180.9 mm, and any plate wider than 167 mm is too wide: 9 of the 32 orbit-and-size combinations, the default among them. With the skirt off the widest plate, Mercury's at 74 mm, runs from 1.3 to 178.7 mm.
- **Why the Kepler wheel solid:** its balance depends on the plastic being where the solid is. Sliced at 100% infill, PrusaSlicer's G-code puts the default wheel's centre of mass 4.86 mm off its axis, 1.58 mm inside the boss, within 0.03 mm of the solid's figure (Pluto at 72 mm with a 0.5 or 0.7 mm bore, likewise). At 20% infill the filled half prints partly hollow, while the narrow rim on the heavy side stays solid. The centre of mass then lands 7.30 mm off the axis, outside the boss: worse than with no fill at all.
- **Dots (optional):** pause and change filament where the dot pockets begin, 2.4 mm up the plate, and the dot floors stay the first colour. Otherwise the dots read as shadows, or rub a soft pencil into them.
- **Assembly:** turn the Kepler wheel (the one with the collar) so its spoke points at the doubled orbit dot and drop it on the Sun's peg. Turn the time wheel so its spoke points at the doubled time dot and lower it onto the knob's peg, nudging until the teeth slip in. If both spokes are not on their doubled dots once the teeth are in, lift the time wheel and seat it again: one tooth out, the pair jams or jumps a tooth within a turn. In the meshing check, one tooth back it locks 25° into the orbit. One tooth on, the planet window reads about a sixteenth of an orbit (roughly 20–30°) behind the time window, until its play closes up at about 211° and it can only go on by jumping a tooth, after which it reads as a pair seated right does. Turn the knob clockwise.

`tools/printcheck.py` on the default set (PrusaSlicer 2.7.2 with 0.2 mm layers and its defaults: 3 perimeters, 20% infill, 0.35 mm first layer), with the Kepler wheel's filament and time from the same slicer at 100% infill:

| File | Size (mm) | Filament | Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| `coupon.stl` | 83.9 × 24.0 × 10.4 | 4.9 g | 38 min |
| `default.stl` (base plate) | 169.1 × 99.8 × 19.5 | 40.3 g | 3 h 46 min |
| `default-time-wheel.stl` | 78.1 × 75.3 × 16.0 | 14.0 g | 1 h 39 min |

No errors and no warnings: every STL is a closed, consistently wound solid on the bed that slices, with nothing needing support (adding supports adds 0.0% filament). `sweep.mjs` ran the same check on all three parts at the default and at the smallest and largest size of every orbit with 0.5 and 0.7 mm bores (51 STLs and the coupon): no errors, no warnings. The widest base plate is Mercury's at 74 mm, 177.8 × 104.6 mm, inside a 180 mm bed with the skirt off. printcheck checks the mesh, not the G-code, so it did not catch the skirt.

## Honest assessment

- **[Certain] It has not been printed.** Everything here is geometry and arithmetic checked by code. Most likely to need adjusting on a real printer: the bore fit (the coupon is for that); the teeth, if the printer over-extrudes or squashes the first layers, since the flanks have 0.48 mm a side nominally but only 0.02 mm with both wheels pushed together as far as their bores allow; the Kepler wheel's seating (next but one); and how freely the knob drives the second wheel, which depends on friction that nothing here models.
- **[Certain] The outlines are Kepler's law.** For all four orbits the tests check that the two pitch outlines roll on each other (equal arc lengths from perihelion to 0.002 mm on 4096-point polylines, contact on the line of centres, tangency to 1e-6 rad) and that integrating the ratio of their radii over a turn gives Kepler's equation back to 1e-9 rad. The equal-area test on the dots integrates the orbit directly, without Kepler's equation.
- **[Certain] Every part is a closed solid on the bed** at the default and at the smallest and largest size of each orbit with the tightest and loosest bore: every edge shared by exactly two faces in opposite directions, positive volume, lowest point at z = 0 on a flat face, inside 180 mm, every face's loops simple and nested and its triangles covering exactly its area. `tools/printcheck.py` agreed on the same files.
- **[Certain] The clearances come from the fit table** as above, at all 96 settings. In the error budget's sampling of all 96, with the wheels anywhere within the bore's full play, the teeth never bound: the Kepler wheel always had somewhere to go.
- **[Likely] The Kepler wheel sits level on its boss, if it is printed solid.** At every one of the 96 settings its centre of mass is at least 1 mm plus the bore's play inside the boss's flats (`budget.js`; by design the least margin over that is 0.05 mm), and the time wheel's is at least 3.9 mm inside. These are centres of mass of the solid, as if the plastic were uniform. The tests check them against the meshes at 17 settings. PrusaSlicer's G-code at 100% infill agrees to 0.03 mm for the three wheels I sliced (the default, and Pluto at 72 mm with both bores); at 20% infill it does not, hence the instruction. The first version had none of this. Its Kepler wheel's centre of mass was outside its boss at 13 settings and 0.12 mm inside at the default, so it would have leaned onto its teeth. A push can still tip it, since its far teeth are only about 1.4° from the plate (an estimate).
- **[Certain] The law against the sky** is as tabled: arcminutes over a few orbits for the planets, 0.16° at the dots of Pluto's current orbit. These are DE441 positions interpolated from Horizons output at steps of 6 hours (Mercury) to 30 days (Pluto), and only at the dot times.
- **[Likely] The reading-error budget.** The figures are the largest found by sampling, not bounds, and they assume a perfect print. The bore circle is the furthest the bore lets a wheel's centre move (0.54 mm for a 0.5 mm bore) in every direction, which overstates it a little in most directions. With 16 or 24 points round each bore instead of 8, the default's *bores* figure rises from 8.04° to 8.16°, and with 24 its dot margin falls from 0.17° to 0.14°. The other 95 settings were sampled with 8, so their figures may be about 1.5% low and their margins a few hundredths of a degree high. The worst readings need both wheels pushed to opposite sides of their pegs at once; while you turn the knob the teeth push the wheels apart, so I expect typical errors between the *teeth* and *bores* figures. That is a judgement, not a measurement.
- **[Likely] Some pair of teeth is always in contact,** since at each of 3600 positions tried (70 mm, 0.5 mm bore) the teeth hold the Kepler wheel within 1.43° (Mercury) to 1.52° (Pluto) of Kepler's angle both ways. More positions might find a little more. Between samples, and on a real print with rough flanks, it is unchecked.
- **[Speculative] The feel.** I think the hurrying round perihelion will be obvious to the eye while the hand turns evenly, and more persuasive than the chart; the fingers will hardly feel it (see *Why I made it*). No one has turned one.
- **What I changed in the inherited code.** It counted rack positions with `ceil` where it needed `round`, so 5 of the 32 orbit-and-size combinations offered here would have been cut with an extra position, depending on the last bit of a division. `Math.hypot`, `sin`, `cos` and `atan2` made the STLs depend on the JavaScript engine. Its bore options went down to 0.3 mm, below the rotating fit. For Earth the perihelion twin dot could sit under the rim, and for Pluto the smaller sizes hid the orbit under the hub. Its 45° chamfers sat exactly on the overhang limit and drew printcheck warnings. Its triangulator was a port of Mapbox's earcut; I replaced it with my own ear clipping, which I checked once on every face of every part at all 96 settings (closed, consistently wound, triangles covering exactly each face's area); the tests repeat the check at 17 settings.
- **Rough edges.** The plate has dots only; the dashed orbit on the page is not printed. Reading needs care: the *loose* figure shows what a careless reading costs. Nothing makes time even except your hand. The folder is about 545 KB, over the house's 450 KB guide and the checker's 500 KB warning, mostly the four committed STLs (the Kepler wheel's grew by a fifth with its filled half and counterweight).

## Where it could go next

- **Print it,** and measure the bore fit, the backlash and the reading error against the budget, whether the Kepler wheel sits level, and whether the hurry is felt.
- **A tighter coupon:** rings at 0.3 and 0.4 mm, offered on the page with their own budget and binding checks, for printers that turn a wheel freely below the fit table's 0.5 mm. The default's *bores* figure would fall from 8.04° to 5.10° at 0.3 mm.
- **A detent:** a printed spring click at each time dot, so the steps of time are counted with the fingers and the reading steadies.
- **A comet.** Gears cannot reach a comet's eccentricity (for Halley's, the comet's wheel would have to turn about 230 times as fast as the knob at perihelion), but a pair cut for *e* = 0.4 or 0.5 would hurry more violently than Pluto's; the cutting would need checking for undercut.
- **Two pairs on one plate,** Mercury and the Earth, with their time wheels geared together at the ratio of their mean motions: a line between the two planets would then show Mercury's elongation from the Sun as seen from home.

## Revision note

An independent reviewer recomputed the entry in its own code: the Kepler law, the outlines, the dial geometry, DE441 fetched afresh from Horizons, the meshes, the STLs and the reading-error budget. All of them held. It then found one real problem and seven smaller ones. I reproduced each with my own code before changing anything, and all of them held too:

- **The Kepler wheel would not have sat level.** As first made, its centre of mass was 6.33 mm off its axis at the default, only 0.12 mm inside the boss it rests on. At 13 of the 96 settings (every Pluto setting, Mercury at 74 mm, and Mercury at 72 mm with a 0.5 mm bore) it was outside the boss, so the wheel would lean until its teeth touched the plate. The wheel's light half is now solid out to the dial, and where that is not enough a counterweight on it (none to 6.4 mm tall) keeps the centre of mass 1 mm plus the bore's play inside the boss at every setting: 1.61 mm inside at the default. Slicing showed that this only works if the wheel is printed solid, so the instructions now say 100% infill for it. The Kepler wheel's STL, the print figures (about 73 g and 7 h 25 min in all) and the page's drawing changed with it.
- **The bore model understated the play.** The 24-sided bore on its 24-sided peg lets a wheel's centre move up to 0.54 mm, not 0.5 mm. The error budget now uses that at all 96 settings, so every *bores* and *loose* figure rose by about 5%. The headline 7.55° is now 8.04°: Mars went from 5.77° to 6.13°, Earth from 5.15° to 5.49° and Pluto from 8.59° to 9.14°. The default's dot margin fell from 0.54° to 0.17°, and 33 of the 96 settings, not 42, keep every reading nearest its own dot. With the wheels pushed together the flank and tip clearances are 0.02 and 0.37 mm, not 0.06 and 0.45 mm; the teeth still never bind.
- **Smaller fixes:**
  - The print settings now say no skirt: PrusaSlicer's default skirt runs off a 180 mm bed with 9 of the 32 plate sizes, the default's among them.
  - The backlash figures are now the largest found at 3600 positions (1.43° for Mercury, not 1.38°), and are called that.
  - One tooth out, the pair jams or jumps a tooth within a turn; it does not stay a sixteenth of an orbit out. One tooth on, the planet window reads roughly 20–30° behind the time window until the pair jumps a tooth. An earlier draft of this fix said 6–9°, which a second check showed is only the drift against a pair already a tooth out, not what the two windows show.
  - The Earth sentence now says that the unequal seasons had shown the 1.9° long before Kepler.
  - The page's paragraph about feeling the hurry is hedged, and says the hurry will be seen more than felt.
  - The README now says the coupon can only loosen the fit, and what a tighter one would gain. I did not add tighter rings: the fit table puts a rotating fit at 0.5 mm or more, and offering less would need its own budget and binding checks.

`page.test.mjs` is new and pins the README's and the page's words. `parts.test.mjs` gains the balance and dial-visibility tests, `budget.test.mjs` the balance at all 96 settings, and `kepler.test.mjs` the play, the denser backlash and the one-tooth-out tests. Each new test fails on the first version except the denser-backlash and one-tooth-out tests; the gears themselves did not change, so those two pin the corrected sentences, which `page.test.mjs` checks are there.
