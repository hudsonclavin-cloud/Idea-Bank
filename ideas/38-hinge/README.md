# Hinge
> State what you believe as ranges and chances. Hinge finds the belief your decision actually turns on, what it would be worth to find out before choosing, and what would have to be true for you to be wrong.

**Kind:** tool
**Run:** Open `ideas/38-hinge/index.html` in a browser. Plain classic scripts, so double-clicking the file works; no server, build, network or install. Tests, from the repo root: `node --test ideas/38-hinge/hinge.test.mjs` (pure Node, about 15 s) and `node ideas/38-hinge/ui-check.mjs` (drives the page in headless Chromium through the globally installed Playwright that `tools/smoke.mjs` uses).
**Made by:** Gudgeon

## What it is

A thinking tool for one kind of problem: *I have to choose, and the choice depends on several things I am unsure about.*

You write the things you are unsure about as **assumptions**: a *range* you would bet 90% on (`exit_value: 100M to 1B`), a *chance* (`survives: 60%`), a *fixed* fact (`years: 4`), or a *formula* that derives one quantity from others (`payment = loan * r / (1 - (1 + r)^-360)`). Then you write each **option** as a formula over those names, such as `Stay = stay_comp * years` and `Startup = startup_comp * years + if(survives and exit_if_alive, equity * exit_value, 0)`. One option is an estimate; two to four are a decision.

Hinge then shows you four things you cannot work out in your head once there are more than two uncertain inputs:

1. **The outcomes.** The distribution of each option, which one is best on average, and how often it actually comes out ahead. These two can disagree, and when they do, that is the most important thing on the page.
2. **Where it hinges.** For every uncertain assumption: what it would be **worth to learn it exactly** before choosing (its value of information, in your own units), the **values at which the best option switches** and which option takes over there, the **chance** your own range puts on those values, and, in small print, its share of the variance of the best option's lead. Assumptions that cannot change the answer on their own say so.
3. **The hinge curve.** Tap an assumption and see the expected outcome of each option as a function of that one assumption, with the switch point marked and your own belief about the assumption drawn underneath it. Where the lines cross is what would have to be true.
4. **A plain-language verdict** at the top, written from the numbers: *"On expected value, Startup beats Stay by $65k. But it actually comes out ahead in only 24% of runs: its average is carried by a minority of big wins… The one thing most worth finding out is exit_if_alive, worth $66.3k on its own. The most likely single thing to change the answer: exit_if_alive if it does not happen (60%), and then Stay wins. stay_comp, startup_comp, gap_months cannot change it alone, however they turn out."* When the best option wins rarely, the verdict says why: its own big wins, the runner-up's rare big losses (an insurance-shaped choice), or, with three or more options, losses split among several rivals.

For a single estimate the same machinery shows where the uncertainty comes from (each input's share of the variance, and how far the expected outcome moves between the input's 5th and 95th percentile) and the chance of landing under a target.

Everything stays in the browser: cases live in `localStorage` (the page works without it), and can be exported and imported as JSON. Three worked examples are built in, and the first one loads on first visit.

### The three decisions I tried it on

- **Take the startup offer, or stay?** (loads first). Expected value favours the startup by $67k over four years (exactly $67.1k; the page shows $65k at its default seed, and re-rolling moves it by about ±5k), but the startup comes out ahead in only about 24% of runs: the average is carried by a 24% chance of a large equity payout, and most of the time you end up about $100k behind. The model splits that 24% into two rows: `survives` (60%) and `exit_if_alive` (40%, an exit *if* it is still alive). The most valuable single row is `exit_if_alive`, worth $66k on its own: that is the value of learning only whether it would exit if it survived. Learning whether it will survive *and* exit would be worth about $86k, nearly all of the $86.5k value of perfect information, and it is the one thing you cannot learn. Uncertainty about the salary cannot flip the decision anywhere in its range, so forecasting raises is wasted effort. The equity can, if it ends up below about 0.11% (a 10% chance). What the table does *not* say is what to negotiate: it ranks what is worth *learning*, not what is worth *changing*. A what-if on the formulas answers that: another $10k a year of salary adds about $39k over four years, for certain; another 0.05 points of equity adds about $48k on average, but pays only in the 24% of worlds with an exit. Which ask is better depends on what the employer will give and how much you mind the risk.
- **Buy the place, or keep renting?** Renting is cheaper by about $33k over five years on expectation, and buying wins in 26% of runs. Of the five uncertain inputs, exactly one can flip the answer on its own: house-price appreciation, which would have to run above about 4% a year (a 25% chance). Maintenance, selling costs, rent growth and what the down payment would earn elsewhere all carry real uncertainty, and none of it matters to the choice, *given that I drew rent growth and appreciation independently*. If they move together they become a pair: tie them with a formula row (a correlation of 0.6, marginals unchanged; there is a test) and rent growth becomes a hinge too, above about 4.2% a year (a 15% chance). I had expected the investment return to be the hinge. It carries 7% of the variance of the margin (the small figure under its bar) and cannot flip the decision.
- **How long will the rewrite take?** I had promised twelve weeks. Median 12.7 weeks, mean 13.9, and a 45% chance of making it (the exact figures; the page's 5,000 samples show 12.8 weeks and 44%). The hours per feature, which nobody had discussed, is 58% of the variance; the number of features, which we had argued about for an hour, is 12%; the "weeks lost to surprises" line is 2%.

## Why I made it

I notice that when people (and I) reason about a decision, we argue about the number that is easiest to argue about, not the one the conclusion depends on. A salary is easy to haggle; the probability of an exit is not, so the conversation drifts to the salary even when what the salary will be cannot change the decision. The question I find most useful in any decision is *what would have to be true for me to be wrong?*, and the honest answer depends on the whole joint distribution of everything else I believe, which is exactly what a head cannot hold once there are more than two uncertain inputs. A computer can hold it, and the answer has a precise form: for each assumption, the set of values at which the expected best choice switches, and how likely you yourself think those values are.

The second thing I wanted was a number for *should I keep researching, or decide?* The value of perfect information is that number: the most that any investigation into an assumption could possibly be worth. When it says $0 for the thing you have been reading about all week, that is a result.

The third is the part that can only be felt. In the startup example, expected value says go and probability says you will most likely regret it, and both are true at once. A table of numbers does not make you feel that. Seeing the fat orange tail on one strip and the narrow blue block on the other, and then the line that says "ahead in 24% of runs", does. The proof is in the tests; the feeling is in the strips.

## How it works

**Assumptions become distributions.** A *range* `lo to hi` is a 90% interval: lognormal with `ln lo` and `ln hi` at the 5th and 95th percentiles when `lo > 0` (you are unsure to within a *factor*), normal otherwise; `uniform` makes `lo` and `hi` hard limits; the shape can be overridden. The 5th/95th percentiles sit at ±1.6449 standard deviations. A *chance* `p` is 1 with probability `p` and 0 otherwise. Assumptions are drawn **independently**. Formula rows are evaluated in dependency order (any order on the page is fine; cycles are reported).

**Simulation.** `n` joint samples (5,000 by default, 20,000 with the "four times" box) by Latin hypercube sampling: each uncertain row gets its own random permutation of the `n` equal-probability strata, so every slice of every input is hit exactly once, which steadies the means. The generator is seeded (mulberry32); the seed is shown and "Re-roll" changes it. Every option is evaluated on every sample.

**Decision quantities.** Options are compared as *values* (negated when smaller is better). The **best** option is the one with the highest mean value; **best in** is the fraction of samples in which that option attains the maximum. The **value of perfect information** is `E[max_j V_j] − max_j E[V_j]`: what you would gain on average by choosing after learning every assumption.

**Per-assumption analysis** (the "Where it hinges" table), for each range or chance row `i`, with common random numbers (every other row keeps the same `n` draws):

- The row is pinned to a value `x` and the downstream formula rows and options are re-evaluated on all `n` samples, giving the *conditional means* `m_j(x) = E[V_j | x_i = x]`. These are the hinge curves; they are drawn at 36 points (the medians of 32 equal-probability slices plus the 0.1%, 5%, 95% and 99.9% quantiles).
- **The answer changes if…** is the set of values `x` where `argmax_j m_j(x)` is not the overall best. The best option at each of the 36 grid points is found (the overall best keeps ties), and wherever it differs between neighbouring points the switch is bisected in quantile space (20 steps). The result is a list of stretches, each with the option that is best on it; touching stretches merge into intervals, reported as *below / above / between / outside* values, or spelled out piece by piece (*below 1 or between 3 and 6*). When different options take over on different stretches, as can happen with three or four options, each stretch is shown with its own winner and chance (*below −0.3 → C (34%); above 0.3 → A (42%)*). The **chance** is the total length of the intervals in quantile space, i.e. the probability your own distribution puts on them. For a chance row the two states are checked directly. The scan can miss a switch region narrower than one slice (about 3% of the distribution) that falls between two grid points, which only happens with non-monotone models, and it does not look beyond the 0.1% and 99.9% quantiles.
- The row's distribution is then cut into **cells**: `G = 32` equal-probability slices, with the two outer slices cut finer (at 1/4, 1/16, 1/64 and 1/256 of a slice from each end, because with a long-tailed input most of what an outer slice averages away is in its last few per cent), and every cell that contains a switch point cut at the switch point. For a chance row the cells are its two states. Every sample's own uniform draw for that row is mapped into cell `c`, so the cell mean `m_j(c) = E[V_j | x_i ∈ c]` is exact up to Monte Carlo error. With `w_c` the probability of cell `c`:
  - **Worth knowing** (partial value of information) `= Σ_c w_c · max_j m_j(c) − max_j Σ_c w_c · m_j(c)`, the value of learning *which cell* the assumption falls in before choosing. Coarser information is worth less, so in expectation this is at most the exact partial EVPI; but because the best option does not change inside a cell, it *is* the exact partial EVPI (up to sampling noise) whenever the scan has found the switch points. The tests check it against closed forms for switch points from the 2% to the 98% quantile and in the far tail (A = x against B = 2.4 for a standard normal x, where the answer changes only in the top 0.8%). Plain 32 slices, which the first version used, kept 96% or more of it for a switch inside the 5–95% band (98% inside 10–90%), for a normal or a factor-of-ten lognormal input, but dropped to zero for a switch in the outer 1%. At 5,000 samples the figure itself is noisy: the startup example's equity row ($1.6k exact) reads between $1.1k and $2.1k over 40 seeds, about ±15%.
  - **Share of uncertainty** `= Σ_c w_c (E[D | c] − E[D])² / Var(D)`, a first-order Sobol index of the cut-up input, where `D` is the margin of the best option over the best of the rest in a decision, or the outcome itself in an estimate. Cutting an input into cells averages a little of its variance away: for an input that enters linearly, the cells keep 99.7% of a normal input's variance and 98.8% of a lognormal one with σ = 0.7 (a factor-of-10 range); plain 32 slices kept 99.1% and 92.6%. The shares of an additive model therefore sum to a little under one, and the "the rest" row under an estimate's table is interaction *plus* that small slicing loss; it is left out when there is only one uncertain input, which cannot interact with anything. In a decision the share is the small figure under each worth-knowing bar.
- In the table the rows are sorted by worth knowing; values below 0.5% of the total EVPI are shown as ≈0.
- **Swing** (estimates) is `m(q95) − m(q05)`: how far the expected outcome moves between the input's 5th and 95th percentile.

**Formulas** use a small expression language: `+ − * / ^`, parentheses, comparisons, `and / or / not`, `if(test, a, b)`, `min max abs sqrt ln log10 exp round floor ceil clamp pow`, and number suffixes `k M B %`. The parser produces an AST; `emitJS` turns the AST into plain arithmetic source (only numbers, whitelisted operators and functions, and indexed reads) which is compiled with `Function` for speed, with a closure interpreter as the fallback; the tests check that both agree. Names can be renamed in the editor and every formula that uses them follows.

**Files.** `js/hinge.js` is the whole engine with no DOM (distributions, parser, compiler, simulation, analysis, narration, import/export normalisation); the page loads it as a script and `hinge.test.mjs` imports it under Node. `js/examples.js` holds the three cases. `js/app.js` is the editor, storage and SVG rendering. Charts are hand-drawn SVG: distribution strips with a faint histogram, the 5–95% line, the 25–75% box, median tick and mean dot; and the hinge curve with the switch band, a crosshair readout, and a table twin behind a disclosure.

## Honest assessment

What works:

- [Certain] `hinge.test.mjs` passes 27 tests. Against closed forms: the normal CDF/quantile pair (known values, an independent approximation, round trips); 90% ranges land on the 5th/95th percentiles and densities integrate to one; the LHS columns are exact permutations; a linear-normal decision reproduces P(best), total EVPI, the exact partial EVPI, the flip threshold (x = 6, to 1e-3) and its probability; a two-input linear model splits the exact partial EVPIs and the variance shares the way the closed form says; partial EVPIs for switch points from the 2% to the 98% quantile of a lognormal input, and in the far tail of a normal one, match their closed forms; a chance variable gives the exact partial EVPI (18 on the nose) and flip state; quadratic models produce correct two-crossing flip sets (*between* and *outside*); a lognormal product reproduces its median, mean, 5th/95th percentiles, the probability under a target, the Sobol indices (within 6%, and exactly what its cells keep), and the swing; a single uncertain input carries all of the variance. Against an independent Monte Carlo (xoshiro128**, Box–Muller, no stratification, 100,000–200,000 draws): the startup example's P(best) and total EVPI, the house example's appreciation threshold (E[Buy | a*] ≈ E[Rent] within 1%), and the rewrite example's median, mean and P(≤ 12 weeks). The startup example's expected values, the partial EVPIs of its chance rows and of `exit_value`, the $86k value of learning "survives and exits" together, and the salary and equity what-ifs are recomputed in closed form; the house example's formulas are checked at the medians against a separate implementation of the mortgage arithmetic. Structural checks: "smaller is better" is the exact mirror of "bigger is better"; the house case with every formula row inlined gives identical numbers (this test caught a real bug during development: after pinning a row, its downstream formula rows were left corrupted for the next row's analysis); the house case with appreciation tied to rent growth makes rent growth a hinge. Words: the verdict for an insurance-shaped choice, for three options with no skew, and for decisions where no single row flips the answer; per-stretch winners with three options; a flip set in several pieces spelled out.
- [Certain] `ui-check.mjs` passes 51 checks in headless Chromium: building a case from a blank through the real inputs, rename-with-refactor, inline formula errors with stale results, edits changing the outcome, tapping rows, hover and touch readouts, the two-step row delete, reload with persistence, export, duplicate, confirmed delete (dismissed and accepted), switching cases, loading examples, importing the exported file in a fresh browser, refusing junk, running with `localStorage` disabled, a phone viewport in dark mode with reduced motion, a dozen blind button clicks that cannot delete anything, the share figure under each bar in a decision, per-stretch winners with three options, "the rest" row (and its absence with one input), and the below-zero warning on a range typed from 0. `tools/smoke.mjs` passes at desktop and phone width with no console errors or overflow.
- [Certain] Recomputing with other seeds moves the headline numbers by about the amount you would expect at n = 5,000. Over 40 seeds, one standard deviation is: the startup margin ±5.3k on 67k (58k to 80k), "ahead in" ±0.3 points, the exit-value threshold ±4M on 251M (242M to 261M), the equity row's chance ±1.4 points (7.5% to 13%, against an exact 10.5%), and its worth-knowing figure ±15% ($1.1k to $2.1k, against an exact $1.6k).

Where the method misleads, and what is rough:

- [Certain] **Independence.** Every assumption is drawn independently. If two of yours move together (rent growth and house appreciation, say), the tool understates or overstates the risk, and the per-row analysis attributes the effect to whichever one you ask about: in the house example, rent growth "cannot flip it alone" only because it is drawn independently of appreciation. The workaround is to derive one from the other with a formula row; a proper correlation control is the first thing I would add.
- [Certain] **Expected value is the criterion.** "Best" means best on average, which is the right rule for a decision you will face many times and the wrong one for a decision you cannot afford to lose. The startup example is the case in point: Hinge says the startup is best and, in the same breath, that you will probably be worse off. It shows you both so you can apply your own appetite for risk; it does not model it.
- [Certain] **One-at-a-time.** "Cannot flip it alone" means exactly that. Combinations can flip it, and the difference between the total value of information and the largest partial is the measure of how much lives in combinations (in the house example $9.0k versus $8.0k; in the startup example $86k versus $66k, and there the gap is mostly one question split over two rows: whether it survives *and* exits). The flip scan can also miss a switch region narrower than one slice (about 3% of the distribution) that sits between two grid points, which only happens with non-monotone models, and it does not search beyond the 0.1% and 99.9% quantiles. When that leaves a one-input decision with no switch at all, the verdict says the stretch was too narrow to locate rather than blaming combinations.
- [Certain] **Perfect information.** The "worth knowing" figures are for learning an assumption *exactly*. Any real investigation gives imperfect information and is worth less, so treat the figure as a ceiling. The estimator targets the exact partial EVPI once the switch points are found, but at 5,000 samples a mid-sized figure is good to only about ±15% (see above).
- [Certain] **Your ranges, your answer.** A positive range is read as lognormal, which puts a heavy right tail on it: `exit_value: 100M to 1B` has a 1-in-100 tail at $1.6B and a mean 28% above its median. That is the right shape for "unsure to within a factor" and the wrong one for "somewhere around here", and the auto choice is announced under each row with its median and tail so you can switch to normal when it is wrong. People's 90% intervals are also usually too narrow, so the real hinge is often outside the range you typed.
- [Certain] Monte Carlo wobble: at n = 5,000 percentages are good to a point or so and a mid-sized worth-knowing figure to about a sixth. A row that cannot flip the answer comes out at zero or a few dollars (stay_comp can in fact flip it above $184k, a 0.02% chance beyond the scanned range, which is worth $1.09 exactly), and the page prints ≈0 for anything below 0.5% of the total. Four times the samples halves the wobble.
- [Likely] Recomputing takes 0.2–0.6 s for the built-in cases on a laptop and two to three times that on a phone, so typing in a formula is debounced rather than instant.
- [Certain] The example numbers are my own guesses chosen to make the method visible, not advice about jobs, houses or schedules. Four options at most; no categorical assumptions with more than two states (use two chances or a formula).
- [Speculative] I have only looked at the page in headless Chromium. The layout uses nothing exotic, but Safari and Firefox are unverified.

## Where it could go next

- **Correlation.** A "moves with" link between two rows (a Gaussian copula with a slider), so rent growth and appreciation can be tied without writing a formula.
- **Risk.** One knob for loss aversion turning each option into a certainty equivalent, so the startup example can say what the equity is worth *to you*.
- **Imperfect information.** "A test that is right 80% of the time" or "a reference class of 30 similar startups": the value of sample information, so "should I spend a week finding out?" gets a number.
- **Two people, one model.** Each gives their own ranges; the tool finds the assumption that accounts for the disagreement, the crux.
- **Combinations.** Pairwise values of information and second-order shares, to show which *pairs* flip the answer when no single row can.
- **Sharing by URL.** A case packed into the hash, so a hinge can be sent as a link.

## Revision note

An independent reviewer recomputed every headline number with separate code and found them right; the problems were in how the results were put into words, and in some method claims. Everything it raised held up when I reproduced it, and is now fixed:

- **The verdict blamed "big wins" for every rarely-winning favourite.** For an insurance-shaped choice (a constant premium against a rare large loss) the safe option has no wins at all; its lead comes from the other option's rare disasters. The verdict now reads the head-to-head numbers and says which it is, or, with three or more options, that the losses are split among several rivals. The startup verdict is unchanged, because there the lead really is the startup's own big wins.
- **With three or four options only one winner was named.** A two-sided flip set reported the winner of its first stretch for both. Each stretch now carries its own winner and chance, in the table and the verdict.
- **The worth-knowing figure could vanish for a switch in the tail.** Plain 32 slices lost value inside whichever slice held the switch point, down to ≈0 for a switch in the outer 1%. The engine now cuts the outer slices finer and cuts at every switch point, so the figure targets the exact partial EVPI. The README's old "97% and 88%, checked in the tests" figures were variance-retention figures, not EVPI. For an input that enters linearly, variance shares now keep 99.7% (normal) and 98.8% (factor-of-ten lognormal) of the exact index, up from 99.1% and 92.6%. The rewrite example's hours-per-feature share moved from 57% to 58% (exact: 58.1%), and the house example's market return from 7.0% to 7.4% (exact: 7.5%).
- **"Interactions" was partly slicing loss.** A one-input case showed "interactions 6.1%". That row is now "the rest", described as interactions plus a little slicing loss, and it is left out for a single input, which now shows about 100%.
- **The startup lesson misread its own model.** `exit` meant "an exit if it survives". Its $66k is the value of learning only that; learning whether it will survive *and* exit is worth $86k. The row is now named `exit_if_alive`. The negotiation lesson (push on equity, not salary) is gone: value of information ranks what is worth learning, not what is worth asking for. A what-if puts +$10k/yr of salary at +$39k for certain, against +$48k on average for +0.05 points of equity.
- **Smaller fixes.**
  - The seed wobble is larger than first stated: the margin moves by ±5k, not ±3k, and the exit-value threshold by ±4M.
  - The decision table now shows the variance share the README quotes.
  - When no single row flips the answer, the verdict no longer says "only combinations can" where nothing can, or where one input's switch region was too narrow to find.
  - "Lower bound" now says what is true of the displayed number: at 5,000 samples it is good to about ±15%.
  - The house example now says its "rent growth doesn't matter" depends on independence. A test shows that, tied to appreciation, rent growth flips the choice above 4.2%.
  - A flip set in several pieces is spelled out.
  - A normal range typed from 0 warns that it goes below zero 5% of the time.
  - Phone-width headers in the estimate table now wrap instead of overflowing.
