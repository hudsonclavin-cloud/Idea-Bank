# Whose Turn
> Tap who's here and it names who drives, hosts or buys this time, and says why. Follow it and, however attendance varies, nobody taking turns in a circle of n people can ever drift more than (n − 1)/2 turns from their fair share; for four people the worst that can actually happen is 1⅙ turns.

**Kind:** tool
**Run:** open `ideas/74-whose-turn/index.html` in a browser (plain classic scripts, so `file://` works; no build, no network). It opens on a made-up school run; *Start your own* makes a real one. Tests, from the repo root: `node --test ideas/74-whose-turn/turns.test.mjs` (Node built-ins only, about 10 s) and `node ideas/74-whose-turn/ui-check.mjs` (drives the page in headless Chromium with the globally installed Playwright that `tools/smoke.mjs` uses).
**Made by:** Fairlead

## What it is

A rota for the things a group takes turns at when not everyone is there every time: the school run, the office coffee, who hosts book club, who buys the round, who takes the bins out. You keep one *circle* per thing. Each time:

1. **Tap who's in.** The page remembers last time's people, so on a usual day this is no taps at all.
2. **Read the pick.** "*Ben's turn.* Ben owes ½ of a turn, more than anyone else among the 4 of you here. If Ben can't: Cleo, then Dev, then Ana."
3. **Tap "Ben did it".** If someone else did it, *Someone else did it* lists the others, and beside each one says what that would do to the promise below ("fence stays the same", or "fence widens to ±2").

Below that, **Where everyone stands** shows each person as a dot on a line from *is owed* to *owes*, inside a dashed fence: the furthest anyone can get from even if the pick is followed. *Over time* draws everyone's balance after each of the last 80 entries against today's fence (after a missed pick, earlier balances can lie outside it); *Show the arithmetic* gives the exact fractions. There is a history with *Remove* and *Undo* (Undo puts back every removal its slip covers), a place to rename people or mark them as having left, and **Share or back up**: a ledger code to paste into a group chat, which the other phone merges in, plus a backup file.

A circle counts one of two ways, chosen when you start it:

- **Turns**: one person does it for everyone there (drive, host, cook). If four of you are in the car, each person's fair share of that run is ¼ and the driver did 1.
- **Rounds**: one person gets one each for everyone there (coffee, drinks). Each person present had 1 portion; the buyer bought one for each of the *k* people there. Buying for eight is a bigger job than buying for two, and a rounds circle counts it that way.

Everything is kept in the browser on that device. Nothing is sent anywhere, and the page works offline, straight from the file.

## Why I made it

The brief was a wildcard with one lens: a tool someone would use every week, for a real, recurring problem people solve badly. I sketched candidates and asked of each what could be proven and whether a person would come back to it:

- **Whose turn** (this one). Groups keep score of turns in their heads, and it goes wrong exactly when attendance varies: a plain rota tends to make whoever comes least do more than their share, and counting who has done it fewest times is worse. There is a real piece of mathematics underneath (the carpool problem of Fagin and Williams, 1983) and an honest social question on top: what does keeping score do to a friendship?
- **A chord-chart transposer** that spells chords correctly in the new key (B♭, not A♯) and finds the capo position with the easiest shapes. Weekly for church and band musicians, and spelling is provable, but good transposers exist, and the proven part is shallow.
- **A unit-price comparer** for the supermarket aisle that understands "3 for £5", "£1.20/100 g" and "1.2 lb for $3.49". Useful every week, but the proof is unit conversion, and shopping apps already do most of it.
- **A settle-up calculator** that pays off a group's debts in the fewest transfers (exactly, by search over subsets). The optimisation is a nice proof, but Splitwise and its kind already handle money between friends well enough.

Whose Turn won because the usual ways of keeping track (a note on the fridge, a spreadsheet, memory) ignore who was there, and I found no tool that does the sum (one search turned up advice about rotas and tallies, not a tool); because the proven part turned out deeper than I expected; and because the felt part is the whole point. I expected to cite a bound from the literature. I could not open the papers, so I tried to prove one myself, and found a short identity that does it for every group size. The search for the true worst case then turned up something I did not expect: for two, three and four people, no other deterministic rule can promise a smaller worst case than this one.

## How it works

**The ledger.** Every person has a balance, what they owe the group. For a turns circle it is the Fagin–Williams fair share (the sum of 1/*k* over every time they were there, with *k* people there) minus the turns they did. For a rounds circle it is the portions they had minus the portions they bought. The person who does it is always counted as there. The balances always add up to exactly zero. `turns.js` keeps them as exact fractions with `BigInt`, so no rounding ever builds up; the page shows halves, thirds, quarters, fifths, sixths and eighths as fractions (1⅙), anything else to one decimal place ("about 0.6"), and a non-zero amount too small for that as "less than 0.1".

**The rule.** Among the people there, whoever owes the most does it. Exact ties go to whoever did it least recently (never counts as longest ago), then to the earlier name in the list. This is the greedy rule that, as later papers describe it, Fagin and Williams proposed; the tie-break is mine. The guarantee below holds however ties are broken.

**The promise, and why it holds.** Let *n* be the number of people who have ever been in the circle, α = ½ for turns and α = 1 for rounds, and for any group *J* of people write *g*(*J*) = |*J*|·(*n* − |*J*|) and *u*(*J*) for their balances added up. Claim: if every group has *u*(*J*) ≤ α *g*(*J*), that is still true after a turn given by the rule.

- A group containing the person *c* who did it does not go up: it gains at most 1/*k* for each member there and loses 1 (rounds: gains at most 1 each, loses *k*).
- A group *J* without *c*, with *a* ≥ 1 of its members there (call them *B*), goes up by *a*/*k* (rounds: by *a*). With *f*(*X*) = α *g*(*X*) − *u*(*X*), *Y* = *J* plus *c*, and *Z* = *J* minus *B*, this identity holds for any balances whatever:

  (*a* + 1) *f*(*J*) = *a f*(*Y*) + *f*(*Z*) + Σ<sub>*b* in *B*</sub> (*u*<sub>*c*</sub> − *u*<sub>*b*</sub>) + α *a*(*a* + 1).

  (Expand it: *g* is the cut function of the complete graph on *n* people, and the *g* terms leave α *a*(*a* + 1) exactly.) Before the turn *f*(*Y*) ≥ 0 and *f*(*Z*) ≥ 0, and *u*<sub>*c*</sub> ≥ *u*<sub>*b*</sub> because *c* owed the most of everyone there. So *f*(*J*) ≥ α *a*, which is at least *a*/*k* for turns (α = ½, *k* ≥ 2) and exactly *a* for rounds. The group stays within bounds.

At the start everyone is 0, so the condition holds forever while the rule is followed. A group of one person gives *u* ≤ α(*n* − 1); the group of everyone else gives *u* ≥ −α(*n* − 1). So **nobody ever owes, or is owed, more than (*n* − 1)/2 turns, or *n* − 1 portions.** For rounds that cannot be improved by any rule: the first round bought for all *n* puts the buyer *n* − 1 portions ahead. Someone joining later starts at 0 and the condition still holds with the larger *n*, so *n* counts everyone who has ever been in the circle, including people marked as having left.

**When the pick wasn't followed.** Then some group may be over its bound. Let *D* be the largest overshoot, the most any *u*(*J*) exceeds α *g*(*J*) (0 if none). The same identity, with every *f* ≥ −*D*, shows that a turn given by the rule never makes *D* bigger. So the page can always promise ±(α(*n* − 1) + *D*) from now on, and says so; before you log someone other than the pick it shows what the fence would become. The largest *u*(*J*) over groups of size *j* is the sum of the *j* largest balances, so *D* takes one sort.

**The true worst case for small circles.** The promise is an upper bound. To find what the rule actually reaches, the tests visit every state it can reach from an even start, over every pattern of attendance and every tie choice. (Balances are multiples of 1/lcm(1..*n*), and since relabelling people changes nothing, states are kept sorted: 2, 8, 195 and 51,823 states for 2 to 5 people.) The worst balances reached, in turns:

| People | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Worst the rule reaches | ½ | ⅚ | 1⅙ | 1⅗ | at least 119/60 ≈ 1.98 (the largest found), at most 2½ |
| Promised, (*n* − 1)/2 | ½ | 1 | 1½ | 2 | 2½ |
| Best any deterministic rule can promise | ½ | ⅚ | 1⅙ | not checked | not checked |

For six people the exhaustive search does not finish. A one-off run stopped when its hash table filled, after 90 million states, so the 49/30 it had reached by then was only the most it had happened to see, not a meaningful lower bound. A beam search (at each step, keep the few thousand states furthest from even) then found an attendance pattern of 64 entries, every one going to the pick, that leaves someone 119/60 ≈ 1.98 turns from even; the tests replay it through the page's own engine. That is the largest found so far, not the true worst case, which could be anywhere from there up to 2½. For rounds circles of 2 to 8 people the same search reaches exactly *n* − 1, as the proof and the first round say it must. The last row comes from solving the game exhaustively: for a bound *B*, discard every state from which some attendance forces every possible choice of doer outside the remaining states; if the even start survives, some rule stays within *B* forever. For 2, 3 and 4 people the even start survives at the rule's own worst case and not one step (1/lcm) below it.

**Why not a plain rota?** The tests run three rules on the same attendance. In a made-up school year of 190 runs for four parents, one of whom came on only 102 of them, a plain rota (skip whoever is away) left someone 5.1 turns out at its worst, giving it to whoever had done it fewest times had that parent driving 48 times against a fair share of about 29.6, and this rule never let anyone past ⅚. Both other rules drift without limit on some patterns: if two people always come and a third comes every third time, both hand the third person every third turn, two-thirds of a turn more than their share each cycle (−40 after 60 cycles, −80 after 120).

**Two phones.** A circle travels as a code, `WHOSETURN1:` and then base64url of a compact JSON: members as a list, each entry packed as `id~seconds~doer~present` (base 36, presence as a bitmask over the member list). A school year for four people (190 entries) is about 6,000 characters. Pasting a code merges: entries are a union by id, a removal on either copy stays removed, names and departures are last-writer-wins with a tie-break on the value, so merging gives the same result in either order and merging twice changes nothing. Balances depend only on which entries exist, not their order, so two phones that have merged each other's codes show the same balances. The tie-break and the history do use entry times, which come from each phone's clock. One thing merging cannot fix: if both phones log before swapping codes, each phone's pick was made without the other's entries, so after the merge some of those entries can read as *not the pick* and the fence can widen, although everyone followed their own phone. The page names this as a possible cause in its promise and its count of entries that went to the pick. Keeping the log on one phone, or swapping codes before each time, avoids it. If the page is served over http(s), *Copy link* puts the code in the URL's `#`, and opening the link offers to merge it.

**Keeping it.** Circles are stored in `localStorage`. Every read and write is wrapped, and if storage is blocked the page still works for the session and says that nothing will be kept. Browsers can clear what a page stores: Safari's tracking prevention deletes a site's script-writable storage after seven days of Safari use without the user interacting with that site (WebKit's March 2020 change, which I know from coverage of the WebKit post rather than the post itself; I don't know how it treats a page opened from a file). So the page asks you to keep a copy early: after a circle's second entry if it has no copy yet, and after that whenever there are entries newer than the last copy and that copy is more than a week old, until it is copied or put off for a week. Copying its code or link counts as a copy, and so does a backup that holds it; a backup downloaded before a circle was made does not count for that circle. *Download a backup* saves every circle to a file that *Restore* merges back; with no circles at all, *Start a circle* also offers to paste a code or restore a backup. The reminder can only show when the page is opened, so it does not protect against the seven-day cap by itself: a circle left unopened through a week or more of Safari use can lose everything since its last copy. Pasting the code into the group chat after each time is the safe habit.

**The page.** `turns.js` is pure: no DOM, no clock, no randomness except a seeded generator for examples. It loads as a classic script (`window.WhoseTurn`) and with `require`. `app.js` draws everything from it. After each entry the buttons rest for about a second, and repeated key events from a held Enter or Space are ignored, so a held key or a double tap logs one entry. *Remove* rests the same way, because after one removal the next entry's button moves under the finger. The pick's name slides in over 260 ms, only when the name changes, at most once every 400 ms, and not at all with `prefers-reduced-motion`; the verdict is redrawn only when its words change. Nothing flashes or blinks. Apart from the log button coming back after its rest and the confirmation slip going away, nothing changes without a tap or key press, and held keys don't repeat. People are real buttons with `aria-pressed`, the pick is announced through a live region, and light and dark schemes follow the system. Keyboard focus stays put: a person's button, a rename field or *Has left* is updated in place rather than rebuilt, the resting log button uses `aria-disabled` so it keeps focus, and after *Remove* or *Someone else did it* focus moves to the next entry's *Remove* or back to the button that opened the list. Names are always set as text, never as HTML, and a pasted code is validated (sizes, ids, every entry naming known people) before anything is merged.

## Tests

`turns.test.mjs` (30 tests, Node built-ins only, about 10 s):

- **Arithmetic and the ledger.** Fractions against an integer computation over lcm(1..30); a hand-worked three-person example for turns and for rounds; 200 random ledgers whose balances sum to exactly zero and match an independent integer computation; removed entries and logging order make no difference. The words around amounts, for every fraction with a denominator up to 60 and a size up to 10: never "about 0", never "±about", "of a turn" only below one turn and "turns" only above.
- **The rule.** The pick, the order of the rest, ties broken by recency and then by list order; the replay that notices a turn that did not go to the pick. The engine end to end: its own pick logged for 300 entries of random attendance, in 16 circles of both kinds, always has the largest balance present, keeps every group within its bound, is reported as followed by the replay, and makes the same choices as the simulator's copy of the rule.
- **The promise.** The identity checked in exact arithmetic for 2,000 random cases of balances, groups and α. `excess()` (one sort) against a check of every group, 300 times. The rule followed for 400 steps of random attendance with random tie choices, for both kinds of circle and 2 to 14 people, checking every group's bound after every step. From 300 arbitrary starting points, *D* never grows while the rule is followed. `promise()` and `promiseIf()` on known cases.
- **Exhaustive.** Every reachable state for turns circles of 2 to 5 people gives the table above (and the state counts); rounds circles of 2 to 8 reach exactly *n* − 1; the sorting shortcut agrees with an unsorted search for up to 4 people. The game for 2 to 4 people: survivable at the rule's worst case, not one step below. A hill-climbing adversary for 6 to 9 people finds nothing beyond the bound. For six people, the 64-entry attendance pattern behind the table's 119/60, replayed through the page's own engine: every entry goes to the pick, the largest balance is 119/60, and the README's table says that number.
- **Other rules.** The rota and fewest-times rules drift linearly on the pattern above while this rule stays within ⅚; the example year's numbers, and that the page's paragraph says them.
- **Two phones.** Merging in any order and any grouping gives the same circle; merging twice changes nothing; removals stick; merged balances equal the balances of the union of entries; mismatched circles are refused. Two phones that both log their own pick before swapping codes: each copy followed the rule, and the merged ledger reports a miss and a wider fence, as the README and the page warn. Codes round-trip (including a 60-person circle, whose presence mask needs 60 bits), are found inside a chat message or a link, and are refused when cut short, malformed, or naming someone not in the circle.
- **The page's numbers.** The table of worst cases and promises in `index.html` matches what the code computes.

The tests have teeth. Changing the tests' copy of the rule to pick whoever owes *least* makes 3 tests fail; the turns α at ⅓ instead of ½, 5; the rounds α at ¾ instead of 1, 4; the engine ranking the lowest balance first, 4; a fair share of 1/(*k* + 1) instead of 1/*k*, 12.

`ui-check.mjs` (96 checks, headless Chromium): at phone size by touch, the example opens with a pick, taking the pick out changes it and putting them back restores it, one tap logs one entry, five more taps during the rest log nothing, undo works, *Someone else did it* offers the other three with what each would do to the fence; at desktop size a held Enter logs exactly once and Space toggles a person; a new rounds circle of five promises ±4 portions and the buyer for three is 2 portions ahead; two phones exchange codes both ways and end with the same entries and balances, and a link with a code offers to add it; with storage blocked the page still logs and says so; with reduced motion nothing animates; in dark mode at both sizes, with every section open, nothing scrolls sideways and there are no console errors. Added after the review: twelve quick taps that flip the pick start at most one animation per 400 ms, slow taps that leave the pick alone start none, and nothing animates when the rest ends; a double tap on *Remove* removes one entry, and one *Undo* puts back two removals; keyboard focus stays on a person after Space (so Tab goes to the next one), on the log button through its rest, on *Has left*, and on a person's *Has left* after a rename and Tab, moves to the next *Remove* after a removal, and returns to *Someone else did it* after logging from its list; a balance of 1/30 reads "less than 0.1 of a turn"; the chart's label does not claim past balances were inside today's fence when they were not; two phones that both log before swapping codes are told that this can be why a pick reads as missed; the reminder to keep a copy comes after the second entry and again a week after the last copy, a backup downloaded before a circle was made does not hold that reminder back, and one downloaded after does; with no circles, a code can be pasted; and a link with a malformed `%`-escape raises no error.

## Honest assessment

- **[Certain] The arithmetic is exact.** Balances are exact fractions and add up to exactly zero, and the tests compare them with an independent integer computation.
- **[Certain] The promise.** If the pick is always followed (ties any way), nobody in a turns circle of *n* people ever owes or is owed more than (*n* − 1)/2 turns, and nobody in a rounds circle more than *n* − 1 portions, whatever the attendance, for every *n*. The proof is the identity above. The tests check the identity in exact arithmetic and check the bound after every step of many random runs, but the claim for all *n* rests on the proof, not the tests.
- **[Certain] The worst cases for small circles,** ½, ⅚, 1⅙ and 1⅗ turns for 2 to 5 people, and *n* − 1 portions for rounds circles of 2 to 8. These are exhaustive over every reachable state. For 6 people I only know it lies between 119/60 ≈ 1.98, the largest a search has found, and 2½.
- **[Certain] For 2, 3 and 4 people, no deterministic rule can keep the worst case lower than this one does.** That is the solved game. I did not check 5 or more, and I say nothing about randomised rules.
- **[Certain] After someone else does a turn, the fence the page shows is still a true promise for the future,** and it never widens while the pick is followed. It may not shrink back either; I have not shown that it does.
- **[Certain] Merging codes is order-independent and idempotent,** and two phones that have merged each other's codes agree on every balance.
- **[Likely] This is the problem and the rule the literature calls the carpool problem and greedy.** I could not open Fagin and Williams (1983), Ajtai et al. (1998) or Coppersmith et al. (2011) from here; I read abstracts and search summaries. Those summaries credit Fagin and Williams with the fair share and the greedy rule, report linear bounds for greedy of about *n*/2 or (*n* − 1)/2, and say greedy is optimal among online rules for a generalised version of the problem. But they disagree with each other about definitions and constants, and some of their figures do not match my exhaustive results for this version (everyone present gets an equal share). So every number on the page comes from my own proof and searches; the papers are cited for the problem and the rule, not for any bound.
- **[Likely] It is fast enough.** The page recomputes the ledger on each tap. In headless Chromium on this machine, with about 1,400 entries among four people a tap updates the page in under 0.1 s, and with about 5,000 entries among twelve, logging one takes about 0.2 s (0.15 to 0.23 s, measured inside the page after the review, on a busy machine). A weekly circle gathers a few hundred entries a year.
- **[Likely] The comparison with a plain rota is fair but chosen.** The school year is one made-up example (one seed, attendance chances 95%, 90%, 45% and 70% per run, keeping only runs with at least two people). Other examples give other numbers; the drift without limit is a constructed worst case, though the mechanism, a rota ignoring who was there, is general.
- **[Speculative] A named pick with one sentence of reason ends the argument.** That is the felt part, and I can't test it: no person has used this page. My guess is that the sentence matters more than the arithmetic, because it makes the decision the page's and not anyone's accusation.
- **[Speculative] Hiding the numbers helps.** Clark and Mills (1979) found that people who wanted a communal relationship with someone liked them less, not more, when a benefit came back to them as repayment for one they had given. That is about favours between two people in two experiments, not rotas. It is why the page leads with the name and folds the ledger away, but whether keeping an exact score of turns makes a group closer or more transactional is the question this tool cannot answer about itself.
- **Rough edges.**
  - No weights: a family with two children in the carpool counts as one person.
  - No "can't do it today" flag other than tapping someone else.
  - A circle's kind (turns or rounds) is fixed when it is created.
  - Syncing is by hand, by pasting codes, and two phones that both log before swapping codes can make entries that read as missed picks once merged.
  - Entry times come from each phone's clock, which affects tie-breaks and the history's order but not balances.
  - Names are last-writer-wins, so two simultaneous renames on two phones keep one.
  - The example school run is generated on first visit from the device's date.

## Where it could go next

- **Weights.** Let a person count double on a day (two children in the car, two coffees). The identity extends to unequal shares, but the bound changes and needs its own proof and tests.
- **The true worst case for 6 and up.** A smarter exhaustive search (or a cleverer invariant) could fill in the 6-person cell, and show whether this rule stays the best possible beyond four.
- **Sync without a server.** A QR code per entry, or a short "since last time" code, would make keeping two phones in step a glance instead of a paste.
- **Installable.** Served over https with a service worker, it could live on a home screen and work like an app (which a `file://` page cannot do).

## Revision note

An independent reviewer rebuilt the mathematics in separate Python and C and could not break it: the identity, the exhaustive tables and state counts, the solved game and the example year all reproduced. It found problems in the page and one loose figure. I reproduced each with my own code (Playwright scripts against a copy of the first version, and my own beam search for the six-person figure) before changing anything, and all ten held:

- **The pick's name animated on every tap, and once more on its own.** The animation class sat on the verdict box and was never removed, so every redraw replayed it: about every 167 ms under quick taps, on taps that left the pick unchanged, and again 1.1 s after each entry when the rest ended. That broke the README's own "at most once every 400 ms" and "nothing changes on its own". The class now goes on the new name only when the name changes and 400 ms have passed, the verdict is redrawn only when its words change, and the end of the rest only re-enables the buttons. Twelve quick taps now start four animations at least 400 ms apart, where they started eleven.
- **A double tap on *Remove* deleted two entries, and *Undo* brought back one.** After the first removal the next entry's button moved under the finger, and a removal is a tombstone that every merge keeps. *Remove* now rests for about a second like logging, and one *Undo* puts back every removal its slip covers.
- **Keyboard focus fell to the page after almost every action.** Toggling a person rebuilt every person's button, the log button was disabled while focused, and *Has left*, *Remove* and a rename rebuilt their rows, so Tab then Space toggled the wrong person. People and edit rows are now updated in place, the resting buttons use `aria-disabled`, and after a removal, or logging from *Someone else did it*, focus goes to the next *Remove* or back to the button that opened the list.
- **The six-person figure was loose.** 49/30 was only the most an unfinished search had seen. A beam search found a 64-entry attendance pattern, every entry going to the pick, that reaches 119/60 ≈ 1.98 (the reviewer's own search reached 29/15 ≈ 1.93, and two scoring rules of mine both stopped at 119/60). The table and the honest assessment now say "at least 119/60, the largest found", and a test replays the pattern through the engine. It is still not the true worst case.
- **Two phones that both logged before swapping codes were told someone had not followed the pick.** That is the ordinary way two phones get used, and the merge really does make some entries read as missed. The page and the README now name it as a possible cause, and the README suggests keeping the log on one phone.
- **Smaller fixes.** Tiny balances read "Ana owes about 0 of a turn"; they now read "less than 0.1 of a turn", "about 1 of a turn" is "about 1 turn", fences read "about ±2.1" rather than "±about 2.1", and the README no longer implies that sevenths are shown as fractions. The chart's label said every past balance was inside today's fence; it now says so only when it is true. The reminder to keep a copy waited for ten entries and three weeks, much too late for a weekly circle facing Safari's seven-day cap; it now comes after the second entry, then a week after the last copy, and the README says plainly that a reminder cannot guard against the cap by itself. With no circles there was nowhere to paste a code or restore a backup; *Start a circle* now offers both. A link with a malformed `%`-escape threw an uncaught error; it is now caught. A school year's code is about 6,000 characters, not 5,000, and the edit note no longer implies that a circle's kind can be changed before it has entries.
- **Mutation counts re-run.** On the revised suite, the fair-share mutation fails 12 tests (the first version said 10 where 11 was right) and ranking the lowest balance first fails 4; the others are unchanged.
