        How it decides, and what it can promise

        The rule

        Everyone has a balance. Each time, the people there split that turn between them: if four of you are in the car, a quarter of the run is each person's fair share, and whoever drives has done one whole run. Your balance is your fair shares added up, minus the turns you did. The pick is whoever owes the most. If two people owe exactly the same, it goes to whoever did it less recently.

        A rounds circle counts portions instead: when one person buys for four, each of the four had one, and the buyer bought four. A turn and a round are not the same thing: buying for eight is a bigger job than buying for two, and a rounds circle knows it.

        This is the greedy rule from Ronald Fagin and John Williams's 1983 paper on fair carpools, as later papers describe it; the tie-break is this page's. The fractions are kept exactly, so the balances always add up to exactly zero and nothing drifts from rounding.

        What it promises

        If the pick is always followed, nobody can ever owe, or be owed, more than (n − 1)/2 turns, where n is the number of people who have ever been in the circle, however attendance varies. For rounds it is n − 1 portions, which is what buying one round for everyone does anyway; no rule can do better than that.

        Why: take any group of people in the circle, of size j. The rule keeps their balances, added up, at most ½ · j · (n − j) (or j · (n − j) for rounds). That holds at the start, when everyone is at zero, and a short identity shows each turn keeps it true, because the person who does the turn owes at least as much as anyone else there. A group of one gives the promise. The whole proof is in the README and the tests check the identity.

        If someone else does a turn instead, the promise can widen, and the page says by how much before you log it. From then on, following the pick never widens it again.

        Why not just go round in order?

        A plain rota (skip whoever is away) ignores who was there, so the people who come least tend to end up doing more than their share. In a made-up school year of 190 runs for four parents, one of whom came on only 102 of them, a plain rota left someone 5.1 turns out at its worst. Giving it to whoever has done it fewest times was worse: the parent who came least drove 48 times against a fair share of about 29.6. This rule never let anyone get further than ⅚ of a turn from even. A rota can also drift without limit: if two people come every time and a third comes every third time, a rota can hand the third person every third turn, two-thirds of a turn more than their share each time round.

        Keeping score among friends

        Margaret Clark and Judson Mills (1979) found that when people wanted a close, communal relationship with someone, a favour given back as repayment for one they had done made them like the other person less, not more. A rota is a kind of score, so this page keeps the numbers out of the way: it names whose turn it is and why, and leaves the arithmetic folded up. The idea is that nobody has to keep count in their head, not that anyone should read the ledger out loud.
