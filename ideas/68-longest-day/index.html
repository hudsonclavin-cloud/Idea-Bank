The Longest Day of Your Life

Since 1973, the length of every day has been worked out against atomic clocks. The Moon, the wind and the Pacific are all in the differences.

A day is meant to last 86,400 seconds. The Earth's turn has been timed against atomic clocks for decades, and the U.S. Naval Observatory publishes the result as one number for every day since 3 January 1973: how many milliseconds longer or shorter than 86,400 seconds that day ran. Today the timing comes from radio telescopes watching quasars, lasers fired at satellites and at reflectors on the Moon, and GPS.

In 19,650 measured days, the Earth has hit 86,400 seconds exactly once, to the file's last decimal place: on 19 July 2008, give or take 3.3 microseconds. Nobody noticed. (Allowing for their stated errors, 49 days could have been exactly 86,400 s; this is the one the file rounds to zero.) Every other day was longer or shorter, and the differences turn out to carry the Moon's orbit, the jet streams and the Pacific Ocean.

How long was your day?

Pick a date: a birthday, a first day somewhere, a last day with someone. The record will tell you how long it was. Nothing you enter leaves this page.

The Moon

Lay the days end to end and ask what rhythms they hold. Take away each day's 365-day average, so only changes faster than a year are left, and run a periodogram, which measures how much of the wiggle repeats at each period. I use the days from 1985 on, for a reason that comes below. Sharp peaks stand up at 13.661, 9.133 and 27.555 days, with smaller ones at 14.765 and 31.807.

These are not round numbers. They are the Moon's. It swings from the equator to the north, back and down to the south once every 27.3216 days, so it crosses the equator every 13.6608. It comes closest to the Earth every 27.5545 days. The other peaks are combinations of these with each other and with the Sun. Every peak lands within 8 minutes of the period worked out from the Moon's orbit. I computed the periods first and then looked; nothing on this page fits them to the data.

The peaks are not faint. Power at 13.66 days is 29,048 times the typical power at neighbouring periods; the weakest lunar peak, at 31.81 days, is 52 times. Under a simple noise model, with no rhythm there, noise reaches even 52 times the typical level about once in 5×1015 tries.

Where nothing appears matters as much. The Moon's best-known rhythm, the 29.5306-day cycle of its phases, shows 0.04 times the background: nothing at all. The phases are about where sunlight falls on the Moon, which the Earth's spin cannot feel. They leave a mark only at half their period, the small 14.765-day peak, because the Sun's pull squashes the Moon's orbit so that it runs slightly nearer at new Moon and at full Moon alike. There is nothing at 27.3216 days either (0.14 times background), because the Moon far north and the Moon far south squeeze the Earth alike.

Squeeze is the right word. The Moon raises tides in the rock as well as the sea, and part of that tide is a slow change in the Earth's shape: a slightly wider waist when the Moon is over the equator, slightly less when it rides far north or south. A wider waist turns slower, the way a skater slows by opening her arms. The fitted ripple agrees about timing: its peaks fall when the Moon crosses the equator and when it is nearest (correlations of 0.998 and 0.9997 with curves that peak at those moments, computed from the Moon's mean orbit: the timing agrees to within about 4 hours). The standard calculation of these tidal changes in the Earth's spin is Yoder, Williams and Parke's (1981).

A fair objection: the people who make this file know about the Moon too. They use a model of these tides while combining the measurements (USNO's notes record an upgrade to it in 2012), so could the peaks be the model, put back in? For the early years, largely yes, and the file itself shows it. In 1974–1982, the wiggle the Moon and the seasons leave behind, at periods from about a week to a month, has only 31% of the power it has had since 1985, although the stated errors then were 39 times larger: those years were heavily smoothed. Yet the fortnightly wave fitted to them is full size, 0.35 ms, the same as since then (0.35 ms). Smoothing that strong would have flattened it too, unless the tide was taken out first and put back afterwards. So before the mid-1980s the 9–32-day ripple in this file is mostly the producers' tide model. From then on the background at these periods is as strong as today's, so the smoothing there is light (the IERS's 2001 explanatory supplement describes Bulletin A's as keeping 95% of any rhythm slower than three days), and a 13.66-day ripple in the file is the ripple the instruments saw. That is why every test of the Moon on this page starts in 1985. Independent evidence is older still: in 1981 Yoder, Williams and Parke found the fortnightly and monthly terms in lunar laser ranging data at the strength their calculation predicted.

Then a forecast. Six lunar sine waves, with periods fixed by the orbit and only their sizes and timing fitted, were matched to the days from 1985 up to 2016. From then until April 2026 they account for 76% of the day-to-day wiggle left once the slow drift and the seasons are taken out.

The fortnightly ripple is not steady. Fit it one calendar year at a time and, from 1985 on, its size swings between 0.22 ms (1995) and 0.51 ms (2024), in step with an 18.6-year wobble of the Moon's orbit (correlation 0.99 over 41 years). When the tilt of the Moon's orbit adds to the tilt of the Earth's axis, the Moon swings farthest north and south each month, an event called a major lunar standstill, and the day's ripple is biggest. Its smallest years come near minor standstills, when the tilts subtract. The pale bars before 1985 follow the same curve, but there it is largely the tide model's curve, not a measurement.

Hear it

Played as sound at 4,000 days a second, fifty-three years pass in under five seconds and the Moon's rhythms become notes: the fortnight sounds near 293 Hz, the closest-approach month an octave lower near 145 Hz. The data are only filtered and sped up; nothing is synthesised. The start is the smoothest stretch, not the noisiest: the file's stated errors in the 1970s are around 0.20 ms, against a few thousandths today, but those years were heavily smoothed when the record was compiled, so they carry less fast wiggle than any later stretch. The second button plays the same days with the six lunar sine waves subtracted. The notes go; what is left is weather and measurement noise, and the first years are the quietest of all.

Sound starts only when you press a button. Use moderate volume.

The wind

Stack the years on top of one another and a second rhythm appears. Averaged over the record, days run longest around 9 April (+0.45 ms against their 365-day average) and shortest around 22 July (−0.75 ms), a swing of 1.21 ms. The northern summer, the season of the longest daylight, is when the planet spins fastest.

The Sun raises the same kind of tide as the Moon, with yearly and half-yearly periods, so part of this curve is tidal. Geophysicists trace most of it to the wind. The atmosphere and the solid Earth share one store of spin; when the westerly winds aloft blow harder, the air carries more of it and the ground turns a little slower. Rosen and Salstein (1983) set six years of global wind analyses beside the day's length and found the same yearly signal in both. This page has no wind data, so here the cause is reported, not shown.

The Pacific

The winds have a slower rhythm too. In an El Niño, warm water spreads east across the tropical Pacific and the atmosphere above it changes. Dickey and colleagues (1994) found that the atmosphere's spin accounted for up to 92% of the year-to-year variance in the day's length, in their study of the 1982–83 El Niño.

The record can be set beside NOAA's Oceanic Niño Index, a three-month average of how far the sea in the central tropical Pacific is from its normal temperature. I compared the two as twelve-month changes (this month's three-month average minus last year's), month by month from March 1974 to August 2026. I fixed that comparison, with no time lag, before computing it. Nothing timestamps that choice, so here is the check that matters: moving either series by a month changes the result by at most 0.04, and each third of the record on its own gives 0.46 to 0.52. The correlation is 0.48. Both series drift, so their 630 months are not 630 independent pieces of information. Counting how long each series' wiggles persist, lag by lag, they carry about 94; allowing for that, a correlation this strong would arise by chance with probability about 9×10−7, roughly one in a million. Scrambling agrees: none of 20,000 copies of the day-length series with the same rhythms but random timing came as close.

Not every El Niño shows. The 1982–83 and 2015–16 events stand out; 1997–98 and 2023–24, as strong in the ocean, barely register in this measure. As I write, the index for July to September 2026 is +2.16 °C, the highest since early 2016, and the three-month average day is 0.94 ms longer than a year before: the largest twelve-month lengthening in the record. Whether the ocean is the reason this time, the data cannot yet say.

The missing seconds

Add the days up and they become seconds. The record's days averaged 1.32 ms longer than 86,400 s, and from 3 January 1973 to 21 October 2026 the excess sums to 25.85 seconds: the planet has fallen that far behind the atomic clocks. Civil time is held within 0.9 s of the Earth by leap seconds, and 25 were added over the same span (27 since the scheme began in 1972).

None has been added since 31 December 2016. Since then the Earth has fallen behind by only 0.63 s, because the days have run close to 86,400 s and, from 2020, often under it. The first day in the record shorter than 86,400 s was 2 August 1986; 2,103 days have been shorter in all, and 2020 was the first calendar year whose days averaged less than 86,400 s, if only by 0.004 ms.

Why are most days long at all? When the second was pinned down in the 1950s and 60s, it was matched to astronomical tables built from observations reaching back to 1750, so in effect it is one 86,400th of a day from the early 1800s (for the history, see Finkleman and colleagues, 2011). The day has kept lengthening since. The Moon's tides brake the Earth enough to add about 2.3 ms per century, but something non-tidal partly offsets them (the authors point to the crust's slow rebound since the last ice age, and to the core), and over the last 2,700 years the day has in fact lengthened by about 1.8 ms per century on average (Stephenson, Morrison and Hohenkerk, 2016). Over these fifty-four years that braking is buried under decade-long swings, which geophysicists attribute mostly to the liquid core trading spin with the mantle (Hide and Dickey, 1991). In 2022 the General Conference on Weights and Measures decided that the allowed gap between Earth time and clock time will be widened in or before 2035, which ends the leap second as we know it. News reports say that at its next meeting, on 15 October 2026, it voted to stop adding leap seconds from 20 May 2027; I could not read the official text.

What the record is, and isn't

  • The number is the length of a mean solar day, not hours of daylight. It is the time Universal Time (UT1, the Earth's own clock) takes to advance one day, minus 86,400 SI seconds, from IERS Bulletin A as collected in the file finals2000A.all.
  • Early values are rougher. The stated error is 0.19 ms on the record's first days and about 0.004 ms in recent years (median of 2016–2025). "Longest" among the 1970s days is a close race decided partly by noise.
  • Early values are also smoothed, with the tide put back. Before the mid-1980s the day-to-day detail is a heavily smoothed curve with the Moon's tide added back from a model, so the Moon is tested here only on 1985 on, and which early day comes out longest is partly the model's choice.
  • Recent values are provisional. The newest weeks are rapid estimates. The bulletin of 22 October 2026 also announces a new processing method and warns that values up to a year old may shift slightly (under 20 microseconds in UT1). Its listed inputs include meteorological predictions of the atmosphere's spin; USNO's notes say that since 2010 these enter the combination only at its newest point.
  • The rhythms are measured here; the causes are cited. The Moon's periods and timing are tested against the data on this page. That the seasonal and El Niño signals come from the wind, and the slow swings from the core, is the published geophysics, not something this page shows.
  • The Niño index is revised. NOAA computes it against shifting 30-year baselines, and the file's latest values can change.
