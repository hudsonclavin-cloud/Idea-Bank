# A Little of Every Year
> An invented solera, drawn from and topped up every year since 1826. Arithmetic says exactly how much of every year is in this year's glass, and that once the casks' evaporation is counted, a glass has held less than one molecule of 1826, on average, since 2011. The chalk on the bottom row still says 1826. Whether it is still the same wine is not arithmetic.

**Kind:** explorable
**Run:** open `ideas/60-solera/index.html` in a browser (plain classic scripts, so `file://` works; no build, no network). Tap *Run the scales* to move one year, *Keep running* to let the years go by. Optional URL parameters: `?now=2040` pretends the present is another year, `?year=1914` opens on that year's glass, `?your=1990` fills in *Your year*, `?evap=0` opens the solera with that much evaporation (a percentage, 0 to 6 in halves). Tests: `node --test ideas/60-solera/*.test.mjs` from the repo root (Node built-ins only).
**Made by:** Venencia

## What it is

A cellar of casks in four rows, invented, founded in 1826. Once a year a quarter of the bottom row is drawn off and bottled. The bottom row is topped up from the row above, that row from the one above it, and the top row with young wine. Through the year every cask also loses 3.5% of its wine through the wood, and that is made up from the row above too. Nobody has emptied a cask since. This is a *solera*, the way sherry and some other wines, vinegars and spirits are aged, and it is the closest thing I know to a ship of Theseus you can drink: every year a little of it is replaced, and it keeps its name.

The page shows the casks (each one coloured by the average age of what is inside, with the bottom row chalked *1826*), a glass of this year's wine, and five things to do with them:

1. **Run the scales.** One tap is one year: the glass is drawn from the bottom row, each row is topped up from the one above, and young wine goes in at the top. *Keep running* lets the years go by and stops at each milestone. A slider goes anywhere from 1827 to 2326.
2. **Who is in the glass.** A chart of every year whose wine is in the glass, on a log scale, with a dashed line at *one molecule per 100 ml glass*. Above the line a glass holds at least one molecule of that year, on average; below it, less than one. Touch any bar to read its share and its molecule count.
3. **Your year.** Type a year (the year you were born, or someone was) and an optional word for it. The page says when that year's wine first reached the glass, when it was at its most, how much of it is in the glass you are looking at, and from when a glass holds less than one molecule of it.
4. **The cellar book.** A timeline of what the arithmetic says will happen, past and future.
5. **Why the numbers are exact**, a short proof on the page, and **Change the solera**, three sliders (rows, the share drawn each year, and the share lost to evaporation) that rewrite everything above.

## Why I made it

I had one free slot and no brief, so I listed what the bank didn't have yet. Nothing about food, smell or taste. Nothing measured in centuries. Nothing about strangers who cooperate without meeting. I wrote down ten candidates and crossed most of them off: a time-locked letter that takes your phone a night of sequential squaring to open (too close to *Two Sheets of Nothing*, and phones kill background tabs at night); the radiocarbon age of deep ocean water, "the last time this water saw the sky" (needs a data set I couldn't fetch and verify); John Cage's 639-year organ piece in Halberstadt (I couldn't verify its future schedule); synchronising fireflies (already done well elsewhere, and *Out of Step* is about synchrony); the physics of a boiled egg (too close to *Backtime*); the zipper merge (its strongest claims are contested); the stone tablets on the Japanese coast that tell descendants not to build below them (too heavy to turn into a toy); and a few more.

The solera won because it is the one food I know that is also a mathematical object, and because the two halves of the batch's theme fall out of it on their own. The part that can be proven is the composition of the glass. It is exact, and the argument fits in a paragraph: follow a molecule backwards and count coin tosses. The part that can only be felt is the question an old solera invites: is this still the 1826? Arithmetic can tell you that, once evaporation is counted, a glass has held less than one molecule of the founding wine, on average, since 2011, while the bottom row is still chalked *1826*. It cannot tell you whether that matters. The cellar master who wrote *1826* on the bottom row had already decided it doesn't.

It is also the cooperation-between-strangers piece I wanted. Nobody who filled those casks in 1826 drank the glass of 2026, and nobody who drinks a glass in 2026 will be alive for the glass of 2206, the last that holds, on average, a molecule of this year's young wine. Every year someone draws a quarter and tops it up for people they will never meet. The *Your year* box exists for that: it says, with exact arithmetic, from when a glass holds less than one molecule of a year you care about.

Two things surprised me while building it, and the independent review corrected the first. I built the first version without evaporation, and in that model the founding wine is not gone yet: about 21,000 molecules per glass in 2026, below one only from 2063. That was the fact I led with, and it does not survive evaporation. Real butts lose about 3.5% of their wine a year, and making that up from the row above flushes old wine out of every row faster: with it counted, a glass has held less than one molecule of 1826, on average, since 2011, and even 1% a year would bring it forward to 2041. The lesson I take is how much a small, steady loss matters when it is replaced from the top. Second, the rows are not mainly a memory device. For the same draw and evaporation, four rows keep the founding wine only fifteen years longer than one cask would (2011 against 1996). What the rows buy is a steadier glass: no wine younger than four years reaches it, and each year's wine arrives a little at a time. If each year's young wine differed from the others at random, the change from one year's glass to the next would be 1.8 times smaller with the rows than with a single cask worked to the same 12-year average age (2.3 times without evaporation). Keeping one bottling like the next is the usual reason given for working a solera in rows, and the comparison is on the page under *Change the solera*. (I first argued this from the spread of ages inside one glass, which is the wrong measure: a single cask actually blends more years together.)

## How it works

**The model.** *n* rows of equal volume. Through the year every cask loses a fraction *e* of its wine to evaporation, every molecule alike. Once a year a fraction *f* of the bottom row is drawn off; each row is then topped up from the one above, enough to replace what it gave and what it lost, and the top row with young wine. So each year row *d* (counting the top row as 1) has a share *g*<sub>*d*</sub> = *f* + (*n* − *d* + 1)*e* of its wine replaced from above, and the young wine at the top replaces the draw and every row's loss, *f* + *ne*. Each cask is perfectly mixed and nothing changes chemically. Year *Y*'s glass is the bottom row as it stands before that year's draw. The young wine put in at the top in year *Y* is labelled *Y*. Defaults: *n* = 4, *f* = ¼, *e* = 3.5%, founded 1826, so *g* = 39%, 35.5%, 32% and 28.5% from the top row down.

**Where 3.5% comes from.** It is the Consejo Regulador's average loss for sherry butts: about 3.5% of their contents a year, about 4% in dry years and 3% in wet, mostly water. I found it quoted from the Consejo in sherry.wine's material (its Spanish teaching note *La crianza del Jerez* gives "evaporación media del 3,5% anual, principalmente de agua"; its English *Sherry ageing* note gives 3–4%; its ageing page gives 3–5% of the wine stored). A 2025 review of the criaderas and solera system (*Foods*, PMC12732140) gives up to about 4.5% a year for fino. I reached these through a search tool; I could not open the Consejo's own documents from here.

**The argument.** Take a molecule in the bottom row and follow it backwards one year at a time. In each year a share *g*<sub>*n*</sub> of that row had just arrived from above, so with mixing (and evaporation taking every molecule alike) the molecule had just arrived with probability *g*<sub>*n*</sub>, independently of every other year. Once it is in the row above, the same holds with *g*<sub>*n*−1</sub>, and so on up. Its age *K* is therefore a sum of *n* geometric waiting times, one per row, and the founding wine is the event that the molecule has not finished its *n* arrivals in all the years since the founding. For the glass of year *Y* (*t* = *Y* − 1826 years after the founding):

- share of wine *k* years old = P(*K* = *k*) = P<sub>*n*</sub>(*k*), where P<sub>*d*</sub>(*k*) = (1 − *g*<sub>*d*</sub>) P<sub>*d*</sub>(*k* − 1) + *g*<sub>*d*</sub> P<sub>*d*−1</sub>(*k* − 1), starting from P<sub>0</sub>(0) = 1. This is the cascade itself, written for ages;
- share of founding wine = P(*K* ≥ *t*), from the same recursion for the tail;
- in the long run, mean age = Σ 1/*g*<sub>*d*</sub> and variance = Σ (1 − *g*<sub>*d*</sub>)/*g*<sub>*d*</sub><sup>2</sup>.

With no evaporation every *g*<sub>*d*</sub> = *f*, and these become closed forms: the share *k* years old is C(*k*−1, *n*−1) *f*<sup>*n*</sup> (1−*f*)<sup>*k*−*n*</sup>, a negative binomial; the founding share is P(Binomial(*t* − 1, *f*) ≤ *n* − 1); the mean age is *n*/*f*. In that case the peak is a tie between ages 12 and 13: C(11,3)·3<sup>8</sup>/4<sup>12</sup> = C(12,3)·3<sup>9</sup>/4<sup>13</sup> exactly, which is why the lossless cellar book says "2038 and 2039". With 3.5% evaporation the peak is at age 9 alone.

**Molecules.** A share becomes a count by multiplying by the molecules in the glass. A 100 ml glass of dry 17% wine holds about 13.4 g of ethanol (0.29 mol) and 83–84 g of water (4.6–4.7 mol), so 2.9–3.0 × 10<sup>24</sup> molecules. The lower figure takes the water as the remaining 83 ml; the upper one uses a mixture density of 0.977 g/ml, which accounts for the contraction on mixing. The page uses 3.0 × 10<sup>24</sup> and ignores the sugars, glycerol and acids, which add little. A year is *in the glass* when its expected count is at least one. Treating the count as Poisson (a huge number of molecules, each with a tiny chance of being from that year), the page also gives the chance of at least one molecule when the expected count is below one.

**Exactness.** `solera.js` runs the recursion in natural-log space. Every term is positive, so nothing cancels and tiny shares never underflow. It keeps the closed forms for the lossless case as a check, and the tests compare both with exact fractions. It has no DOM, clock or randomness, and loads both as a classic script (`window.Solera`) and with `require`. `app.js` draws the casks, the glass and the chart on canvases and keeps the prose in step. Every share, count and year the page computes comes from `solera.js`. The few fixed figures in its prose (the molecule count, the evaporation caveat) are ones the tests pin.

**The page.** The casks are a front view of the rows, each with a window onto the wine coloured by its average age; the colours are a fiction (older is darker) and not the colours of real sherry. The row labels give each row's average age just after this year's scales, and the glass was drawn before them, so the bottom row reads a year younger than the glass. A step animates the year's loss and the draw, then each refill from the bottom row upward, then the young wine at the top. With `prefers-reduced-motion` the step is instant and *Keep running* advances one year every 0.6 s with nothing moving but the numbers. Light and dark schemes follow the system setting. The chart can be read by touch (drag sideways; vertical swipes still scroll). The only thing stored is your year and its note, in `localStorage`, wrapped so the page works without it. Nothing is sent anywhere.

**Tests** (Node built-ins only):

- `solera.test.mjs` (22 tests). **Exact cascade:** without evaporation, the closed forms and the recursion equal a step-by-step cascade done in exact rational arithmetic, for every vintage in every glass and every row, for 40 years and six combinations of rows and draw. With evaporation, the recursion equals the solera worked by volumes in exact fractions (lose *e*, draw *f*, refill each row to full from the one above), with no turnover formula, for five combinations over 30 years; the young wine there comes to *f* + *ne* exactly. **Far tail:** the log-space shares match exact rationals 200 and 500 years out, to nine significant figures. **Forward simulation:** 20,000 labelled molecules per row, evaporated, drawn and refilled at random with no formulas, agree with the glass formula (chi-square), with and without evaporation. Scored against wrong rules (3 or 5 rows, a 22% or 28% draw, no evaporation, 2% or 5%), the same check fails by more than 10 times its pass mark. **Quoted figures:** every figure in this README is pinned, for both columns, along with the evaporation table, the comparisons and the molecule-count ranges below. **Run of years:** every year in the run the page reports has at least one expected molecule and no older year does, for six settings and every seventh year to 2326. **Caveats:** the water-only variant described below is computed separately and pinned.
- `page.test.mjs` (8 tests) runs `app.js` in a stand-in DOM and checks the words it writes: the 2026 facts, the run of years in the glasses where the founding wine outlasts the oldest years, "1 molecule", the "on average" on every "less than one molecule" milestone, the year-to-year comparison, the label on the casks, the evaporation slider, and that the README tagline and cellar book carry the evaporating figures.

## Honest assessment

- **[Certain] The arithmetic is right for the model.** The recursion is the cascade itself, and the tests check it against exact fractions, with and without evaporation, against the closed forms without, and against a forward simulation of molecules that uses no formula. Nothing on the page is simulated or estimated: every share, count and year is computed exactly from the model, then rounded for display.
- **[Certain] The quoted numbers are what the code computes** with 4 rows, a quarter drawn a year, 3.5% lost to evaporation, a founding in 1826, and 3.0 × 10<sup>24</sup> molecules in a glass (and, in the second column, with no evaporation). The tests pin each one.
- **[Certain] How the "one molecule" years depend on the glass.** They are threshold crossings, so they move with the molecule count. With evaporation, 2011 holds for any count from 2.67 to 3.73 × 10<sup>24</sup>, and a 75 ml glass makes it 2010. 2026's last glass with a molecule is 2206 for any count from 2.45 to 3.42 × 10<sup>24</sup>, so for any 15–20% wine. Without evaporation, 2063 holds from 2.79 to 3.67 × 10<sup>24</sup>, a 75 ml glass makes it 2062, and 2026's last glass is 2257 only from 2.94 × 10<sup>24</sup>. The page computes the 75 ml case for whatever the sliders say.
- **[Certain] It runs.** `node tools/check.mjs ideas/60-solera` passes: README format, size, tests, and the headless smoke test at desktop and at 390 px phone width with no console errors and no horizontal scroll. I also drove it headless at phone size with reduced motion and *Your year* set to 1990: *Keep running* from 2026 stopped at 2030, 2035, 2171 and 2207 with the texts above, then ran on to 2326.
- **[Likely] A real solera worked like this one would no longer hold its founding wine in a 2026 glass, on average.** The 3.5% is the Consejo's average, the losses are made up from the row above, and across its wet-year to dry-year range (3% to 4%) the model puts the founding wine below one molecule per glass by 2016 at the latest. The model's weak point is that it treats every molecule alike when the real loss is mostly water. That makes old ethanol leave too fast. In the opposite extreme, where only water leaves, the year moves from 2011 to 2023 (and to 2026 at 3% a year, 2021 at 4%), but the wine would by now be 26.5% alcohol, well above the 17–22% the sherry rules list for an oloroso. So the truth lies between, and either way the 2026 glass holds less than one expected molecule. What could still overturn this is the mixing (next but one).
- **[Likely] The outline of the real method is right.** A real solera has rows of casks, with the solera on the floor and the *criaderas* above it. Bottling draws from the solera (the *saca*), and each row is refilled from the next younger one (the *rocío*), the whole operation being *correr las escalas*, running the scales. I checked these terms against several wine references found by search but could not open the Consejo Regulador's own documents. I deliberately state no regulatory limit on the saca (a 40% annual cap is widely repeated, but I could not confirm it in the official specification) and no particular bodega's practice.
- **[Speculative] Perfect mixing is a fair stand-in for a real solera.** A real cask is not mixed the way the model's are: wine is drawn from one place and added at another, and a row is many casks, so how evenly wine is spread between them matters too. The model is exact only for the idealisation. A real solera's oldest traces could be larger or smaller than these depending on how unevenly casks are worked; I have no data to say which.
- **[Speculative] The felt part works.** With evaporation counted, the moment is no longer in the future: the 2026 glass the page opens on already holds, on average, none of 1826, and the facts say so beside the chalk. I think that is a sharper version of the question than a date in 2063 was. I can't know whether it lands. The risk the reviewer named is real: the bodega looks the same in every year, so the beat rests on a few sentences and a dashed line on a log chart, and the page can read as a well-made explainer about sherry.
- **Rough edges.** The casks show only colour, so *Keep running* is visually quiet at steady state. The chalk font falls back to a serif where no handwriting font is installed. With *Change the solera* at 8 rows and 5% with no evaporation, the founding wine's last glass is centuries past the slider's end (the knob text still gives the year). The 1826 founding is fixed. I did not let you choose the founding year, so that the felt claim stays about one particular, if invented, solera. Evaporation is the same in every row; real top rows and bottom rows sit in different air.

## Where it could go next

- **A real solera.** With a bodega's actual cask records (how much was drawn and when, row by row, including the irregular years and the measured losses), the same arithmetic would give the real composition of a real bottle. The method does not need equal draws; it needs the history.
- **Water and ethanol apart, and uneven casks.** Track water and ethanol separately, with the loss split between them as measured, and let individual casks be worked unevenly. Then show how far the idealised numbers move. That would turn the two biggest caveats into measured ones.
- **Other perpetual things.** A perpetual stew, a sourdough starter and a Chinese master stock are, roughly, one-row soleras drawn far more often, so their founding matter thins out in months rather than decades. A sourdough adds a twist: its microbes can be *descendants* of the founders even when none of the founding flour is left, a different kind of continuity. That deserves its own piece.
- **A shared chalk.** Several visitors, a shared store, and each one's year written on the top row: a solera of strangers, where everyone can see whose year is still in the glass.

## Revision note

An independent reviewer rebuilt the arithmetic with exact fractions in separate code. It reproduced every number in the first version and found two real problems and five smaller ones. I reproduced each with my own code before changing anything, and all of them held:

- **Evaporation was left out, and it removes the lead.** The first version said evaporation would make the oldest traces "smaller", which was true, but did not say how much. At the Consejo Regulador's 3.5% a year, made up from the row above, the year a glass falls below one molecule of 1826 moves from 2063 to 2011, so the 2026 glass no longer holds the founding wine, on average. Evaporation is now part of the model (`evap`, default 3.5%, with a slider from 0 to 6% and `?evap=`), with the turnover of each row derived and checked against the solera worked by volumes. The tagline, the lead, the facts, the cellar book, the proof and this README now use it, and the lossless figures are kept only as a comparison. Other headline changes: average age 16 → 12 years, every year from 1826 → from 1846 in the 2026 glass, a glass holds less than one molecule of 2026 from 2207 instead of 2258, and of 1990 from 2171 instead of 2222. A caveat brackets the uniform-loss assumption with a water-only variant (2023).
- **"Every year from 1826 to … is in it" was false in some glasses.** In 2059–2062 without evaporation (2008–2010 with it) the founding wine outlasts the oldest single years, and the page claimed an unbroken run from 1826 and named the wrong scarcest year. The run is now counted back from the youngest year and the founding wine is reported on its own.
- **"The last of 1826 leaves the glass in 2063"** dropped its "on average" in the knob text, the coda and the tagline. Every such line now says "less than one molecule …, on average".
- **The case for the rows used the wrong measure.** The spread of ages in one glass does not measure how alike one year's glass is to the next. The page and README now give the year-to-year change instead (1.8 times smaller than a single cask's with evaporation, 2.3 without) and name the mechanism.
- **Smaller fixes:** "about 1 molecules" is now singular; the casks are labelled as they stand just after the scales and the glass as drawn before them, which explains why the bottom row reads a year younger than the glass; the README's 1990 row in the cellar book now says it needs 1990 typed into *Your year*.

`page.test.mjs` is new and pins the page's words; `solera.test.mjs` pins the evaporating model and every revised figure. Each new test fails on the first version.
