A Little of Every Year

Four rows of casks. Once a year a quarter of the bottom row is drawn off and bottled. The bottom row is topped up from the row above it, that row from the one above, and the top row with young wine. Nobody has emptied a cask since 1826.

What the arithmetic says will happen, and when. Keep running stops at each of these.

    The arithmetic can say how much of every year is in the glass, and in which year the last of 1826 leaves it. It cannot say whether it is still the same wine. The chalk on the bottom row says it is.

    Why the numbers are exact

    Take one molecule in this year’s glass and follow it backwards, a year at a time. Every year, a quarter of the bottom row had just come down from the row above. The casks here are perfectly mixed, so our molecule was one of those newcomers with chance ¼, whatever happened in any other year. Once it is in the row above, the same is true there, and so on up. It went in at the top on its fourth arrival, counting backwards.

    So its age is the number of tosses it takes a coin that lands heads one time in four to show four heads, and every share on this page is a count of coin tosses:

    • Share of the glass that is k years old C(k−1, 3) · (¼)4 · (¾)k−4, for k ≥ 4
    • Share that is still the founding wine the chance of fewer than 4 heads in (year − 1827) tosses
    • Average age, once the founding wine is gone rows ÷ draw = 4 ÷ ¼ = 16 years

    To turn a share into molecules takes one more number. A 100 ml glass of dry 17% wine holds about 3.0 × 1024 molecules of water and ethanol (about 84 g of water and 13 g of ethanol). A year counts as in the glass when its share times that number is at least one.

    What the arithmetic leaves out

    • A real cask is not perfectly mixed. Wine is drawn and added with care, and mixing inside a cask is slow, so a real solera only approximates this one.
    • Wine evaporates through the wood, and casks are topped up with younger wine to make up the loss. That gives every molecule one more way out each year, so with the same draws the traces of the oldest years would be smaller than these, not larger.
    • Molecules do not sit still chemically. Ethanol oxidises, esters form, and water molecules trade hydrogen atoms constantly; “a molecule of 1826” means a molecule’s worth of that year’s wine.
    • Real soleras differ in the number of rows, how often and how much they draw, and how young the young wine is. This one is invented.
    • Years that hinge on “one molecule” move by a year or two with the size of the glass: a 75 ml glass loses the last of 1826 in 2062.
