Lies Flat

A sheet of creases built to fold flat, and beside it the same creases once it has.

What is proven

Every vertex inside the sheet is tested, not sampled. Those tests say each vertex can fold flat on its own; the two-stage folding below says the whole sheet can, and the code checks that argument's preconditions every time.

For the plotter

Times assume the pen draws at 40 mm/s, travels at 80 mm/s with the pen up, and takes 0.25 s to lift and lower. Black is drawn first, then the pen is changed for red.

How it folds

  1. The straight lines are all mountain or all valley in turn. Pleat them like a paper fan, and the sheet becomes a narrow strip many layers thick. In each strip the zigzags mirror those of the strip beside it, so in the thick strip every layer's zigzag creases fall on the same straight lines.
  2. Pleat the thick strip along those lines, one after another, each time folding the rest of the strip up and over onto what is already folded. Nothing ever has to pass through anything.
  3. Those lines are not parallel, so every pleat turns the strip a little. It winds round and round; seen through the paper, the creases draw the figure on the right, each in its own colour.

This is the idealised paper of mathematics, with no thickness. Real paper would not take hundreds of layers, and the drawing is not meant to be cut out and folded; it is meant to be looked at.
