# After You
> A mirror game for two people on one screen. Each of you is secretly told to lead or to follow; afterwards the trace shows who really went first.

**Kind:** two-player game
**Run:** open `ideas/57-after-you/index.html` in a browser (double-clicking the file works; no server, build or network). Two people, one device. Either lay a phone or tablet flat on the table and sit facing each other across it (each of you owns the half nearest you, and your words face you), or sit side by side at a laptop or a tablet in landscape (Orange on the left, Blue on the right). You each keep one finger on your own half, both moving at once. No touchscreen: Orange steers with W A S D, Blue with the arrow keys, and a mouse works for one of you. Rule tests: `node --test ideas/57-after-you/mirror.test.mjs` (about 15 s). Headless two-player playthrough with two simultaneous touches: `node ideas/57-after-you/check.mjs` (about 2 minutes; uses the globally installed Playwright that `tools/smoke.mjs` uses).
**Made by:** Tain

## What it is

The screen is a pane of glass with a mirror line across the middle. Orange owns one half, Blue the other. Each of you has a dot under your finger, and in your half a ring shows where your partner's dot would be if it were reflected through the line. The job is to stay mirror images: keep your dot on the ring while you both move. When you manage it, the mirror line glows and each dot sings the same note. When you stop, or drift apart, the glass mists over.

Before each round you each press and hold a card to read a secret order, shielding it with your other hand: **LEAD** or **FOLLOW**. Each order is its own coin toss, so half the time you get the same one. Then the glass is up for 30 seconds, and nobody talks. The mirror has to hold for at least 10 of those seconds, every round, or the glass breaks and you both lose. Afterwards each of you secretly guesses the other's order, and the reveal shows:

- both orders and both guesses;
- a strip of the 30 seconds, coloured half-second by half-second by who was ahead: Orange, Blue, *together* (nobody ahead), *apart*, *too close to call*, or *still*;
- both paths laid over each other, with a scrubber, so you can put the dots back where they were at the moment you are arguing about;
- who moved first, and how far behind the follower typically was.

There are four scored rounds (fewer if the glass breaks), plus a practice round with no orders that just asks each of you, privately, "who was leading?" and then shows you the trace. The game ends with one question you answer out loud before you are allowed to see the bar: *which of you usually leads?*

## Why I made it

There is an old theatre exercise called the mirror. Two people face each other, one leads, the other copies, and after a while the teacher says "now nobody leads", and they carry on. If it goes well, they report something odd: the movement keeps going and neither of them is making it. Noy, Dekel and Alon turned this into a lab task in 2011 (*PNAS* 108:20947): two people sliding handles along parallel tracks, mirroring each other. As I read the paper, a designated follower trails with jittery, corrective motion, while expert improvisers, told to lead nobody, sometimes reached stretches the authors called *co-confident*: smooth, synchronised and jitter-free, with no apparent leader.

What I wanted to know is whether "who is leading" is a fact about the motion or a feeling about it. Part of it is plainly a fact. If your dot keeps arriving where mine was a quarter of a second ago, you are following me, and that can be measured and proven to be measured right. But the part people care about, *who meant to*, is not in the trace at all. A follower who predicts a regular pattern gets ahead of it and the trace calls them the leader. Two people who were both told to follow can still produce a dance, each convinced the other started it, the way a Ouija planchette moves with nobody pushing. I wanted a game where the measurable part and the felt part are both on the table, and where the gap between them is the thing two people end up arguing about.

The form constraint (two people, one screen) suited it, because a mirror needs exactly two people and cannot be played alone in any meaningful way. The other person's motion *is* your input.

Ideas I listed first and crossed out:

- **Both or neither.** Each of you secretly answers yes or no to a question, and the screen reveals only whether you *both* said yes (the "dating problem", or Bert den Boer's five-card trick). Real cryptography, but on one phone the device sees both answers, so the proof is trivial and it plays like a single tap.
- **The two-handed monochord.** One person frets a string, the other plucks it. Lovely, but the conversation is just "lower… no, higher", and it is mostly a music toy.
- **The hidden-agenda planchette.** A shared pointer moved by the sum of two fingers, each player secretly steering toward a different word. Funny for one round; the vector sum is all there is to prove.
- **Meeting point.** Both secretly pick a spot on a strange map and score if you match (Schelling focal points). Close to existing party games, and nothing happens *between* you while you play.
- **Tangram dictation.** One describes abstract shapes, the other picks them, and your descriptions shrink into private nicknames round by round (Clark and Wilkes-Gibbs). The best "understand each other" idea, but one screen means passing the phone dozens of times.
- **Tap together.** Both tap a beat at once and the screen shows who adjusts to whom (joint-tapping studies find both do). Strong measurement, thin game.
- **After you, the door.** A pure war of attrition: who gives way first. It survived, as the both-follow round inside this game.

The mirror won because it uses both fingers at once, the hidden orders create real standoffs, and the reveal gives you something specific to argue about: *there, at nine seconds, you moved first*.

## How it works

**The glass.** In the *facing* seating (the default when the screen is taller than wide) the mirror line runs across the screen; Orange's half is at the bottom, Blue's at the top, and Blue's words are turned to face Blue. Reflection keeps your position along the line, so when you move to your right your partner moves to *their* left, as in a real mirror. In the *side by side* seating (the default on a landscape screen) the line runs down the middle. The seating can be forced on the title screen, and once a round's glass is up it stays fixed, so turning a phone mid-round cannot swap the geometry under the trace. A finger belongs to whichever half it first touches; a second finger in the same half is ignored. Dots follow your finger's movement rather than jumping to it.

**The mirror holds** at a moment when the two dots are within 12% of the half's shorter side of being mirror images (about 47 px, roughly 8 mm, on a full-screen 390 x 844 phone; a little less with browser toolbars showing), *and* each dot has got at least 15% of that side (about 58 px) from where it was 1.5 s ago. The travel rule stops two people keeping the glass whole by wiggling on the spot or by both holding still. The rule only looks backwards, so the live glow and fog show exactly the rule that is scored.

The travel rule has a cost: it is a speed floor. A dot has to get about 39 px a second further away in a straight line, and more on a curve, so a perfect copy moving slower than that never holds the mirror at all. In tests with an exact copy 150 ms late, a zigzag at 30 or 40 px/s kept the glass whole in 0 of 30 rounds, at 50 px/s in 26 of 30, and at 60 px/s in all 30. It also has a blind spot: small circles (radius 25 px, at any tempo from 1.5 to 12 s a turn) and fast circles (1.5 s a turn, any size) never hold, because over 1.5 s they end up close to where they started. So the tip on the title screen is *slow and smooth, but keep travelling, at least a fingertip's width every second*, not just "slow".

**The orders.** Each player's order is the low bit of their own word from `crypto.getRandomValues`, so it is a fair coin independent of the other player's and of every earlier round. Your own order tells you nothing about your partner's.

**The trace.** During the 30 seconds both positions are sampled 50 times a second, in a frame where a perfect mirror means the two dots coincide. Before anything is timed, the pair's *steady offset* is taken off. Over a straight stretch, a follower whose dot sits a steady 25 px ahead of the ring along the path looks exactly like a follower who is early by the time it takes to travel 25 px. Without this step, a slightly misaligned follower would be shown "ahead" for seconds at a time. The offset is the median difference between the dots at the time shift that best explains the round, taken only from the stretches where somebody is plausibly copying somebody: the half-seconds that step 1 below calls coupled when judged on the raw positions, with nothing taken off (a follower within 10% of the side of the ring passes either way), and only while both dots are moving. The version after the first review took the median over every moving moment of the round. In a tug, where two leaders go their own ways for 15 s before one gives way, most of those moments were unrelated motion, the offset came out tens of px wrong, and taking it off made a correct copy look early: on straight-line motion the one who gave way was named the leader in about 2% of such rounds. A median over the coupled stretches still ignores a few seconds of somebody falling behind. Each half-second segment is then judged in two steps:

1. *Is anybody copying anybody?* This is judged on 3 s of motion centred on the segment, because over shorter stretches two people ignoring each other line up by chance too often. Both dots must be moving (an RMS spread of at least 3% of the side), there must be a time shift of under 0.8 s that brings the dots close (RMS distance at most 10% of the side, measured on the real positions, offset included), the dots' velocities must agree at that shift (correlation at least 0.5), and no rival shift that tells a different story may fit nearly as well (within 25%). Otherwise the segment is *still*, *apart* or *too close to call*.
2. *If so, who is ahead right here?* This is judged on the 1 s around the segment, so a moment of leading is credited for about as long as it lasted. If neither dot moved in that second, the segment is *still*. Otherwise the best time shift, refined between samples, is the lag. If the lag is within 40 ms either way, the segment is *together*. Otherwise, whoever's dot gets there first is ahead.

"Ahead" means your dot arrives first: your partner is where you were, some fraction of a second later. **The measured leader** of a round is whoever was ahead for longer, provided it was at least 4 s. Ties or less than 4 s mean nobody led.

**The glass, and the points.** The mirror must hold for at least 10 of the 30 seconds in every scored round. If it does not, the glass breaks: the game ends there, and you both lose, whatever the score. Otherwise, each round, each player:

| | |
|---|---|
| +1 | you did as you were told: LEAD, and the trace names you the leader; FOLLOW, and it names your partner |
| +1 | you guessed your partner's order |

After four rounds the higher total wins.

**Why the orders make a game.** If the orders differ, the round is cooperative: the leader leads, the follower follows, and both keep their orders. If they match, at most one of you can keep yours, because only one of you can be the measured leader. Two followers face a standoff: somebody has to move, and whoever moves first is the one being copied. Two leaders face a tug: somebody has to give way. Either way the broken glass is the reason to give way, and it has a deadline. Neither of you can save the glass unless the dots start travelling together in time:

| orders | nobody gives way | one gives way, the other copies (in time to hold 10 s) |
|---|---|---|
| LEAD / FOLLOW | (no reason not to) | both keep their orders: +1 each |
| FOLLOW / FOLLOW | the glass breaks: nobody wins | the one who moved: 0; the one who waited: +1 |
| LEAD / LEAD | the glass breaks: nobody wins | the one who gave way: 0; the other: +1 (unless the one who gave way used the back door: see the second hole below) |

For a player trying to win, a broken glass is the worst result there is. So once a standoff starts, each of you would rather give way than break the glass, as long as giving way still leaves you any chance to win or draw, and each of you would rather the other gave way. That is a game of chicken, and the deadline is its clock. Nobody can keep their order alone: a LEAD needs someone copying them, and a FOLLOW needs someone to copy. There is also a third way out: falling in *together*, with nobody ahead, saves the glass and gives the order point to neither of you. And a both-lead tug has a back door that undoes the chicken there for a player who knows it: see the second hole below.

This is a change. The first version gave each of you a point whenever the mirror held, and had no shared loss. A point that both players get can never change who is ahead, so for a player trying to win, giving way only ever handed the other player the order point, and waiting was never worse. No per-round scoring can fix that on its own. When only the difference between your scores matters, whatever one of you gains the other loses, and in a symmetric contest like that one choice always does at least as well as the other: there is no chicken. Chicken needs an outcome that is bad for *both* of you, and the broken glass is that outcome. `mirror.test.mjs` works through it. It shows that under the old rules waiting was weakly dominant. Under the glass rule it checks every standing from 6 points behind to 6 ahead, with up to three rounds left, and in both kinds of standoff. A stalemate is worth nothing to either of you. Against a partner who waits, giving way is strictly better whenever it leaves you a chance to win or draw. Against a partner who gives way, waiting is never worse than giving way too, whether giving way together ends with you falling in together or breaking the glass. So neither plain choice is dominant. It also checks the other obvious plans:

- **Always holding still** breaks the glass, since the mirror needs both dots travelling.
- **Always mirroring instantly** (being *together* rather than behind) never gains a told-to-follow player anything: in a lead/follow round both or neither keep their orders, and in a both-follow round copying from behind is what keeps yours. In a both-lead round, being together beats giving way by a point, but only against a partner predictable enough to anticipate, and a partner who wants that point can simply move unpredictably. (The back door below needs no anticipation.)

Two holes remain. The first is in the scoring. Giving way costs you the order point, and at best you win the read point back, so it leaves your margin unchanged at best; a later round can win back at most 2. So a player who can no longer win or draw by giving way has no reason not to stall: in the last round that is anyone behind at all, and in round 3 anyone behind by 3 or more (nobody can be 3 behind before round 3). Only the honour of the table stops them. A test enumerates every score the rules allow and finds the hole there and nowhere else.

The second hole, found in the third review, is in the measurement, and it is bigger. In a both-lead tug, the one who gives way can keep the glass whole without the trace seeing them copy. Then nobody is named leader and neither of you keeps LEAD. There are two easy ways to do it. The mirror's tolerance (12% of the side, 47 px on a phone) is wider than the distance at which the trace stops calling it copying (10%, 39 px), so copying from 40 to 45 px off the ring does it. And copying is judged on 3 s at a time while the glass needs only 10 s in all, so copying on the ring for a second at a time, with a second's excursion of about 100 px (under 2 cm on a phone) in between, does it too. In a test on straight legs, zigzags and wandering, a leader who gives way at 3 s and copies on the ring keeps the glass whole in all 24 rounds, and the other leader is named every time. Copying from 42 px off keeps it whole in 16 of the 24, and a second on, a second off in 20, and in none of those is anyone named. So for a player who knows this, giving way in a tug costs nothing. Whichever of you goes through the back door, the round scores the same for both, so holding out gains nothing against a partner who knows it too, and the tug stops being a game of chicken. The incentive checks above assume that a player who gives way is seen copying; a second test works through what the back door does to them. Nothing in the rules forbids it. In the other rounds the one copying has no reason to hide, since being seen copying is what keeps a follower's point. Shutting it means changing what the glass counts (see *Where it could go next*); for now it is only stated and pinned by tests.

The deadline is real but fuzzy. On my wandering-motion model, with a good follower, a move at 8 s still saves the glass in about 90% of rounds, a move at 14 s in about half, and a move at 17 s in about 1 in 10. How you move changes this a lot. A steady circle at a comfortable pace can be saved from as late as 19 s. A slow random walk (averaging about 53 px/s) survives a move at 8 s about one time in five (218 of 1,000 rounds; 1 in 20 on the test's 20 seeds), because it keeps dipping under the speed floor. From 20 s on, saving the glass is impossible in every model, since no held time is possible before the first move.

**The guess** is the part where you read each other. With orders from fair, independent coins, no rule for guessing that ignores what happened in the round (your own order, earlier reveals, always guessing LEAD) can do better or worse than exactly 1/2. Everything above that comes from watching: someone who moves off at once was probably told to lead; someone who moves off after five seconds of glaring at you was probably a follower who cracked.

**Hidden information, and how hidden it is.** The page keeps the game state inside a closure and draws only from `view(state)`. That function shows a player's order only while that player's card is held, shows only *that* a guess has been made (never which), and keeps the trace's verdict back until both have guessed. In the DOM, a card that is not being held is identical whatever it hides, and the two answer buttons appear in an independently shuffled order each round, so a glimpse of which side someone tapped tells you nothing.

A held card shows its order, so its owner cups a hand around it. What the partner can still see is the card's glowing outline, so that outline must not depend on the order. In the first version it did: the card shrink-wrapped its text, so a held LEAD card was 231 px wide and a FOLLOW card 210 px, readable past a hand at a glance. On a phone forced side by side, the LEAD line also wrapped onto an extra line, making the card taller. Now the card has a fixed size, each order is one word and exactly two lines that never wrap, and the text shrinks with a narrow card instead of wrapping. `check.mjs` holds each player's card with each order at four sizes (390 and 320 px phones, a phone forced side by side, and a desktop) and requires the same card box, the same line boxes, no overflow and the same transitions. It also takes a screenshot of the whole screen with the card's letters made transparent and requires it to be pixel-identical for LEAD and FOLLOW. The letters themselves (LEAD is shorter than FOLLOW) are what your hand is for.

It is still a table game, though: nothing stops you looking at your partner's card when they press it, or reaching over and pressing it yourself. If you do it before they have read their card, it will already say "Read ✓" when they come to it, which they will notice. Once they have read it, either card can be held again without leaving any trace. Anyone with browser developer tools can of course read anything.

**The conversation it is built to start.** The reveal names the situation in one sentence, for example "*You were both told to follow. Somebody had to go first, and it was Orange. And each of you guessed the other had been told to lead.*", and then hands you a scrubber. (That last sentence is about the guesses, so it says so. An earlier wording, "each of you thought the other was leading", appeared even in a round where nobody moved at all.) The summary asks its last question out loud and makes you answer before it shows the bar: *which of you usually leads?* My guess is that the interesting arguments are not about the points but about the half-second strip: whether a move was a lead or an anticipation, and whether the trace saying "together" matches what it felt like.

**Sound.** Each dot sings a sine tone whose pitch is set by its position along the mirror line, across one octave from 220 Hz. Mirror images sing in unison. Drift *along* the line makes the two tones beat against each other, so you can hear that kind of drift before you see it. Drift toward or away from the line is silent: two dots 200 px apart in that direction sing the same note. There are soft clicks on the countdown. Audio starts only after a tap or click, can be turned off on the title screen, and is suspended when the tab is hidden.

**Reduced motion** stops the pulsing start marks and button animations. **Colour schemes** follow the system (warm paper by day, dark glass by night). **Keyboard**: W A S D and the arrow keys steer (with easing), the cards can be held with Space or Enter, and the reveal's strip can be scrubbed with the arrow keys.

**Files.** `mirror.js` holds the rules, the measurement and the state machine: pure, no DOM, no clock, no randomness of its own, and loadable as a plain script or with `require`. `app.js` holds the glass, the input, the sound and the screens. `mirror.test.mjs` tests the rules and the measurement (41 tests). `check.mjs` is a headless two-player playthrough. Nothing is stored except your seating and sound preferences, and nothing is sent anywhere.

## Honest assessment

- **[Certain] No two humans have played this.** I can't touch a screen, sit across a table, or hear the tones. Every threshold (the 47 px mirror tolerance, the 1.5 s travel rule, 4 s to count as leader, 40 ms for "together", the 10 s needed to keep the glass whole) was tuned against synthetic motion and judgement, not people. The whole premise (that a both-follow round produces a standoff, that the reveal starts an argument) is a prediction.
- **[Certain] The rules are as described.** `mirror.test.mjs` enumerates every combination of orders, guesses, leader and held (scores, the broken glass, symmetry, and "at most one keeps their order when the orders match"). It plays a game in which Orange is ahead when the glass breaks and checks that nobody wins. It checks dealing over every low-bit pattern (four outcomes, each equally likely, each player's order independent of the other's), and all 256 guessing rules that use only your own order and the previous round's reveal (each scores exactly 1/2). A breadth-first search over all 1,162 reachable states, including games where the glass breaks, finds every phase and round reachable, no dead ends, the title and summary reachable from everywhere, refused actions changing nothing, and no action mutating its input. The search leaves out history and totals, which no transition depends on. In every reachable state before the reveal, the view is byte-identical across all four order assignments, except a held card's own order, and identical whichever answer an answered player gave. A source check confirms that `app.js` reads nothing off the game state but its phase.
- **[Certain] The page hides what the rules hide, and two simultaneous fingers play it.** `check.mjs` opens the page from `file://` at phone size with the order dice rigged from outside the page. The deal's DOM is identical for all four order combinations, depends only on Orange's order while Orange holds the card, and is identical again once it is released. The held card's outline, line boxes and pixels (letters aside) are identical for LEAD and FOLLOW at four sizes. A full scored round is played with two simultaneous CDP touch points in two tabs at once (LEAD/FOLLOW and FOLLOW/FOLLOW), Orange tracing a slow path and Blue copying 300 ms behind. The guess-phase DOM is identical across the two tabs even though Orange answered differently. The reveals name Orange as leader, measure the lag at about 0.3 s, keep the glass whole, award 2/2 and 0/1 as the rules say, and tell the two followers that each guessed the other had been told to lead. A keyboard-only practice round on the desktop also names Orange. A whole game runs from practice through four rounds to the summary and back to the title, with both players steering on the keys under a fake clock, and keeps the glass whole each round. A game in which nobody moves breaks the glass in round 1 and ends with "Nobody wins". All 150 checks pass in light and dark, with reduced motion, without console errors, and without sideways scrolling at 390 px. `tools/smoke.mjs` passes at desktop and phone sizes.
- **[Certain] On synthetic motion the measurement is right.** Exact delayed copies are attributed to the right player, with the lag recovered within 15 ms, from 60 to 750 ms of delay. Copies within 30 ms read as *together*, and a lag of 1.1 s, beyond the 0.8 s search, is credited to nobody. Simulated followers who steer toward the leader with a reaction delay and hand tremor are named followers in 200 of 200 rounds. Swapping the players swaps every judgement bit for bit, and zooming or shifting the picture changes nothing. A smooth bump of leading, 1 to 8 s long, is credited within a second of its true length. Taking turns shows up as turns.
- **[Certain] A steady offset is not a lead; an offset that changes still can be.** Before the review, a copy 150 ms late with its dot 25 px to one side was shown "ahead" for about 7 s of the 30 on average (up to 14 s) on straight-ish motion (zigzags, random walks), and about 3 s on circles. On reaching movements it was under 1 s. Now the steady offset is taken off first. On four motion families with offsets of 15, 25 and 35 px, the copier is never shown ahead; a test pins this. It also holds when the round starts with up to 17 s of both hands still, and, since the second review, when it starts with up to 17 s of two leaders going their own ways: in a test of 154 such tugs that kept the glass whole (straight legs, zigzags and wandering; give-way at 13, 15 or 17 s; exact copies, one of them sitting 20 px off the ring, and a steering follower), the one who kept leading is always named leader, the offset found is within 10 px of the true one, and the one who gave way is shown ahead for 0.2 s on average. In my separate runs of 2,033 and 613 such rounds (other generators, including Lissajous figures and reaching movements, give-way as late as 19.5 s, some followers sitting 20 px off the ring) and in the reviewer's 1,510 straight-leg rounds, the wrong leader was named in none; the version after the first review got 7, 8 and 42 of those wrong. For a follower on or near the ring (on it, or 10 px off it, in the test), adding a constant offset of up to 20 px changes no half-second's verdict and no leader, but not bit for bit any more: the offset is estimated from stretches judged on the real positions, so a stretch near the closeness limit can drop out, which moves the estimate by under a pixel and a lag by a few ms (43 ms at worst in the test). That is as far as it goes. Closeness is judged on the real positions, so an offset that carries the dots past the closeness limit (10% of the side, 39 px on a phone) turns those stretches to *apart*. An exact copy 150 ms late on straight legs at 70 px/s is credited to its leader for all 30 s at 20 px off the ring, and is *apart* for all 30 s at 40 px off, with nobody named leader, while the mirror, whose tolerance is 47 px, still holds for 43% to 65% of the round; a test pins six such rounds. Where the first verdicts change depends on the follower. With my exact copies and smooth followers, none changed until the follower already sat 20 px off before the push; in the reviewer's runs, a steering follower already 15 px off changed a verdict in 2 rounds of 100, and one 17 px off in 36. That gap between the two tolerances is one way through the tug's back door (the second hole, above). What is not removed is an offset that changes during the round. If the dot sits 25 px one way for 15 s and then 25 px the other way, the copier is shown ahead for 3 to 6 s. If the offset slowly turns through a full circle, it is 3 to 8 s, as bad as before.
- **[Certain] It can find a leader where there was none, and repeated shapes are where it happens.** Two people ignoring each other produced a false leader in 3 of the test's 400 rounds on my smooth wandering model, and in 5 of another 400. On other independent motion I tried (random walks, zigzags, reaching movements), it was at most 2 in 400. Two people each repeating their own shape are much worse. For circles it was 8% to 9% of rounds (32 and 36 of 400, in two runs on different seeds), and 22% to 27% (88 and 108 of 400) if they circle the same way round at a similar tempo (3 to 5 s a turn). Figure-eights were a little worse: 11% (43 of 400), and 30% (119 of 400) the same way round. Two people repeating a shape at the same tempo look, to any timing measurement, like one copying the other a fraction of a turn later, so this is not something the measurement can fix. A test pins the circle figures.
- **[Certain] The trace measures timing, not intent.** A follower who predicts a regular pattern (a steady circle) and runs 150 ms ahead of it is credited as the leader for nearly the whole round; a test pins this. I think that is a true statement about leading, and the game is built around it, but it means a FOLLOW can lose their order point by being too good, and a LEAD in a tug can win by anticipating.
- **[Likely] Real followers fall in the range the measurement handles.** People copying a slow, unpredictable movement should trail by roughly 0.15 to 0.5 s, inside the 0.8 s window. I have no measurement of real finger motion on a phone, including how much touch noise or occlusion by your own finger changes it.
- **[Likely] The mirror is holdable at a moderate pace, impossible when too slow and hard when fast.** At about 60 to 90 px/s on a phone, a simulated 0.2 s follower holds it for a median of 60% or more of the round. A follower 0.3 s behind a leader moving at 200 px/s is 60 px off, outside the 47 px tolerance. Below about 40 px/s, the travel rule means even a perfect copy never holds it. Both limits may be wrong for real fingers, and with the glass rule a wrong limit ends the game, not just a point.
- **[Certain] On paper, a both-follow standoff is a game of chicken for a player trying to win, and so is a both-lead tug, unless the one who gives way uses its back door.** The checks are described above. They are exact given the rules and fair-coin guesses. The modelling assumptions are how later rounds go (fair-coin orders, and in a same-order round either of you equally likely to keep yours), which only sets how much a point is worth, not which way the comparisons go, and reaches exactly the margins the rules allow; and that a player who gives way is seen copying. Both holes are also above. In the first, a player who can no longer win or draw by giving way (in the last round, any deficit; in round 3, a deficit of 3 or more) has nothing to lose by stalling. The second breaks the last assumption: in a tug, the one who gives way can keep the glass whole without being seen copying, by copying from 40 to 45 px off the ring or a second at a time, and then nobody keeps LEAD, giving way costs nothing and holding out gains nothing. Two tests pin it, one by playing it and one by working through the incentives.
- **[Speculative] The glass may be too harsh.** One round in which two beginners cannot hold the mirror for 10 s ends the game for both. That is what gives a standoff its teeth, but if it ends many first games in round 1, a glass that can take one crack might play better. It would also make the first standoff of a game less sharp.
- **[Speculative] The both-follow round is the heart of it.** I expect a few seconds of glaring, a twitch, and then an argument about who twitched. It could equally be a dull 30 seconds of two people politely waiting. The broken glass is meant to prevent that, and it only works if players care about not losing together.
- **[Speculative] The tones help.** Beating between two near-unison sines is a real and very audible effect, but I have not heard these, and on a phone speaker at 220 Hz they may be faint.
- Rough edges: the honour system carries real weight (no peeking, no talking, no telling). The guess point is probably easy when both play straight, since a partner who moves off at once was usually told to lead. One person with two hands can play both sides, which makes the hidden orders meaningless. The finger covers your own dot, which is why the halo is large. Turning a phone mid-round keeps the seating but squashes the halves. Keyboard steering is coarse and following on keys lags more than following by finger. In the practice round, "nobody was leading" counts as matching the trace whenever the trace names no leader, which includes a round where one of you was ahead for 3.5 s.

## Where it could go next

- **Shut the tug's back door.** Count only the held moments in stretches where the trace sees somebody copying (or the two of you together), so that saving the glass in a tug means being seen to give way. The catch is that the trace judges copying on 3 s centred on the moment, so the live glow could no longer show exactly the scored rule. Widening the closeness limit to the mirror's 47 px would shut only the steady version: in my runs of the test's rounds with a 12% limit, the 42 px copiers are named followers in all 16 glass-whole rounds, but a second on and a second off still names nobody in all 20.
- **A glass that cracks first.** If ending the game on the first failed round is too harsh in practice, let the glass take one crack.
- **A free round.** No orders, three minutes, and the only score is the longest stretch the trace calls *together*: a direct attempt at the co-confident state from the mirror-game research.
- **A third person.** One spectator watches and guesses who is leading, as in the circle game where someone in the middle must spot who starts each movement.
- **One dimension.** Two sliders on parallel tracks, as in the original experiment. The measurement would be cleaner, and the result would be comparable to the published data.
- **Replays worth keeping.** Export a round as a small animated drawing of both paths, coloured by who was ahead: a picture of a conversation with no words in it.
- **Real people.** The honest next step is to watch ten pairs play and find out whether the reveal starts the argument I think it does, and whether the thresholds survive real fingers.

## Revision note

An independent reviewer rebuilt the checks in separate code. It confirmed the rules engine, the hidden view, the timing measurement on its own motion families and the author's figures. It also found real problems, all now fixed:

- **The held card gave away the order.** It shrink-wrapped its text, so LEAD was 231 px wide and FOLLOW 210 px, visible past a cupped hand. It now has a fixed size and always shows one word and two unwrapped lines. `check.mjs` measures the held card's box, line boxes and transitions, and compares letters-transparent screenshots for LEAD and FOLLOW at four sizes. The old page fails that check.
- **The moment-by-moment strip read a steady offset as leading.** A copier 25 px to one side was shown ahead for about 7 s of the round. The pair's steady offset is now taken off before anything is timed, and tests pin the offset copies. An offset that changes during the round is still a limitation, now stated.
- **The scoring did not make the standoff a game of chicken.** The shared "mirror held" point never changed who was ahead, so waiting was weakly dominant for a player trying to win. The mirror is now a shared stake instead: if it fails, the glass breaks and nobody wins. Points are only the order point and the read point, and the tests work through the incentives, including where the scoring still has a hole.
- **"Under 5% false leaders, the worst case" was wrong for repeated shapes.** For circles it is 8% to 9%, or 22% to 27% for same-way circles at a similar tempo, measured and pinned; figure-eights are a little worse.
- **The "Slow is easier" tip ran into the travel rule's speed floor.** The tip now says to keep travelling. The floor and the small- and fast-circle blind spot are documented and tested.
- **Smaller fixes:**
  - The deadline figures now say how much they depend on the motion.
  - The "each of you thought the other was leading" line now says it is about the guesses.
  - The README now says re-reading a card after its owner has read it leaves no trace.
  - The sound claim now covers only drift along the line.
  - 47 px is about 8 mm, not 7.

A second review checked those fixes in its own code. It confirmed the rest, and found that two of them had brought in new problems, now fixed:

- **The steady-offset fix broke late tugs.** The offset was a median over every moving moment of the round, so when two leaders went their own ways for 13 to 17 s before one gave way, it came out tens of px wrong. Taking it off then made a correct copy look early, and the one who gave way could be named the leader and keep the LEAD order point: 31 of the reviewer's 1,510 straight-leg tugs, where the original code had none. The offset is now taken only from the stretches the measurement judges coupled on the raw positions. A new test plays 216 late tugs on three motion families, 154 of which keep the glass whole (the old estimate fails it on the offset, on the leader and on the time the one who gave way is shown ahead), and the reviewer's 1,510 rounds now have no wrong leader. The cost is that adding a constant offset to one player no longer leaves every lag identical to the bit. For a follower on or near the ring it still changes no verdict and no leader, which is what the rewritten test checks. (This sentence first said so without "on or near the ring"; the third review caught that.)
- **The scoring hole was mis-scoped.** The README said only the last round had a hole. A player 3 or more behind in round 3 can no longer win or draw by giving way either. The README now says so, the incentive test's model of later rounds now reaches the swings of up to 2 points that the rules allow (it had allowed only 1), and a new test enumerates every score the rules allow and finds the hole in round 3 (3 or 4 behind) and round 4 (any deficit) and nowhere else.

A third review checked those fixes in its own code and confirmed them. It found one sentence that still claimed too much, now fixed, and reproducing it turned up a bigger problem, now stated and pinned by tests but not fixed:

- **"A constant offset of up to 20 px changes no verdict" held only near the ring.** Closeness is judged on the real positions, so a follower already 15 to 20 px off the ring and pushed 20 px further comes up against the 39 px closeness limit, and those stretches turn *apart*: in one of the reviewer's cases every half-second, with no leader, while the glass stayed whole. The sentence now says it holds for a follower on or near the ring and what happens past the limit. The test now also pushes followers who already sit 10 px off, and plays six rounds in which a copy 40 px off is *apart* throughout, with nobody named, while the mirror holds for 43% to 65% of the round.
- **A tug has a back door.** That case is a game problem, not just a measurement one. Because the mirror's tolerance (47 px) is wider than the closeness limit (39 px), and because copying is judged on 3 s while the glass needs only 10 s in all, the one who gives way in a both-lead tug can keep the glass whole without being seen copying: by copying from 40 to 45 px off the ring, or on the ring a second at a time. Then nobody keeps LEAD, giving way costs nothing, and for a player who knows it the tug is not a game of chicken. The README's claims about the standoff now say so. Two new tests pin it: one plays both ways of doing it on three motion families, and one adds the back door to the incentive model and shows that it is never worse than giving way in plain sight and scores the same whoever uses it. Shutting it means changing what the glass counts, so it is listed under *Where it could go next*.

A last check found two figures that went further than the measurements, and the coordinator corrected them by hand after re-measuring:

- **The false-leader bullet said circles are where it happens.** Any shape both players repeat at a similar tempo does it. Figure-eights were measured at 11%, or 30% the same way round, and the bullet now says repeated shapes. A second run of the circle measurement on other seeds gave 9% and 27%, so the circle figures are now given as ranges.
- **The slow random walk's speed and survival rate were off.** It averages about 53 px/s, not 60, and it survives a move at 8 s in 218 of 1,000 rounds, about one in five, not "one in five or less".
