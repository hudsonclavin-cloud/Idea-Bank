# The Extinction Debt
> Clear a quarter of a model forest and no species dies. Its best competitor is doomed anyway, and the model says when.

**Kind:** simulation
**Run:** open `ideas/44-extinction-debt/index.html` in a browser (plain classic scripts, so it works straight from disk or any static server; no build, no network). Tests, from the repo root: `node --test ideas/44-extinction-debt/model.test.mjs` (Node 22, about 35 s). The stochastic-forest statistics below come from `node ideas/44-extinction-debt/ensemble.mjs` (about 2 minutes per clearing level).
**Made by:** Ledgerwood

## What it is

A square of 10,000 forest sites and ten tree species that share it. Every species is ranked: a better competitor always takes a site from a worse one, and in exchange the worse competitor spreads faster. That one trade-off lets all ten coexist at a stable mix. The rates are set, as in Tilman and colleagues' papers, so that the best competitor (#1) is also the commonest tree; the trade-off does not require that, and it can produce any order of abundance (tested below).

You have a bulldozer. Drag on the map to clear land (or set a percentage and let it fall at random), and watch. The first thing you will notice is that nothing happens. The trees on the cleared land are gone, but the forest that is left looks the same, and every species is still in it. But the **ledger** on the right already says *doomed* next to #1, with a year, because the mathematics has already decided: once more than 20 % of the land is gone (its own undisturbed share), the best competitor cannot hold on, and its share slides toward zero along an exact logistic curve with a time constant of centuries. The number at the top, the **extinction debt**, counts species that are doomed and still standing.

Then you can wait (10, 50 or 200 years per second), or give the land back. Restore enough land while the species still stands and *doomed* flips to *safe* and the share climbs again. Restore it a year after the last tree and nothing comes back, because in this model there is no outside. And once a species is gone, more land is not always kinder: with #1 gone, nothing holds #2 in check, and giving back land until less than 20 % is cleared lets #2 crowd out #3. The ledger forecasts that extinction too.

Around the map: a chart of every species' share over time with the model's forecast drawn dashed beyond the present; a gauge of cleared land with each species' threshold marked on it; an event log that records what you did and when each species died, and how long it had been doomed; and a short, live derivation of the thresholds and the decline rate. Settings let you change the mean lifetime (which only rescales years), the dominance parameter *q* and the number of species.

## Why I made it

I wanted something that could be proved and something that could only be felt, and extinction debt is both at once. The provable part is a short calculation with a sharp answer: for this model, with every species standing, species *k* survives if and only if less than 1 − (1−*q*)^(2*k*−1) of the land is cleared (apart from isolated knife-edge values, below). The felt part is the silence after the bulldozer. I know the theorem, and I still find it eerie to clear 30 % of the forest, see ten species where there were ten, and read *doomed, gone by about year 2,560*.

Three things in the model are the opposite of what I would have guessed. The species that goes first is the best competitor. With these rates it is also the commonest one, not the rarest, but that part is a parameter choice (Tilman's); the part that is not a choice is why it goes. It goes because of what it is good at: being the best competitor means barely out-spreading your own death rate, so you need almost all the land to break even, and it is doomed as soon as the cleared share exceeds its own undisturbed share, whatever that share is. And the better the clearing is for the trees you kept, the longer the dead species takes to vanish: cut just past the threshold and every species can still be standing thousands of years later, though #1 stops being the commonest within about a century.

Before choosing I listed candidates: why there are no giant insects (the mechanism is genuinely contested, and I didn't want to pretend otherwise), Kleiber's law and heartbeats (everyone's first idea), Rosenzweig–MacArthur and the paradox of enrichment (canonical textbook material), Fisher's sex ratio with Hamilton's local mate competition, the marginal value theorem as a foraging game, the selection shadow behind ageing, and this one. I picked extinction debt because the sharp result, the counterintuitive ordering, the real conservation stakes and the "nothing happens" moment all come from the same four lines of algebra.

## How it works

**Lineage.** One species on an infinite set of identical sites is Levins's metapopulation model (Levins 1969, *Bull. Entomol. Soc. Am.* 15:237–240): d*p*/d*t* = *cp*(1−*p*) − *mp*. Nee & May (1992, *J. Anim. Ecol.* 61:37–40) added a second, dominant species and habitat destruction. Tilman (1994, *Ecology* 75:2–16) extended it to a ranked community of any size, and Tilman, May, Lehman & Nowak (1994, *Nature* 371:65–66) added destruction to that, found the ordered, delayed extinctions and named the debt. This page is their model with a stochastic forest in front of it.

**The equations.** A site is cleared (fraction *D*), empty, or held by one individual of species *i* ∈ 1..*S*, ranked 1 = best competitor. Species *i* sends propagules to every site at rate *c_i p_i*; a propagule takes any site held by a worse competitor and dies on a better one; every individual dies at rate *m*. With *p_i* the share of **all** sites (cleared ones included),

d*p_i*/d*t* = *c_i p_i* (1 − *D* − Σ_{*j*≤*i*} *p_j*) − *m p_i* − *p_i* Σ_{*j*<*i*} *c_j p_j*.

Because species *i* is affected only by better competitors, the equilibrium solves top-down:

*p_i** = 1 − *D* − *m*/*c_i* − Σ_{*j*<*i*, present} *p_j** (1 + *c_j*/*c_i*), and species *i* is absent when this is ≤ 0.

The right-hand side with *p_i* → 0 is exactly species *i*'s invasion growth rate divided by *c_i*, so the sign of the formula decides presence (checked numerically in the tests). "Present" means two things: a positive equilibrium, and still standing in the forest. There is no outside source of propagules, so a species that has died out stays out, and the sum must skip it. That matters: lose rank *j* with everyone else standing, and rank *j*+2 can then persist only while more than *j*'s threshold stays cleared, because rank *j*+1 has nobody holding it down (tested for every *j*, and the masked equilibrium checked against long ODE runs for random sets of survivors).

**Parameters.** The trade-off is the geometric one: *c_i* = *m*/(1−*q*)^(2*i*−1), with *q* the share of the best competitor. I derived (and the tests confirm to 1e-12) that this gives undisturbed shares *q*, *q*(1−*q*), *q*(1−*q*)², …, so the best competitor is the commonest species and abundance falls geometrically down the ranks; bare ground is (1−*q*)^*S*. That ordering is chosen, not implied: inverting the equilibrium formula, the reversed abundances (#1 the rarest, at 2.7 %) come from rates *c*/*m* = 1.03, 1.09, 1.19 … 30.3, still increasing down the ranks, and #1 is then doomed from 2.7 % cleared (tested). What holds for any rates is that #1 is doomed once *D* exceeds its own undisturbed share 1 − *m*/*c*₁. I believe this is the parameterisation of Tilman (1994) and Tilman et al. (1994, 1997); I could not reach the papers from this sandbox, so take the attribution as [Likely] and the formulas as proven here. Defaults: *S* = 10, *q* = 0.2, so the shares are 20.0, 16.0, 12.8, 10.2, 8.2, 6.6, 5.2, 4.2, 3.4, 2.7 % with 10.7 % bare ground, and the colonisation rates run from 1.25 *m* (#1) to 69.4 *m* (#10).

Time inside the model is measured in mean lifetimes (1/*m*). The page converts to years with a lifetime you can set; the default of 50 years corresponds to the roughly 2 % per year stem mortality that Condit, Hubbell & Foster (1995, *Ecol. Monogr.* 65(4)) measured forest-wide in the Barro Colorado Island 50-ha plot outside the 1983 drought interval. Changing the lifetime rescales every rate together, so it changes how many calendar years a story takes and nothing else.

**What the algebra says (all tested).**
- *Thresholds.* With every species standing, species *k* persists iff *D* < *D_k* = 1 − (1−*q*)^(2*k*−1), except at the knife-edge points below. For *q* = 0.2: 20.0, 48.8, 67.2, 79.0, 86.6, 91.4, 94.5, 96.5, 97.7, 98.6 %. Extinctions come in rank order, best competitor first, and the gaps between thresholds shrink down the list, which is Tilman et al.'s "the more fragmented a habitat already is, the greater the number of extinctions caused by added destruction". Only the amount cleared matters, never its shape, because propagules reach every site equally; but the history matters, because losses move the thresholds (above).
- *Strict best-first order is a property of these exact rates, not of the model class.* The geometric trade-off puts every odd rank's equilibrium to zero at exactly *D* = *q* (next bullet), so a tiny generic change in the rates decides which of them goes first. Nudging every *c_i* at random by up to ±0.1 % or ±1 %, #1 was the first species lost in only 57 and 54 of 200 draws; in the rest an odd rank (#3, #5, #7 or #9) drops out first, in a window below #1's threshold, and the stretch of *D* over which the extinct set is out of rank order grows with the nudge (median 0.5 percentage points at ±0.1 %, 5.5 at ±1 %; tested). Klausmeier (1998, *Am. Nat.* 152:303–310) showed that with three or more species a species other than the best competitor can be the first to go; I know that result from papers citing it.
- *The best competitor's decline is exactly logistic.* Nothing displaces #1, so d*p*/d*t* = *p*(*r* − *c*₁*p*) with *r* = *c*₁(1−*D*) − *m* = *m*(*q*−*D*)/(1−*q*). At a 50-year lifetime the time constant 1/|*r*| is 800 years when 25 % is cleared, 400 at 30 %, 200 at 40 %, 133 at 50 %.
- *Release.* Below the first threshold the runner-up gains land as #1 loses it: *p*₂* rises with slope (1−*q*)². At 10 % cleared, #2's share goes from 16.0 % to 22.4 %.
- *A parity knife-edge.* For *D* ≤ *q*, every odd rank falls in step with the leader, *p*_{2*k*+1}* = (1−*q*)^(2*k*) (*q* − *D*), while every even rank gains, *p*_{2*k*+2}* = *q*(1−*q*)^(2*k*+1) + *D*(1−*q*)^(2*k*+2). So at exactly *D* = *q* all the odd ranks are at zero at once ({1, 3, 5, 7, 9}), and the same alternation repeats at every later threshold (at 48.8 %: {1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10}). A hair either side they recover. At the knife-edge itself the long-run share really is zero, but it is approached algebraically (#1's share times *t* tends to 1/*c*₁, and the other odd ranks keep a fixed ratio to it), so the ledger calls those ranks *knife-edge*, not *doomed*, and leaves them out of the debt. In a finite forest the odd ranks settle at very small populations whenever the land is held near a threshold (at 19 % cleared: 64, 41, 26 and 17 of 10,000 sites for #3, #5, #7, #9), where chance can finish them. I did not expect the alternation, but it is not new: work on Tilman's model reports that below the first threshold odd-ranked competitors decline while even-ranked ones gain, and Nakagiri et al. (2010, *Ecol. Inform.* 5:241–247) describe a related odd–even "parity law" under habitat destruction. I found these through abstracts and search snippets only [Likely].

**The forest.** The map is a 100 × 100 lattice stepped synchronously. Each step, every site draws one random number: an empty site is colonised by species *i* with probability *c_i p_i* d*t*; an occupied site dies with probability *m* d*t* or is taken by a better competitor *j* with probability *c_j p_j* d*t*; cleared sites do nothing. d*t* is chosen each step so the largest per-site probability is 5 %. Using the shares at the start of the step, this is an explicit Euler step of the ODE in expectation, so the lattice and the equations agree up to demographic noise of order √(*p*/*N*). Clearing kills whatever stands on the cleared sites, so every species drops by the fraction *D* at the moment of the cut, and the slow part begins from there.

**The forecast.** Whenever the land changes, a species dies out, and once a second while running, the page integrates the ODE with RK4 from the lattice's current shares. A species is *doomed* if its equilibrium, solved over the species still standing, is zero at the current *D* (and not merely at a knife-edge point); its "gone by" year is when the forecast falls below one individual in 10,000, and its "save it" range is where that equilibrium is positive, found by scanning *D* and bisecting each edge. After a loss that range can have a floor as well as a ceiling, and the gauge shows floors as ‹#*k*. *Precarious* means the model leaves it fewer than ten sites. The dashed lines on the chart are that forecast.

## Honest assessment

What works:
- [Certain] Every formula on the page is derived in this README and checked numerically: equilibria (over the species still standing), thresholds and their knife-edge exceptions, ordered extinction for these rates, the logistic decline of #1, the release of #2, the parity structure, and the agreement between the stochastic forest and the equations.
- [Certain] The two headline claims of Tilman et al. (1994) that the page dramatises, extinction in order from best to worst competitor and a time-delayed but deterministic loss of the dominant competitor after moderate destruction, are what their abstract says and what this model does with their geometric rates. The first is exact only for those rates (see *How it works*).
- [Likely] The "nothing happens" moment lands. The debt counter, the *doomed* badge with a year, and the event log's "it had been doomed since year 11" are the felt part, and the chart's dashed future makes the delay visible rather than asserted.
- [Certain] Restoration works as the model says: a doomed species still standing recovers when *D* drops below its threshold, a species that is gone stays gone, and after a loss giving land back can doom another species, which the ledger forecasts and the event log attributes correctly.

What's rough:
- [Certain] "Gone by" overstates the wait near a threshold. It is the year the deterministic forecast drops below one tree in 10,000; a real population lingering at a dozen trees for centuries dies sooner by chance. At 30 % cleared, the 127 of 200 forests that lost #1 within 2,000 years did so 22–58 % earlier than the forecast; at 40 % cleared the forecast was closer (8 forests: mean 1,200 vs 1,324; 200 forests: median 1,085, range 694–1,982). The page says "about" and explains this in the proof box, but the badge still shows a single year.
- [Certain] Finite-size chance kills species the model does not doom, usually #10, then #9 or #8 (at 40 % cleared they settle at 150–340 sites), mostly in the slow reshuffle after a cut, in 14–42 % of forests (27, 52, 63 and 83 of 200 at 19, 25, 30 and 40 % cleared). The event log says so when it happens ("the model did not doom it"), but a reader who cleared 40 % and lost two species will reasonably feel the ledger under-promised. A bigger lattice would reduce this; 100 × 100 keeps phones smooth.
- [Certain] The time scale for trees is long. With a 50-year lifetime the first payment comes 900–4,700 years after the cut, which is the model's honest answer, not a tuned one; the lifetime slider is there for grasslands and insects, where the same curves play out in decades. Tilman et al. themselves wrote in generations. Empirical debts in plants are typically measured over decades to a century (Kuussaari et al. 2009, *Trends Ecol. Evol.* 24:564–571); I take Helm, Hanski & Pärtel (2006, *Ecol. Lett.* 9:72–77), who found that today's plant richness in Estonian alvar grasslands tracks the grassland area of the 1930s rather than today's, as the classic field example, from memory [Likely].
- [Certain] The map's colours are generated per rank (lightness by rank, hue alternating by parity) and were validated for adjacent-pair separation at ten species; with more species neighbouring ranks get closer in colour, and the ledger's numbers are the reliable identity.
- [Certain] Global dispersal means the shape of a clearing does not matter here, and the map looks like confetti rather than a forest. Both are honest consequences of the model, and the page says so, but a reader who draws a careful corridor and sees it make no difference is being told something about the model, not about forests. Local dispersal would change the numbers and is the first thing I would add.
- [Certain] What's simplified: identical sites, one individual per site, strict competitive hierarchy, equal mortality for all species, no seed bank, no immigration, no age structure, destruction as an instant and permanent removal of sites. Tilman, Lehman & Yin (1997, *Am. Nat.* 149:407–435, "Habitat destruction, dispersal, and deterministic extinction in competitive communities") report, across many variants, that the poorest dispersers (which may be the best competitors and the most abundant species) can be among the first driven extinct; that is weaker than the strict best-first order this page shows, and I have not reproduced the paper.
- [Likely] The competition–colonisation trade-off is a weak description of real forests, and the tagline's "best competitor" is a model role, not a field observation. The model lets a seed take a site from an established adult of a worse competitor the moment it lands, which seedlings do not do to trees. McCarthy, Lindenmayer & Drechsler (1997, *Conserv. Biol.* 11:221–226) single out the two assumptions the headline rests on, that the best competitors are the poorest dispersers and the most abundant species, as questionable, and report that in fragmented systems it is mostly poor competitors and rare species that are lost. Muller-Landau (2010, *PNAS* 107:4242–4247) finds the evidence in plants inconsistent with the strong competitive asymmetry the trade-off needs. I have read those abstracts, not the papers.
- [Likely] The attribution of the geometric parameterisation *c_i* = *m*/(1−*q*)^(2*i*−1) to the original papers is from memory; the formulas and their consequences are independent of who wrote them first.
- [Certain] The parity knife-edge and the strict best-first order are both artefacts of the geometric parameterisation, the same non-genericity seen twice (tested above). I would not lean on either as a biological prediction.

## Where it could go next

- **Local dispersal.** Propagules landing only on neighbouring sites would make clearings' shapes matter, slow everything down, and let a corridor do real work. The ODE forecast would then be only an approximation, which is itself worth showing.
- **Unequal mortality** and a looser hierarchy (a better competitor wins only with some probability), to reproduce Tilman, Lehman & Yin's robustness results in the browser.
- **Immigration from outside**, a trickle of propagules of every species, which turns permanent extinction into a long absence and makes restoration a race against a different clock.
- **A "fragments" mode**: several small forests instead of one, each with its own lattice, to show how one of Diamond's relaxation estimates (1972, *PNAS* 69:3199–3203: thousands of years for land-bridge islands) arises from the same mathematics.
- **Sound.** A tone per species whose volume follows its share. The debt would then be a chord that very slowly loses its root.

## Revision note

An independent reviewer rederived every formula and closed-form number and reproduced them all. It also found real problems, all now fixed:

- **The ledger forgot who had died.** The forecast solved the equilibrium over the full species pool, including species already extinct on the lattice. After #1 died and the land was restored below 20 %, the page called #3 *safe*; when #3 then died, as the model predicts, the log blamed chance. The equilibrium is now taken over the species still standing. The "save it" ranges, the gauge ticks (now with floors, ‹#*k*), the verdict and the log all follow from that, and restoration-triggered and knock-on extinctions are forecast and attributed correctly.
- **"The commonest tree for most of that time" was false.** Faster colonisers overtake #1 within 70–120 years, under 8 % of its decline. The prose now says so.
- **The knife-edge was shown as debt.** At exactly *D* = *q* (a reachable slider stop), the page showed a debt of 5 with self-contradictory remedies. Isolated zeros are now *knife-edge*, outside the debt, and the "if and only if" statement now names its exceptions.
- **Best-first order and "the commonest tree" were parameter choices presented as results.** The tagline went from "Clear part of a forest and nothing dies. The commonest tree is already doomed, and the model says when." to "Clear a quarter of a model forest and no species dies. Its best competitor is doomed anyway, and the model says when." The README and page now say what is a choice. They cite Klausmeier (1998), the parity literature and the critiques of the trade-off.
- **Smaller fixes:** "briefly thin" became "settle thin"; "a third" became "30 %" (a third gives about 1,950 years); and the lattice and ensemble numbers are now from 8 seeds and 200 forests per level, with the ensemble script in the folder.
