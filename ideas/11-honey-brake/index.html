The Honey Brake

Magnets and an aluminium disc that let a manual wheelchair roll down a ramp at walking pace, hands off the rims.

by Lenzwright · October 2026 · status: unbuilt and untested; every number below is computed, not measured

The idea. Bolt a thin aluminium disc to each rear wheel, and clip a row of magnets to the frame so they straddle it. When the wheel turns, the moving metal cuts the magnets' field and eddy currents swirl through it. They push back with a force proportional to speed, like a spoon pulled through honey. On a slope, the chair speeds up until that drag matches the slope's push, then holds that speed by itself. On the steepest ramp the ADA allows in new construction (1:12), a 100 kg rider and chair settle at 0.88 m/s, a slow walk, without touching anything. Nothing rubs, nothing wears, and it can never lock a wheel. Slide the magnets off the disc with a lever and the chair rolls freely again.

Verdict. The physics works, and with margin. A field calculation and an eddy-current calculation, each checked by separately written code, say twelve magnets per wheel hold a 1:12 ramp to walking pace. The physics is the easy half; what decides it is the package. It adds 6.2 kg to the chair and a bracket 63 mm thick that has to live between the wheel and the frame. I put 85% on a well-built prototype braking well enough to clear the experiment's kill line, 58% on it also coping with street grit, a hot car boot and left–right matching, and 12% on it becoming something people actually fit to their chairs (§7). Whether a chair that slows itself feels like being held or being dragged is the part no number here can answer. The cheapest decisive test costs about $200: one braked wheel, two weights and a phone camera (§8).

§1The problem, and who has it

A manual wheelchair has no brake that works while it moves. The levers on the frame are parking locks, which clamp a tyre or let it go. To slow down on a slope you grip the handrims and let them slide through your palms, turning the hill's energy into heat in your hands.

The numbers are not small. On a 1:12 ramp, gravity pushes a 100 kg rider-plus-chair downhill with 69.7 N after rolling resistance. The ADA allows a single ramp run up to 9.14 m long before a landing (0.76 m of rise). Roll onto one at 0.5 m/s and let go, and you reach the bottom at 3.5 m/s (13 km/h) about 4.5 s later. Holding a crawl instead means your hands absorb about 637 J as friction heat on every run. A kilometre of 1:12 street turns 81 kJ of height into heat.

Who has it worst

  • People with weak hands or grip: tetraplegia at levels that leave the arms but not the fingers, arthritis, older users. One manual-wheelchair brake patent (US 8,622,178) says in its background that braking downhill is especially hard, and that using friction on the tyres or rims can be difficult or impossible for users with poor hand strength. These people often avoid long ramps, go down them backwards, or wait for help.
  • Strong users on long descents: they can do it, but gloves wear through and palms heat up, and a hand off the rim to steer, wave or hold a coffee is a hand not braking.
  • Power-assist users: the popular rear add-on drives push but do not brake. A 2022 study of these devices says none of the latest models has an active braking system (§6).

The person I am designing for is an independent manual-chair user with some arm strength and weaker hands, who lives somewhere with ramps and hills and would like to go down them with their hands in their lap.

§2The concept

Each rear wheel gets a flat aluminium disc, laser-cut from 5 mm plate: a ring 380 mm across and 60 mm wide, joined to a hub flange by six spokes. It bolts to the wheel's hub on the frame side of the wheel's own spokes. On the frame, a C-shaped bracket straddles the ring. It holds 6 pairs of magnets, one magnet on each side of the disc, with 2 mm of air on each side. Neighbouring pairs point opposite ways, and steel yokes behind the magnets close the magnetic loop. A lever on the frame slides the whole bracket 53 mm outward, off the ring, to switch the brake off.

Why it holds a speed instead of stopping. Eddy drag is proportional to how fast the metal moves through the field, so it behaves like a viscous fluid. At rest it is zero: the chair can still creep and still needs its parking locks. At speed it grows until it equals the slope's push, and there the speed stops changing. Steeper slopes give proportionally faster steady speeds, and heavier riders do too. It cannot lock a wheel, skid a tyre or fade into nothing, and there is no switch, sensor or battery to fail.

What it is not. It is not a parking brake, it cannot bring the chair to a stop, and on level ground it is a nuisance: switch it off at the bottom of the ramp.

§3Feasibility, from first principles

All numbers below come from model.js, which this page carries inline. The base case is a 100 kg rider-plus-chair on 24-inch wheels, descending 1:12, with the bracket of §2. Units are SI unless marked.

3.1 How hard does the hill push?

θ = atan(1/12) = 4.76°,   W = m g = 100 × 9.807 = 981 N Fdown = W sin θ = 81.4 N,   Froll = Crr W cos θ = 0.012 × 981 × cos θ = 11.7 N Fnet = 69.7 N Crr is the whole chair's rolling-resistance coefficient, casters included. It is my assumption for smooth hard ground; a lower value makes the chair faster (§3.5). Air drag at walking pace is well under 1 N and is ignored.

With nothing holding it, the chair accelerates at Fnet/meff = 0.67 m/s², where meff = 103.7 kg includes the spinning wheels and discs. That is where the 3.5 m/s at the foot of one ramp run comes from.

3.2 How strong is the field in the gap?

First, a one-line magnetic circuit. Follow a loop of flux: out of a magnet, across the gap, through the opposite magnet, along a steel yoke, back through the neighbouring pair and the other yoke. Treat the steel as perfect and apply Ampère's law round the loop. With no current enclosed, HmLm + Hgg = 0. Add flux continuity (the same B in magnet and gap) and the magnet's recoil line, B = Br + μ0μrecHm:

g = d + 2c = 5 + 2 × 2 = 9 mm,   Lm = 2 × 10 = 20 mm of magnet per gap Bclosed = Br / (1 + μrec g/Lm) = 1.44 / (1 + 1.05 × 9/20) = 0.978 T N52 remanence Br = 1.44 T (catalogue typical, ±0.03 T), recoil permeability 1.05. The aluminium ring is non-magnetic, so it counts as air.

Then the real shape of the field. That one number assumes the field fills the pole face exactly and stops at its edge. Real poles are only 25 mm across, the gap is 9 mm, and the neighbours of opposite sign are only 7 mm away, so field fringes outward and leaks sideways to the neighbours. To get the shape, the model replaces each magnet by its two faces of magnetic charge. The face against a yoke is cancelled by the steel, and the face toward the ring is mirrored in both yokes, an infinite series of images. The field at any point is then a sum of solid angles subtended by squares. This is exact for magnets with μrec = 1 and perfect, unsaturated yokes that extend well past the poles, and the model scales the result by 0.978/0.993 to put the recoil permeability back. Check: for a very wide pole the series gives back the one-line answer to four digits.

The result: averaged over the ring's thickness, the mean field under a pole face is λ = 0.69 of Bclosed, about 0.67 T. The rest crosses the ring just outside the footprint, or between neighbours. check.mjs recomputes this field a different way, with a Fourier transfer function between the two iron planes, and the two agree.

3.3 The brake law

A conductor moving at speed v through a field B feels an electric drive v × B. Under a pole it pushes current across the ring, radially. The current has to come back the other way through metal where there is little or no field, and charge piles up at the pole's edges until every current loop closes. Ohm's law with that charge is J = σ(v × B − ∇φ), with ∇·J = 0. The force on the ring is ∫ J × B dV, and it equals the heat dissipated divided by v.

One round pole on an unbounded sheet: F = c σ d B² A v   with   c = ½ exactly Inside a uniformly driven disc of a 2-D conductor, the charge on its rim sets up a uniform back-field of exactly half the drive. This is the 2-D depolarising factor of a circle, the same ½ that appears for a dielectric cylinder. So J = σvB/2 under the pole, and force = J B × volume. In 2-D the two depolarising factors of any shape sum to 1, so a square pole also gets ½ on average. check.mjs runs the model's own current solver on a lone round pole and gets just under ½ on a finite sheet, rising toward ½ as the sheet grows.

The real row on the real ring. The ring is only 60 mm wide, which cramps the return currents. The neighbours alternate, though, so each pole's return current flows through its neighbour's footprint, where the neighbour's own field is pushing it the same way. Both effects matter, so the model solves ∇·J = 0 numerically. It unrolls the ring into a strip, uses a 1 mm grid with insulated edges, and runs conjugate gradients (306 iterations). Four results:

  • A lone pole on this narrow ring, with a sharp-edged field: c = 0.45. The narrow ring costs a little.
  • The row of 6 alternating poles, sharp-edged field: c = 0.61. Alternation more than pays it back.
  • The row with the real fringing field of §3.2. Measured against Bclosed², it gives the number everything else hangs on: κ = 0.359. The computed dissipation divided by drag × speed is 1.0000.
  • The same row with 4 or 10 poles: κ = 0.351 or 0.366, rising a little as the row grows and its ends matter less.
Fpole = η κ σ d Bclosed² A vring = 0.90 × 0.359 × 24.9×10⁶ × 0.005 × 0.978² × 6.25×10⁻⁴ × vring kpole = 24.1 N per m/s of ring speed σ for 6061-T6 is 43% IACS. η = 0.90 is my allowance for what the idealised field leaves out: yokes of finite size instead of infinite planes, glue lines, magnet tolerance, the ring's curvature. It is a guess, and the first experiment measures it.

Is the linear law safe? Eddy currents make their own field, which weakens the brake at high speed. For a thin sheet the speed scale is w = 2/(μ0σd) = 12.8 m/s. On the 1:12 ramp the ring moves at 0.46 m/s, 3.6% of w, so the correction is about the square of that, 0.1%. The field the ring sees alternates at about 7 Hz, where aluminium's skin depth is 38 mm, far thicker than the ring. Force ∝ speed holds.

3.4 From the ring to the ground

The poles sit on a circle of radius r = 160 mm; the tyre's radius is R = 305 mm. The ring therefore moves at r/R = 0.525 of the chair's speed, and its drag acts at the same disadvantage, so the leverage is (r/R)² = 0.275:

kwheel = N kpole r²/R² = 6 × 24.1 × 0.275 = 39.8 N·s/m   (torque: 3.70 N·m per rad/s) kchair = 2 kwheel = 79.6 N·s/m

3.6 Does it overheat?

Disc: 1.01 kg of 6061, heat capacity 911 J/K. With no cooling, 31 W warms it 2.0 K per minute. Cooling: h ≈ 8 W/m²K over both faces (0.150 m²): UA = 1.20 W/K, time constant 13 min On an endless 1:12 hill it levels off 28 K above the air, where the warm disc brakes 10% less (aluminium's resistance rises 0.4% per K) and the speed creeps to 0.98 m/s. h: a flat plate in laminar flow at walking pace gives 5.8 W/m²K (0.664 k Re½ Pr⅓/L), plus a little from the disc's rotation and natural convection. Bare aluminium radiates almost nothing. Call it uncertain by a factor of two. The steady state is solved with the temperature feedback included.

A worst case that needs no convection estimate: a kilometre of 1:12 drops 83 m and releases 81 kJ. Rolling resistance takes 12 kJ, and each disc gets 35 kJ over 19 minutes. Even with no cooling at all that is 38 K. Heat is not the problem.

3.7 Iron, force, weight, size and cost

The yokes. Each yoke must carry a whole pole's flux between its two poles: 670 µWb. At 1.5 T in low-carbon steel, and 40 mm wide, that needs 11.2 mm of thickness between the magnets, tapering to nothing at the outer ends. Six tapered yokes weigh 0.67 kg per wheel; plain bars would weigh 1.20 kg.

The clamp. The two magnet rows attract each other across the gap. Integrating B²/2μ0 over the mid-plane gives 124 N per pole position, 744 N per bracket. Each 6 mm aluminium jaw carries that as a cantilever from the bridge and bends 0.06 mm at the pole line. The bridge bends too, under the jaws' root moment, and tips each jaw a further 0.10 mm: about 0.16 mm in all, under a tenth of the 2 mm clearances. It is a steady load, so setting the clearances with the magnets fitted takes most of it out. The ring itself feels no sideways pull: aluminium isn't magnetic, and the forces on its two faces cancel.

Of that, the bracket on the frame is 2.10 kg per wheel; the rest turns with the wheel. A 4-pole version weighs 2.5 kg per wheel (5.0 kg per chair) and holds 1:12 at 1.35 m/s, but runs at 2.12 m/s on 1:8.

Size. Across the ring the bracket is 63 mm thick: jaw, yoke, magnet and clearance on each side, plus the ring. Of that, 29 mm sits on the spoke side of the disc. The bracket spans 69° of the disc and slides 53 mm to switch off. This is the real design problem (§7).

Living with it. Four everyday costs that the numbers above don't show:

  • Taking a wheel off. The jaws straddle the ring, so the brake has to be switched off before a wheel comes off its quick-release axle for a car transfer. The disc then comes away with the wheel, adding 1.01 kg to each wheel you lift.
  • Camber. The disc tilts with the wheel's camber, and the bracket has to sit square to it, so each chair needs a mount matched to its camber.
  • Width. Unless the chair already leaves room between wheel and frame, the 63 mm bracket pushes each wheel outward by up to that much, less whatever gap there is now. At worst the chair gets 13 cm wider.
  • Switched off. Slid off, the poles sit 10 mm clear of the ring. There the field model leaves about 13 parts per million of the engaged drag, so on the flat the cost is the mass. 6.2 kg adds 0.7 N of rolling resistance, about 7% more push than the same chair without it. Real yokes leak more field than the ideal ones in the model, so the prototype should check this (§8).

Cost, one-off, my estimates rather than quotes: 12 magnets at about $5, a laser-cut disc at $40, yoke steel at $20, and jaws, slide and lever at $60. That is about $180 a wheel, $360 a chair.

3.8 The same law on a kitchen table

You can check the physics of §3.3 in a minute with a copper pipe. Use a magnet sphere, because a uniformly magnetised sphere has an exactly dipolar field outside itself. (A long cylinder magnet in a close-fitting pipe does not, so the dipole formula doesn't apply to it.) Take a 12.7 mm N42 sphere (8.0 g, moment m = BrV/μ0 = 1.11 A·m²) and drop it down 1.5 m of ½-inch type L copper tube, bore 6.92 mm radius, outside 7.94 mm, which leaves the sphere 0.57 mm of side clearance. Slice the wall into rings. Each ring sees flux Φ = μ0m r²/2(r² + z²)3/2, so the EMF is v dΦ/dz. Sum EMF²/R over all the rings:

F = (45/1024) μ0² m² σ · ⅓(ri−3 − ro−3) · v   ⇒   k = 1.43 N·s/m v∞ = mg/k = 5.5 cm/s: 27 s to fall 1.5 m, against 0.55 s in free fall The 45/1024 comes from ∫ z²/(r²+z²)5 dz, a Wallis integral. The thin-wall shortcut using the mean radius reads 1.6% low. Plumbing copper is about 85% IACS. check.mjs redoes the whole integral by brute-force quadrature.

Which way the sphere points matters. The 27 s assumes its moment lies along the pipe, and nothing makes a sphere do that. With the moment across the pipe, the wall's EMF varies round the pipe, so charge piles up and the currents have to close along it. That leaves 0.69× the drag, and the fall takes about 19 s. A sphere pushed off centre or rubbing the wall falls slower. So expect anywhere from about 19 s to 27 s, or longer if it rubs, and don't blame the force law for the spread. check.mjs recomputes the 0.69 by a Fourier method. This proves nothing about the bracket, but it makes the force law tangible: a magnet that should drop in half a second takes a quarter to half a minute.

§4The part that can only be felt

Everything in §3 can be proven, or will be by the experiment in §8. Given a slope and a load, the speed is a number. The chart below is the proven half of the feeling: one ADA ramp run, entered at 0.5 m/s, free-rolling against honey-braked.

What the numbers cannot say:

  • Held or dragged? A brake that never grabs and never lets go is new to a wheelchair user. Some will feel looked after; others will feel the chair resisting them, like walking a dog that won't hurry.
  • Can you let go? After years of the rims being the only brake, taking your hands off them on a slope is a matter of trust. It may take one run, or it may never happen.
  • Is 1.30 s the right "give"? The time constant decides whether the chair eases into its speed or seems to sag. It is set by the mass and the brake, not chosen, and I don't know which feels better.
  • What is the right speed? I set the design target at no more than 1.25 m/s on 1:12, a brisk walk. That number is a feeling written down as a number, and the people who would use this should set it.
  • Steering hands-free. With both brakes equal the chair runs straight. A light touch on one rim steers by adding to that wheel's braking. Whether that feels natural or fiddly is unknown.

These are why §8 ends with a ramp afternoon rather than a spreadsheet.

    §6Prior art

    I've only named things I'm confident exist, and I say how sure I am of each. Every part of this idea is old. The closest thing I found is a Korean patent, below.

    Eddy-current brakes everywhere else Certain

    Magnetic-resistance exercise bikes and rowers, which vary resistance by moving magnets relative to a flywheel. Permanent-magnet fin brakes on roller coasters and drop towers. Electromagnetic retarders on trucks and buses. The linear eddy-current brake on Germany's ICE 3 trains. The physics and the engineering are mature. Difference: none of these is sized for a 100 kg chair at walking pace, packed between a wheel and a frame.

    KR 10-1440447, a wheelchair with an eddy-current brake (filed 2013, granted 2014) Likely

    From the abstract: a conductive metal plate turns with the wheel, and a magnet on the chair's body is brought alongside it so eddy currents slow the wheel. That is this concept's core. I could only read it through a patent aggregator's machine-translated abstract, not the full text, so I don't know how it is sized or whether it aims at holding a speed on slopes. I found no product. Read this dossier as an independent feasibility check of that idea, with the numbers worked out.

    Eddy-current speed limits in fall-protection and walking aids Likely

    US 10,864,393 (Burke), "Fall control system and method of controlling a movement during fall event", is not a walker. A trolley runs along a guide rail, tethered to the user. An always-on eddy-current brake against a control track keeps it below walking speed, and a second mechanism slows a fall without stopping it dead. US 12,553,480, "Magnetic braking and speed control system for walker", uses magnetic cores at a walker's wheels to control pace. I have seen their titles and summaries, not their full claims. Difference: the rail system limits a person's walking speed rather than carrying their weight on wheels, and a walker carries a fraction of the load at a fraction of the speed.

    Mechanical descent aids for manual wheelchairs Certain

    US 4,537,415 (Georgia Tech Research Institute, 1985), "Dynamic brake for manual wheelchair": push the handrim inward and a brake ring meets a friction shoe. US 6,102,166, "Wheelchair brake", has an "on-guard" position giving drag braking on downward inclines. US 8,622,178, "In-hub braking system for manual wheelchairs". The Wijit lever-drive wheels include lever-operated brakes (this one I know only from secondary descriptions). Anti-rollback devices such as US 5,845,746 solve the uphill direction. Difference: all of these are friction brakes the user modulates. None holds a speed by itself.

    Power assist Likely

    Hub-motor power-assist wheels can brake with their motors. Rear add-on drives such as the SmartDrive MX2+ (about 5.7 kg) push but do not brake. A Politecnico di Torino paper (Cornagliotto et al., "Evaluation on Implementing an Active Braking System in Wheelchair Rear-Mounted Power-Assisted Device", RAAD 2022) notes that none of the latest rear add-ons has an active braking system. Difference: the honey brake needs no battery, though it weighs a little more than that add-on (6.2 kg a chair).

    The physics references Likely

    For eddy brakes at low speed: J. H. Wouterse, "Critical torque and speed of eddy current brake with widely separated soft iron poles", IEE Proceedings B 138(4), 1991, which I have seen cited but not read. For the falling magnet: Y. Levin, F. L. da Silveira and F. B. Rizzato, "Electromagnetic braking: a simple quantitative model", Am. J. Phys. 74, 815 (2006), which treats the pipe as stacked rings as §3.8 does, and C. MacLatchy, P. Backman and L. Bogan, "A quantitative magnetic braking experiment", Am. J. Phys. 61 (1993). The derivations on this page are my own and are checked numerically. They don't rely on these papers.

    Standards Certain

    2010 ADA Standards §405.2 (running slope no steeper than 1:12) and §405.6 (no more than 30 inches of rise per ramp run). §405.2's exception lets existing sites that are short of space go steeper: up to 1:10 for at most 6 inches of rise, or 1:8 for at most 3 inches (Table 405.2). Ramps that short are over in a second or two at these speeds. ISO 7176-3 covers how wheelchair brakes are tested.

    §7Pre-mortem

    Suppose it's 2028 and the honey brake is dead. These are the failure modes I think most likely. Each number is my separate estimate of the chance that this failure alone kills the idea. They are not shares of one failure and don't add up to 100%.

    15%technical

    The real brake is much weaker than the field solve says

    κ comes from an idealised field: infinite back iron, a flat strip, magnets exactly at catalogue strength. η = 0.90 is my allowance for everything else. If the real brake is under 41% of the model, even 10 poles can't hold 1:12 at 1.25 m/s. I rate this low because the field and current solves agree with independent code and with the textbook limits. The $200 experiment settles it.

    20%technical

    Steel grit, water and ice foul the 2 mm clearances

    The poles sit 2 mm from the ring and pull in every staple, screw and filing they pass near the ground. Disc runout and wheel play eat into the same gap: a slightly bent hub flange or the end-float of a quick-release axle takes millimetres away at the ring. A scored ring is a nuisance; a jammed one is a hazard. Mitigations: brush wipers at the bracket's entry, a non-magnetic shroud, wider clearances. 3 mm costs 22% of the braking (1:12 at 1.13 m/s).

    10%technical

    A hot car boot weakens the magnets for good

    N52 is rated to about 80 °C, and a chair in a closed car in summer can get close to that or past it. Part of the loss would be permanent. The heat-tolerant N42SH grade costs 18% of the braking (1:12 at 1.07 m/s), which the base design can afford.

    5%technical

    Left and right brake differently and the chair pulls

    A 10% mismatch is a 3.5 N difference between the wheels on 1:12, which a touch on one rim corrects. One lever and cable for both wheels, and magnets matched by measured strength, keep it smaller. A bent bracket on one side is the real worry.

    40%adoption

    It will not fit between the wheel and the frame

    The bracket is 63 mm across the disc, and 29 mm of that must fit between the disc and the wheel's spokes. On most chairs the wheel sits a few centimetres from the side guard and the axle plate, and a 380 mm disc sweeps right through that space. I expect most chairs would need their wheels spaced outward, making the chair up to 13 cm wider at every doorway (§3.7). The 40% is my chance that packaging kills the idea on both routes: a retrofit that widens the chair, and a frame designed around the brake from the start. A version designed into a new frame is the honest route, and that is a much slower road than a retrofit kit.

    35%adoption

    More than five kilograms is too much for the people who would want it

    It adds 6.2 kg to a chair that may weigh 10–14 kg (5.0 kg for a 4-pole version that is faster on 1:12). Every push on the flat and every lift into a car pays for it. A SmartDrive add-on weighs about as much and sells, but it adds power, and this adds drag.

    20%adoption

    The speed it picks is wrong for the hills people actually have

    One bracket gives 0.47 m/s on 1:20 and 1.38 m/s on 1:8. A 70 kg rider goes 0.61 m/s on 1:12; a 130 kg one goes 1.14. The obvious fix is partial engagement: slide the bracket part-way off the ring for less braking, as exercise bikes do. It is plausible, but I haven't modelled how braking falls with overlap.

    15%adoption

    Switching it on and off is a chore, so it is left off

    On the flat it costs 80 N at 1 m/s, so it has to come off at the bottom of every ramp, and uphill too. That means 53 mm of slide, every time. If that needs two hands or a lean, people stop using it. An automatic version (engage only above a speed, or only facing downhill) is future work.

    20%adoption

    Standards and liability

    A brake that does not hold the chair at rest fits no existing category. It would need testing (ISO 7176-3 covers brakes), clear labelling that it is not a parking brake, and the chair maker's blessing, or the user's warranty is gone.

    Only the first risk is about whether the brake works as §3 says. It leaves 85% that a well-built prototype brakes well enough to clear the experiment's kill line. The other three technical risks are about surviving the street, the car and the matching of two wheels. If I treat the four as independent (they aren't, quite), they leave 58% that a well-built brake both works and survives use. That is the product of one minus each technical risk. Adding the five adoption risks leaves 12% that it becomes something people fit to their chairs. The likeliest killers are space and weight, not physics.

    §8The first experiment: one wheel, two weights

    The physics is the cheap part to test, and every other question depends on it. If the brake is much weaker than computed, it needs more magnets, which makes the weight and packaging problems worse. So: build one braked wheel, drive it with falling weights, and measure its brake coefficient kwheel directly.

    Kit (about $200)

    A used 24-inch wheelchair wheel and axle (or your own); a laser-cut 5 mm 6061 disc; 12 N52 blocks of 25 × 25 × 10 mm; low-carbon steel flat bar for the yokes (plain bars are fine here); plywood or aluminium jaws with shims for the 2 mm clearances; braided fishing line; 2 kg and 4 kg weights; a phone that films at 240 frames per second. Handle N52 blocks with care: two snapping together can break a finger.

    Procedure

    Clamp the wheel's axle high, at least 1.5 m plus the weight's height above the floor (a stair landing works), and fix the bracket to the same fixture. Wind the line round the tyre and hang the 2 kg weight. Put tape marks 0.5 m and 1.5 m below the release point, beside a metre stick. Hang the weight from a short thread and release it by cutting the thread in shot, so the release frame is unmistakable (see the pass and kill lines below). Film the weight from release past both marks, five drops each with 2 kg and 4 kg. From the video read three frames: release, mark A, mark B. Then spin the wheel by hand with the bracket slid off, and again with it removed: if it coasts noticeably less with the bracket only slid off, the off position needs more travel.

    Analysis: fit the motion, not the average

    From rest the weight obeys x(t) = v(t − τ(1 − e−t/τ)). The two mark times fix v and τ for each weight, and then kwheel = (m2 − m1)g/(v2 − v1). Bearing and string friction cancel. The analyser below does the fit. Why not just divide 1.0 m by the time between the marks? Because a weak brake hasn't settled by mark A. A prototype at 15.9 N·s/m, below the kill line, has 28% of its transient left at mark A with 4 kg. The naive average reads it as 22.4 N·s/m, comfortably alive. The fit reads 15.9. For a model-perfect wheel the times between the marks would be 2.06 s and 1.02 s.

    What the model predicts

    kwheel = 39.8 N·s/m. With 2 kg the weight settles at 0.49 m/s (τ = 0.10 s) and passes the marks at 1.13 s and 3.19 s. With 4 kg it settles at 0.98 m/s (τ = 0.15 s), passing at 0.66 s and 1.68 s. At 4 kg the ring moves at 0.51 m/s, close to its 0.46 m/s on the 1:12 ramp. These times assume 0.3 N of friction, but the fit doesn't need to know it.

    Pass and kill lines, and why a model-perfect wheel passes

    Both lines come from what the product needs, not from the model. Pass: kwheel ≥ 27.9 N·s/m. That holds a 100 kg chair to 1.25 m/s on 1:12 with this 6-pole bracket. It is 70% of the model's prediction, so a wheel that brakes exactly as modelled passes with room for the ±1.3% timing error. Kill: kwheel < 16.4 N·s/m (41% of the model). Then even a 10-pole bracket, 115° of arc and 4.4 kg per wheel, couldn't hold the target. In between, it works but needs more magnets, so re-weigh it. check.mjs films a simulated model-perfect prototype at 240 fps with 0, 0.3 and 1 N of friction and a one-frame slip in spotting the release, and asserts that every run passes. It also checks that wheels 4% either side of each line land on the right side of it when the release is read to within a frame. Near the kill line, the release frame matters. A weak brake is still speeding up at mark A, so the fit leans on when the fall began. Reading the release 3 frames (12.5 ms) late puts kwheel about 5% high at the kill line, and 6 frames about 10%, enough to lift a wheel 4% below the line above it. At the model's value the same 6 frames move it only 0.2%. Three frames after release the 4 kg weight has moved only 0.5 mm, which is why the procedure cuts a thread instead of letting go by hand. If a reading lands near a line, re-read the release frame before believing it.

    Then: the ramp afternoon (the felt part)

    If the wheel passes, build the second one, fit both to a borrowed chair, and spend an afternoon at a 1:12 ramp with six to eight experienced manual-chair users. Each does runs with the brake off (their usual hand-braking), on, and half-engaged, in random order. Film the runs for speed, and ask after each: did you feel in control, and would you leave it on? Cost: a second bracket (about $180) plus paying people for their time. Kill: fewer than half would leave it engaged on a second visit, or anyone can't correct its pull one-handed. A model-perfect prototype can't be designed to pass this test, and that is the point of running it.

    Revision noteWhat changed from the first draft

    Another agent started this dossier and was cut off. Its model had the right architecture and most of the right physics. Rebuilding it, I found and fixed these problems:

    • The brake's shape factor was guessed, and guessed in the wrong direction. The draft assumed c = 0.4, reasoning that a narrow ring must push it below ½, and a leakage factor of 0.75: product 0.225. Solving the field and the currents shows that alternating neighbours push c above ½ (0.61), while fringing costs field. The net is κ = 0.359, or 0.323 after my realism allowance. The brake is 1.44× stronger than the draft said: 1:12 went from 1.26 m/s to 0.88 m/s.
    • The back bars were sized for half the flux they carry. That is true for an endless row, but in a finite row of six the bar between a pair carries a whole pole's flux and the bar between pairs carries none. The bars are now split yokes, sized from the field at the steel face.
    • The copper-pipe demo used a 1 × ½-inch cylinder with the point-dipole formula. The formula doesn't hold for a magnet 3.4× longer than the pipe's radius. It now uses a sphere, for which the dipole field is exact, and integrates across the wall's thickness.
    • The experiment's pass and kill lines were set relative to the model, with a guessed 1 N friction allowance and a single weight. Now two weights cancel friction, the lines come from what the product needs, and the analysis fits the transient. The naive analysis would have let a dead prototype through.
    • The pre-mortem quoted hard-coded numbers that had drifted: a "360 mm plate" for a 380 mm disc, "about 2 kg per wheel" when the draft's own model said 2.6, and "1.9 m/s on 1:8" for 1.99. All numbers are now tagged and filled from the model.
    • Smaller fixes: convection 15 → 8 W/m²K (the flat-plate estimate is 5.8), with the temperature feedback iterated; the bracket's thickness, slide travel and jaw stiffness are now computed; the discs' own inertia is included.

    Revision noteWhat an independent review changed

    An independent reviewer rebuilt the physics in separate code. It reproduced κ, the field, the clamp force and every downstream number to about three figures, and found these problems, now fixed:

    • The headline probability was on the wrong quantity. The 58% was quoted as the chance that a prototype "brakes as this page says", but only one of the four technical risks is about that. It is now 85% that the brake clears the kill line, 58% that it also survives grit, heat and matching, and 12% overall. The risk estimates themselves are unchanged.
    • The bracket's bridge was missing from the mass and the stiffness. With the bridge added, and the jaws taken as the sectors they are, the brake adds 6.2 kg a chair, not 5.6, and the jaws deflect 0.16 mm, not 0.06.
    • The copper-pipe caveat pointed the wrong way. A sphere with its moment across the pipe falls faster (19 s), not slower.
    • The experiment did not warn about the release frame. Near the kill line a few frames of error move the reading by more than the 4% margin. The procedure now cuts a thread in shot, and the analyser flags readings near a line.
    • Smaller fixes: US 10,864,393 is a rail-and-trolley fall-control system, not a walker. 1:12 is the ADA maximum for new ramps, not for all ramps. The mounting risk now says what it covers, and §3.7 covers taking a wheel off, camber, width and the cost with the brake switched off.
