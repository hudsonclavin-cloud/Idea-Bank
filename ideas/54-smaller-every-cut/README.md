# Smaller Every Cut
> Cut a head and the hydra grows back more of itself. It dies anyway, whatever you do, and the proof counts past infinity.

**Kind:** game
**Run:** open `index.html` in a browser (plain classic scripts, works straight from disk; no build, no network). Tests, from the repo root: `node --test ideas/54-smaller-every-cut/hydra.test.mjs` (the mathematics, about 35 seconds) and `node --test ideas/54-smaller-every-cut/ui.test.mjs` (drives the page and measures flashing in headless Chromium; skips itself if Playwright is missing).
**Made by:** Iolaus

## What it is

A playable version of Kirby and Paris's hydra (1982), with its proof running beside it.

A hydra is a tree. The bottom is its body, the round ends are heads. You tap a head to cut it. If it grew straight from the body, it is gone. Otherwise the neck it hung from grows back: on your *n*th cut, that neck (without the head) sprouts *n* more copies of itself, with everything on it, from the joint below. The first cut regrows one copy, the tenth cut ten, the thousandth a thousand. The game ends when only the body is left.

It looks as if you should lose. You can't. Every hydra dies, whatever order you cut in. The page lets you try to prove that wrong:

- **Five hydras to fight.** *Sprout* (one neck), *Fork* (two heads on a neck), *Stick* (three necks in a row), *Two sticks*, *Tall stick* (four in a row). The Stick dies in 20 to 37 cuts, whatever you do, and you can finish it by hand.
- **The clock.** Next to the hydra is a number that goes down at every cut and never goes up during a fight: the hydra's reading as an ordinal, like <i>ω</i><sup><i>ω</i></sup>·2 + <i>ω</i> + 3. Under it, the page shows which term got smaller and why. Below the game, every reading so far is listed with “>” between them. A tick box writes each joint's own reading on the hydra.
- **Let Hercules play.** Two strategies: the *fast way* (always cut in the biggest part) and the *slow way* (always the smallest part), at 1 or 2 cuts a second with animation, or 30, 1,000, or as many cuts a second as the browser can manage. On the Tall stick the slow way runs to millions of cuts and millions of heads while the first terms of the clock stay frozen.
- **Grow your own.** Tap any joint to sprout a head (up to 24 joints and heads, 6 deep), watch the clock rise, then fight it. The page works out what it can about how long the fight takes.
- **Facts for each hydra.** The fewest and most cuts possible, when the page or the tests could search every order of cuts; otherwise the fast way's count and the slow way's count or a proved floor under it.

The part that can be proved is in the panels beside the hydra: the clock falls at every cut, the counts are exact, the floors are proved. The part that can only be felt is the hydra itself. You cut and it grows, you cut and it grows again, the heads pass a million and the clock calmly ticks down. Knowing the proof doesn't make the hydra look any less immortal. Then the page tells you that ordinary arithmetic, the Peano axioms, can't prove the general fact you have been watching at work: that every hydra dies, whatever Hercules does.

## Why I made it

I wanted a result that stays strange after you understand it. Most paradoxes come apart once you see the trick. This one doesn't. The proof is short, and I can follow every step. I still can't make the hydra *feel* mortal. The proof works by attaching an infinite ordinal to a finite tree, and Kirby and Paris showed that no proof can stay inside Peano arithmetic, the usual axioms for whole numbers. It is a fact about finite trees and whole numbers, and every proof of it has to reach past them.

What I didn't expect was the cliff between the Stick and the Two sticks. One stick dies in 20 to 37 cuts. Add a second identical stick and the fastest possible fight is 988 cuts, and the slow way is beyond any tower of exponents you could write. The reason is in the clock. A stick reading <i>ω</i><sup><i>ω</i></sup> that is first cut on cut *n* becomes <i>ω</i><sup><i>n</i>+1</sup>. The first stick is cut on cut 1 and becomes a mild <i>ω</i><sup>2</sup>; the second can't be cut before cut 2, so it becomes at least <i>ω</i><sup>3</sup>. I wanted people to hit that cliff with their own thumbs.

## How it works

### The game, exactly

A hydra is a finite rooted tree; the root is the body, leaves other than the root are heads. Cut number *n* (*n* = 1, 2, 3, …) removes one head *h*. If *h*'s parent is the root, nothing else happens. Otherwise let *P* be *h*'s parent and *G* be *P*'s parent: after *h* is removed, *n* copies of the subtree rooted at *P* are added as new children of *G*. Hercules wins when only the root is left. This is the Kirby–Paris rule (the step number is the number of copies). Trees are unordered; the page puts the copies next to the neck they copy so that the picture moves as little as possible.

### The clock

Every node gets an ordinal: a head reads 0, and a node with children reading *a*, *b*, … reads <i>ω</i><sup>*a*</sup> # <i>ω</i><sup>*b*</sup> # …, where # is the natural (Hessenberg) sum: write the terms biggest first and collect equal ones. The hydra's clock is the root's reading. This is Cantor normal form, and it runs both ways: every ordinal below ε<sub>0</sub> is the reading of exactly one hydra, up to reordering necks. So in `hydra.js` an ordinal *is* a hydra: an array of terms `[exponent, coefficient]`, with a hydra as each exponent and a BigInt coefficient counting identical necks.

**Every cut lowers the clock.** Cut a head from a joint *P* that is not the root. *P* read β + 1, now β. At *G* the term <i>ω</i><sup>β+1</sup> = <i>ω</i><sup>β</sup>·<i>ω</i> became <i>ω</i><sup>β</sup>·(*n*+1), which is smaller for every finite *n*. The natural sum is strictly increasing in each term, and so is *x* ↦ <i>ω</i><sup>*x*</sup>, so every joint below *G* reads less too, down to the root. A head on the root just takes 1 off the root's reading. Since the ordinals are well-ordered, the clock can't fall forever, and it reads 0 only when no heads are left. Nothing in this uses how many copies grow back, so the hydra dies under any growth rule at all.

**What Peano arithmetic can't do.** Kirby and Paris proved that every strategy wins, and that the statement “every recursive strategy is a winning strategy”, which can be written in first-order arithmetic, is not provable in Peano arithmetic (PA), assuming PA is consistent. The proof above uses well-ordering of the ordinals below ε<sub>0</sub>. Gentzen (1943) showed that PA proves transfinite induction up to any ordinal below ε<sub>0</sub> but not up to ε<sub>0</sub> itself.

### The fast way and the slow way

- **Slow way:** at every joint, go into the neck with the smallest reading, up to a head. Write α[*k*] for the standard fundamental sequence on Cantor normal form: (γ + 1)[*k*] = γ, (γ + <i>ω</i><sup>β+1</sup>)[*k*] = γ + <i>ω</i><sup>β</sup>·*k*, (γ + <i>ω</i><sup>λ</sup>)[*k*] = γ + <i>ω</i><sup>λ[*k*]</sup> for a limit λ. The slow way's cut number *n* takes the clock from α to exactly α[*n*+1]: the copies make <i>ω</i><sup>β</sup>·(*n*+1) = (<i>ω</i><sup>β+1</sup>)[*n*+1], and a cut deeper inside the smallest term changes only that term. So the slow way on a hydra reading α lasts exactly *H*<sub>α</sub>(2) − 2 cuts, where *H* is the Hardy hierarchy: *H*<sub>0</sub>(*m*) = *m* and *H*<sub>α</sub>(*m*) = *H*<sub>α[*m*]</sub>(*m*+1).
- **Fast way:** at every joint, go into the neck with the biggest reading. While the biggest term is <i>ω</i><sup>*m*</sup>·*c* with *m* finite, the next *c* cuts each turn one copy into *s*+1 copies of <i>ω</i><sup>*m*−1</sup> (at cuts *s* = *n*, …, *n*+*c*−1), which `fastWay` adds up in one step: *c*(*n*+1) + *c*(*c*−1)/2. A biggest term with an infinite exponent is cut one copy at a time. That is how the page counts the Tall stick's fast way exactly: five cuts take it to one neck carrying 15 heads, at cut 6, and the rest is level arithmetic. The answer has 13,597 digits; it starts 265,586,598.

### The numbers

| Hydra | Clock | Fastest possible | Slowest possible | How it was checked |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sprout | <i>ω</i> | 3 | 3 | every order of cuts |
| Fork | <i>ω</i><sup>2</sup> | 10 | 13 | every order of cuts |
| Stick | <i>ω</i><sup><i>ω</i></sup> | 20 | 37 | every order of cuts |
| Two sticks | <i>ω</i><sup><i>ω</i></sup>·2 | 988 | slow way: more than 2↑↑↑76 | branch and bound over every order (`hydra.js`); floor below |
| Tall stick | <i>ω</i><sup><i>ω</i><sup><i>ω</i></sup></sup> | fast way: a 13,597-digit number | slow way: more than 2↑↑↑36 | exact batched count; floor below |

For the first three, the fast way is the fastest and the slow way the slowest. For the Two sticks, the fast way's 988 is also the fastest possible.

**Searching every order.** The exhaustive search memoises on (shape, cut number). For the minimum it is a branch and bound with this lower bound: give each non-root node at depth *d* the weight *n*<sup>*d*−1</sup>, with *n* the next cut number. A cut at depth 1 lowers the total by exactly 1. A cut at depth *d* ≥ 2 removes *n*<sup>*d*−1</sup> but regrows *n* copies of a neck whose top node alone is worth *n*<sup>*d*−2</sup>, so the total doesn't fall, and it can only rise as *n* grows. The game ends at total 0, so at least that many cuts remain.

**Floors under the slow way.** When *H*<sub>α</sub>(2) is too big to compute, the page proves a floor using four facts, all checked numerically where they can be:

1. *H*<sub>α</sub>(*x*) > *x* for α > 0, and *H*<sub>γ+δ</sub> = *H*<sub>γ</sub> ∘ *H*<sub>δ</sub> when γ + δ is in normal form (induction on δ). So *H*<sub>γ+δ</sub>(*x*) ≥ *H*<sub>δ</sub>(*x*), and *k*-fold iterates are at least one application.
2. *H*<sub><i>ω</i><sup>β+1</sup></sub>(*x*) = *H*<sub><i>ω</i><sup>β</sup></sub><sup>*x*</sup>(*x*+1) ≥ *H*<sub><i>ω</i><sup>β</sup></sub>(*x*+1).
3. *H*<sub><i>ω</i><sup>λ</sup></sub>(*x*) = *H*<sub><i>ω</i><sup>λ[*x*]</sup></sub>(*x*+1) for a limit λ.
4. *H*<sub><i>ω</i><sup>2</sup></sub>(*x*) = 2<sup>*x*</sup>(*x*+2) − 1 ≥ 2<sup>*x*</sup>; iterating with (2) gives *H*<sub><i>ω</i><sup>3</sup></sub>(*x*) ≥ 2↑↑(*x*+1) and *H*<sub><i>ω</i><sup>4</sup></sub>(*x*) ≥ 2↑↑↑(*x*+1) (Knuth's arrows: 2↑↑*k* is a tower of *k* twos, and 2↑↑↑4 is a tower of 65,536 twos).

The page evaluates the smallest terms exactly while it can, then walks the exponent of the first one it can't down to 4. Two sticks: *H*<sub><i>ω</i><sup><i>ω</i></sup>·2</sub>(2) = *H*<sub><i>ω</i><sup><i>ω</i></sup></sub>(39) = *H*<sub><i>ω</i><sup>39</sup></sub>(40) ≥ *H*<sub><i>ω</i><sup>4</sup></sub>(75) ≥ 2↑↑↑76. Tall stick: the exponent goes <i>ω</i><sup><i>ω</i></sup> → <i>ω</i><sup>2</sup> → <i>ω</i>·3 → <i>ω</i>·2+4 → … → <i>ω</i> → 19 → … → 4, reaching <i>ω</i><sup>4</sup> at argument 35, so the slow way takes at least 2↑↑↑36 − 2 cuts, and in fact more than 2↑↑↑36: *H*<sub><i>ω</i><sup>4</sup></sub>(35) = *H*<sub><i>ω</i><sup>3</sup></sub><sup>35</sup>(36) ≥ (2↑↑)<sup>35</sup>(36), while 2↑↑↑36 = (2↑↑)<sup>35</sup>(2), so the 2 subtracted is lost in the slack. The same goes for every “more than” floor on the page. These floors throw away almost everything (every copy after the first, every bigger term). Iterating (2) the same way as in (4) gives *H*<sub><i>ω</i><sup>*k*</sup></sub>(*x*) ≥ 2↑<sup>*k*−1</sup>(*x*+1) for every *k* ≥ 2 (with *k* − 1 arrows; the step uses *H*<sub><i>ω</i><sup>2</sup></sub>(*x*) ≥ 2<sup>*x*+1</sup>), so the true counts are far above the floors the page shows: at least 2↑<sup>38</sup>41 − 2 for the Two sticks and 2↑<sup>18</sup>21 − 2 for the Tall stick. The page shows the three-arrow floors because they are the ones a reader can picture. None of these numbers rank the hydras against each other. I expect the Tall stick's true count is far bigger than the Two sticks', but proving that needs the monotonicity of *H* in its argument, which I haven't used or checked here.

### The page

`hydra.js` holds all the mathematics as pure functions (ordinals, cuts, layout, counts, searches) and loads both as a classic script and under Node. `app.js` draws the hydra on a canvas: tapered Bézier necks, heads with two eyes, the body at the bottom. Each drawn joint has a key that survives a cut, so the page knows which necks are new. A cut is shown as a 420 ms cross-fade from the picture before to the picture after, with the regrown copies growing out of the joint below them, in amber, along their own curves. (Why not let the necks glide to their new places? See *Safety* below.) When there isn't room, a bundle of identical necks is drawn as a few copies (at most 12) with a “×*m*” badge. When Hercules plays fast, the ordinal engine runs on its own and the picture is rebuilt from the ordinal twice a second. Keyboard: focus the hydra, arrows pick a head, Enter cuts it (or sprouts, in Grow your own).

## Honest assessment

- [Certain] **No human has played this.** I tested it only in headless Chromium, at desktop size and at 390 px with touch, in light and dark schemes and with reduced motion. Whether the vertigo arrives in a real person, and how fast, I can't say.
- [Certain] The theorem (every hydra dies under every strategy, and under any regrowth rule) and its proof by ordinals, as given above.
- [Certain] The unprovability statement is Kirby and Paris's, *Bull. London Math. Soc.* 14 (1982) 285–293: “every recursive strategy is a winning strategy” is not provable in PA, assuming PA is consistent. I could not open the paper from this machine. The wording is checked against several secondary sources that agree on it (MathWorld; Matoušek & Loebl's 1991 abstract; recent papers on hydra battles), and it matches what I know of the result. The page says “every computable strategy”, the same thing in plainer words. The page also says Gentzen showed that the Peano axioms prove induction along every ordinal below ε<sub>0</sub> but not along ε<sub>0</sub> (*Math. Ann.* 119, 1943); that is the standard reading of that paper, and I did not read the paper itself either.
- [Certain] Every count in the table, checked as the last column says. The exhaustive results (Sprout 3/3, Fork 10/13, Stick 20/37) are reproduced by two separate searches, one on `hydra.js`'s ordinals and one on plain nested arrays in the test. The Two sticks' 988 is the minimum by the branch-and-bound search in `hydra.js` (about 25 seconds); a plain branch and bound on nested arrays, written separately in the test, was too slow for that hydra, so the test instead checks that the two searches agree on 60 random small hydras, and the plain model confirms independently that the fast way's 988 cuts are a legal game. The lower bound the search prunes with is proved above.
- [Certain] The slow way lasts *H*<sub>α</sub>(2) − 2 cuts: checked against cut-by-cut play on 250 random hydras (every cut compared with α[*n*+1], games of up to 1,500 cuts), and against Hardy values computed from the definition on plain trees.
- [Certain] The Tall stick's fast way is exactly the 13,597-digit number the page reports: the first five cuts are played in the plain model, the level formula is checked against cut-by-cut play for <i>ω</i><sup>*k*</sup> from cut *t* (*k*, *t* ≤ 4), and the batched count equals it exactly.
- [Certain] The floors 2↑↑↑76 and 2↑↑↑36 follow from facts (1)–(4). The chains are recomputed in the test on plain trees with a separately written fundamental sequence, and (1), (2) and (4) are checked on every case small enough to compute. They are floors only, and weak ones.
- [Likely] The fast way is the fastest possible and the slow way the slowest more generally, but that is only evidence. Over all 84 hydras with up to six necks, the exhaustive search (in a scratch run, with a budget of 3 million positions) finished the fastest-possible question for 38 and the slowest-possible question for 22, and the fast way and the slow way matched every one of them. The page claims it only for the presets and for hydras it searches itself. Matoušek and Loebl (1991) show that Hercules has a “short” strategy (a primitive recursive number of moves) and a “long” one; I haven't compared mine with theirs.
- [Certain] For hydras you grow yourself the page gives up honestly: it searches every order only within about 80 ms, stops counting the fast way after 20,000 steps or 60 ms, and for big hydras says the slow way is finite but too long for it to bound, because walking the exponent down to 4 would take more steps than it allows.
- [Certain] The meters round numbers above 7 digits to 3 significant figures (truncated, so a “more than” never overstates). The clock collapses long sums to their first terms, an ellipsis and the last term.
- **Safety (WCAG 2.3.1 and the brief).** Nothing on the page blinks or strobes, and there is no sound. The page animates cuts only up to 2 a second; above that, the picture and the numbers are replaced together at most twice a second. A cut is a 420 ms cross-fade on an opaque canvas, so each pixel moves once, in one direction, per cut; regrown necks only ever grow. I got there by measuring. My first version glided every neck to its new place, and at 2 cuts a second a fan of thin necks sliding sideways made small patches of the picture go light–dark–light up to 7 times a second; on the fine grid below, the patches over 3 a second added up to about 117,000 px², more than WCAG's 341 × 256 px area. A second version (glide small moves, fade big ones) still let one quarter-size region reach 3 flashes in a second. `ui.test.mjs` measures the rendered canvas on every animation frame: it averages WCAG relative luminance over a 4 × 4 grid (each cell about a quarter of WCAG's rectangle) and over a fine 64 × 48 grid (cells about 10 × 12 px), and counts pairs of opposing changes of 0.1 or more. It runs four scenarios: the slow way and the fast way on the Tall stick as fast as the page can go, the fast way on the Two sticks and the slow way on the Tall stick at 2 cuts a second. In my last four runs of it, no grid region flashed at all and no small cell went over 3 flashes in a second (the worst reached exactly 3). The test fails if any grid region goes over 3 in a second, or if the small cells that do add up to a quarter of WCAG's area. Under `prefers-reduced-motion` a cut shows its result at once, with no cross-fade or growth; the twice-a-second snapshots of fast auto-play remain, since they are what auto-play is. A one-line note under the auto-play controls says how the picture updates. The hydra is a cartoon with round heads and dot eyes, and a cut head just fades; nothing is meant to frighten, and nothing here claims any lasting effect on anyone.

## Where it could go next

- **Hidden Hydra Helper.** Matoušek and Loebl's variant gives the hydra's friend extra moves after each of Hercules's. A two-player mode, where the friend can make the game long only from two moves per turn up, would let people feel where that line falls.
- **Buchholz hydras**, whose joints carry labels and whose proof of death needs ordinals far beyond ε<sub>0</sub>.
- **Goodstein's clock.** The same ordinals drive Goodstein sequences; a side panel could show a Goodstein sequence and a hydra counting down the same ordinal side by side.
- **Better floors.** With the monotonicity of Hardy functions the floors could keep the inner iterates and would order the hydras correctly.
- **Is the fast way always fastest?** It was on every hydra I could search. A proof, or a counterexample from a wider search, would settle it.
