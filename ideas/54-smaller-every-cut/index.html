Smaller Every Cut

Cut a head and the hydra grows back more of itself. It dies anyway, whatever you do, and the proof counts past infinity.

Tap a head to cut it. If it grew straight from the body, it is gone. Otherwise the neck it hung from grows back: on your nth cut, that neck (without the head) sprouts n more copies of itself, with everything on it, from the joint below.

Why it always dies

A clock that can only run down

Picture a countdown clock that can show more than whole numbers. Besides 0, 1, 2, … it can read ω (omega), which is bigger than every whole number. A countdown may jump as far down as it likes at each tick, to any smaller reading, but never up and never sideways. From ω the next tick has to land on a whole number. Any whole number: five, or a million, or a googol. From there it counts down the ordinary way.

Then there are bigger readings. ω + 1 ticks once to reach ω. From ω·2 you can drop to ω + 1000, which still has a whole ω left in it. From ω2 you can drop to ω·1000; from ωω to ω1000; and the tower goes on: ωωω, and higher. These readings are Cantor’s ordinal numbers, written in his normal form. Every one on this page sits below ε0, the limit of the tower ω, ωω, ωωω, …

The fact everything rests on: every countdown on this clock reaches 0 in finitely many ticks, however you choose the jumps. That is what it means for the ordinals to be well-ordered: there is no endless falling sequence of them. The catch is that every jump lands strictly lower, and below ω there are only whole numbers. You may jump to a huge number, but never to anything with an ω left in it.

Every hydra has a reading

A head reads 0. A joint reads ωa + ωb + …, with one term for each neck growing from it, where a, b, … are the readings at the far ends of those necks, biggest terms first. The hydra’s clock is the reading of its body. So a neck ending in a head adds ω0 = 1, a neck ending in a joint that reads 1 adds ω, and the stick of three necks reads ωω. Tick Show every joint’s clock to see them all.

The reading is the hydra. Two hydras read the same exactly when they have the same shape, necks in any order, and every ordinal below ε0 is the reading of exactly one hydra.

Every cut lowers it

Cut a head from a joint P that is not the body. P read β + 1 (the head was its +1), and now it reads β. At the joint G below P, that neck’s term was ωβ+1 = ωβ·ω. After the cut there are n + 1 copies of the shortened neck, so the term is ωβ·(n+1). However large n is, that is less than ωβ·ω. So G now reads less, and a joint whose neck leads to a smaller reading reads less too, all the way down to the body. If the head grew straight from the body, the body just loses its +1. Either way the clock goes down. The page shows the step for every cut you make, under the clock.

So it dies

The clock goes down at every cut. It cannot go down forever. It reads 0 only when no heads are left. Nothing in the argument used how many copies grow back: the hydra could grow a million copies every time, or any number it chose, and it would still die. This is Kirby and Paris’s theorem: whatever order Hercules cuts in, the hydra dies after finitely many cuts.

The fast way and the slow way

Hercules cannot lose, but he can take a very long time. The page plays two ways for him.

  • The fast way always cuts in the biggest part: from the body it follows the neck with the biggest reading, then the biggest from there, up to a head. It deals with deep necks early, while n is still small.
  • The slow way always cuts in the smallest part. Then every cut takes the clock one standard step down, and the fight lasts exactly Hα(2) − 2 cuts, where α is the starting reading and H is the Hardy hierarchy, a family of functions that grows faster as α grows (details in the README).

The two sticks die in 988 cuts at the very fastest, which is what the fast way takes; one stick, which looks almost the same, dies in 20 to 37. The “more than” numbers are floors that a few lines of proof can certify. They are far below the true counts and they don’t rank the hydras against each other.

What arithmetic can’t see

Everything in the game is finite: finite trees, whole numbers, one cut at a time. Yet the proof above reasoned about infinite ordinals, all the way up to ε0. Kirby and Paris showed that this was no accident. The statement “every computable strategy for Hercules kills every hydra” can be written in the language of ordinary arithmetic, and it is true, but it cannot be proved from the Peano axioms, the standard axioms for the whole numbers (assuming, as everyone does, that those axioms are consistent). It sits on a line Gentzen had drawn in 1943: the Peano axioms can prove induction along any one ordinal below ε0, but not along ε0 itself. The hydras, between them, have every reading below ε0.

That is the part to hold on to after the game. Any one game, once it is over, is just a finite list of cuts; the trouble is the word every. Counting can state this fact about counting, and it is true, but counting alone can’t prove it. To see why every hydra dies you have to step past every whole number.
