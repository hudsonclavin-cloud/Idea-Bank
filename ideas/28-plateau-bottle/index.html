The Plateau Bottle

A hot water bottle you can hug all night: a wax jacket that melts at 43.8 °C holds the skin side near 40.6 °C instead of starting at 48.4 °C and sliding.

Kind: redesign · Made by Mushy Zone · Every number on this page is computed by the model embedded in it (model.js), and check.mjs recomputes it, partly with separately written code.

The short version.

  • Used exactly as its makers say (water at 80 °C, two thirds full, inside its fleece cover), a plain rubber bottle puts, in the model's base case, 48.4 °C on the skin it touches. It stays above the all-night burn line of 43 °C for 2.9 h. Skin with better circulation sees less (43.7 °C); skin with poor circulation sees more (54.6 °C).
  • The cheapest fix is a cover 3× thicker. On that night it works as well as my jacket. I didn't expect that, and it is the most important thing on this page.
  • The jacket earns its weight on the nights that actually put people in burns units: kettle-hot water, a lost cover, poor circulation. Out of the 9 nights modelled below, the plain bottle spends time above 43 °C on 8, the thick cover on 6, and the jacket on 0.
  • It costs +801 g (+44%), two to three times the price, and people with warm, well-perfused skin will find it lukewarm. My estimate is that it is better than the plain bottle for most people at 30%, and for people who can't feel or can't move away from heat at 75%.

1. The object, and what is quietly wrong with it

The rubber hot water bottle is a 2 litre bag made to BS 1970. About 61% of UK households own one, according to a Uswitch survey. It costs £4.99 to £17.35 and needs no electricity. Makers' instructions say to fill it at most two thirds full, never with boiling water, and to use it in a cover. Fashy recommends water at 60–80 °C, and other sellers give 80 °C as the maximum.

The evidence that it hurts people

  • The International Burn Injury Database recorded 5944 hot water bottle burns in England and Wales in 2014–2023, 594 a year. Winter 2022, when energy prices spiked, had 423 against 295 the winter before, a rise of 43% (Burns, 2024).
  • One regional burns unit's series of 85 patients broke them down by cause: the bottle burst 48.2%, spilled while being filled 33.0%, and simply touched skin for too long 18.8% (2013). Apply that last share to the national figure and it suggests roughly 112 contact burns a year. That is a rough number, since it mixes two datasets.
  • Another UK unit saw 80 patients, 30 of them with full-thickness burns, and costed their care at over £68,000 (Burns, 2019).
  • Repeated heat at 43–47 °C causes erythema ab igne, "toasted skin syndrome", a net-like brown stain. Case reports put the lower back as now the commonest site, because of hot water bottles held there for pain.
  • Fashy, a major German maker, says in its own safety sheet: "For children or sensitive skin, a surface temperature of < 42 °C must be maintained and there must be no direct skin contact."

Why contact is too hot, from first principles

A hot water bottle is a large heat store behind a thin wall that conducts well. Its 2 mm rubber has a resistance of only 0.0154 m²K/W, or 0.0204 including the film of water inside. Treat the body as tissue with a resistance Rt = 0.03 m²K/W down to a 37 °C core. Then the skin under any wall settles at

Tskin = 37 + (Twater − 37) · Rt / (Rt + Rwall)

  • Bare rubber at 80 °C: about 47.3 °C in the first second, from the two materials' thermal effusivities (skin 1194, rubber 521 W·s½/m²K). It then climbs toward 62.6 °C. The cover is the only thing between you and that.
  • In a fleece cover (0.050 m²K/W, compressed where you lie on it), the steady skin temperature is 49.9 °C. The full transient model, with the bed, the duvet and the cooling water, gives a peak of 48.4 °C at 4 minutes. The skin is then above ISO 13732-1's 10-minute burn line of 48 °C for 12 min, and above its 8-hour line of 43 °C until 3.0 h. After that it slides: 46.3 °C at one hour, 41.7 °C at four, and 38.5 °C at eight.
  • A fixed wall makes the skin's excess over 37 °C a fixed fraction of the water's excess. So the bottle is always either too hot early or too cool late, and the right cover depends on how hot you filled it and how well your blood carries heat away.

The part you can only feel. You know this already. For the first hour or two it is too hot to hold, so you wrap it in a towel or push it down to your feet. By the time it is comfortable, it is somewhere you aren't. People who can't feel heat properly, or can't move away from it, don't get that warning.

Re-read as the engineer who designed the original

The original is a good object. It costs about £5, it has no electronics, and at 80 °C it holds 212 kJ of heat above 43.8 °C. Makers already say 80 °C at most, and a cover. Followed exactly, they give the night above. And there is an obvious cheap fix: a thicker cover. The thinnest plain cover that keeps the as-instructed night under 43 °C has a resistance of 0.141 m²K/W, found by bisection on the full model. Rounded up to 0.15 m²K/W, that is 3× the fleece, about 6 mm of fleece-like fabric. The steady formula below asks for 0.165; the full model needs less, because the water has cooled before the skin could reach steady state. This cover peaks at 42.7 °C and spends 9.0 h of 9 between 38 and 43 °C. On the as-instructed night my jacket cannot beat that.

A fixed cover, though, is only right for one night. The same formula gives the cover each night would need to stay under 43 °C:

NightCover needed (m²K/W)

95 °C, straight from the kettle0.240

95 °C and poor circulation0.500

The last cover is about 20 mm of fleece. Put the same cover on a careful 60 °C fill against warm, well-perfused skin and the skin sits at 37.6 °C, which is no warmth at all. What the bottle needs is a resistance that rises when the water is hot and falls when it isn't. A wax that melts is close to that.

2. The redesign

Keep the bottle; change what sits between the water and you. On each face there is 1 mm of polyester felt, then 7.5 mm (average, solid) of lauric acid sealed in two offset layers of film tubes. That is 381 g per face and 761 g in all. A thin woven cotton sleeve holds everything. You fill it exactly like the old one.

How it works. Lauric acid (dodecanoic acid, a fatty acid from coconut and palm-kernel oil) melts at 43.8 °C and soaks up 175 kJ/kg doing it. Hot water melts the wax from the inside. While any solid wax remains between the melt front and the skin, the outer surface cannot rise above the melting point, however hot the water is. When the water falls below 43.8 °C the wax freezes again and hands the heat back. The skin under a melting front sits at

  • 40.1–41.5 °C for normal tissue, depending on where the front is;
  • 42.4 °C at most with poor circulation, which is under the 8-hour line;
  • 43.8 °C if the tissue carried no heat away at all. That is the hard ceiling, 0.8 K above the ISO 8-hour line.

Skin temperature is the temperature where cover or sleeve meets skin. "In band" means 38–43 °C: clearly warm, and under the ISO 13732-1 line for contact of eight hours or more. The small steps in the plateau line are the model's six wax slices freezing one at a time, not physics.

Checks you can do on the back of an envelope

  • Heat budget. At 80 °C the water holds 1.4 kg × 4186 J/kg·K × (80 − 43.8) K = 212 kJ above the melting point; at 95 °C, 300 kJ. The wax can absorb 133 kJ melting, plus 40 kJ warming from 18 °C, 173 kJ in all. So the wax alone could not swallow a kettle fill. The body and the bed must take the rest. That is why the failure case is kettle-hot water left a long time in an empty bed (section 4).
  • Melt speed. A Stefan estimate for a front crossing 7.5 mm of wax, with the hot side held at 80 °C, gives 12 min (8 min at 95 °C). That is fast, which is why the felt spacer is there: it slows the heat going into the wax.
  • Plain bottle time constant. 1.4 kg of water plus the rubber is 6.53 kJ/K. It loses heat at 0.32 W/K, mostly through the skin, which gives τ = 5.6 h. That is why a plain bottle is still lukewarm in the morning if it stays against you: 42 °C water at eight hours.
  • Heat into the body. Over nine hours, 131 kJ from the plain bottle against 58 kJ from the plateau bottle. The jacket gives less than half the heat. What you feel is skin temperature, but less heat is less heat, and for cold feet that may matter [Likely].
  • Warm-up. The jacket starts at room temperature, so the plateau bottle takes 7 min to reach 38 °C at the skin. Most people put the bottle in the bed first anyway. After 30 minutes there, it is warm from the first touch: 0 min to 38 °C, peak 41.2 °C.

Where the plateau bottle loses.

  • Warm skin. Against well-perfused skin it peaks at only 39.4 °C and is in the band for just 3.7 h; the thick cover manages 7.0 h. Heated skin opens its blood vessels, so this case may be commoner than my base case. It is the most likely way the design fails (section 7).
  • A careful 60 °C fill. There is too little heat above the melting point for the wax to matter: 5.3 h in band, about the same as the others.
  • The ceiling is not absolute. Put kettle-hot water in an empty bed for two hours and all the skin-side wax melts while the water is still 55 °C. The first touch then reaches 46.3 °C, against 41.1, 41.2 and 41.4 °C after 0, 30 and 60 min. The skin freezes the outer wax again within minutes: 4 min above 43 °C, well short of the 10-minute line. It still breaks the ceiling the design is built around. Try it in the calculator. The same applies to the half of the face that looks at the duvet. As instructed, its wax only ever gets 79% melted. Kettle-hot, it reaches 100%, so rolling onto that side early in the night would give a brief spike too. The empty-bed case is a conservative stand-in for it, because in bed the other half of the face is draining heat into the sleeper.
  • Seal lines. If heat did not spread sideways at all, a seal line would behave like a jacket only one layer thick. As instructed, that bound peaks at 41.3 °C, which is fine. Kettle-hot, it peaks at 44.2 °C with 1.3 h above 43. The seal is 3 mm wide and the wax around it is 4–9 mm thick, so heat should spread sideways and the true value should sit near the cell's. That is an argument, not a calculation [Likely]. The test puts a probe on a seal line.

Prior art: this is not a new idea

  • US 11,090,187 B2, "Hot water bottle containing phase change material". Inventor Benjamin Ohmer, assignee WFI Wärmflascheninnovation UG. The family includes DE 10 2014 000 941 B3, EP 3 096 723 and US 2016/0346116 A1. From the parts I could read in search excerpts: a latent-heat insert, with sodium acetate as the named example, goes inside the bottle, where the hot fill partly melts it. It is sized so it only ever partly melts, and it releases heat as the water cools. The description names the burn risk of filling too hot and claims a more even heat "between 38 °C and 48 °C". Google Patents lists it as expired for unpaid fees. I could not read the claims in full, so I cannot say exactly what they cover. I found no product on sale.
  • The Burnout mug (ThermAvant International, 2018; a NASA spinoff story, "The Science of the Perfect Cup for Coffee"). A bio-based wax called HeatZorb, sealed between vacuum-insulated shells, pulls hot coffee down to drinking temperature and gives the heat back as it cools. Retail and sponsored sources give its melting point as about 140 °F (60 °C); I could not verify that from NASA or the maker. The Temperfect mug (around 2013) did the same.
  • ThermaCare heat wraps hold about 40 °C for about 8 hours by oxidising iron. They are evidence that ~40 °C for a whole night is a temperature people will pay for.

What is different here, and what isn't. Putting phase-change material in a hot water bottle is not new. The placement is the design. A mug's wax sits next to the drink, because the drink is what touches your mouth. A hot water bottle's outside is what touches you, so its wax belongs between the water and the skin. There it caps the surface directly, instead of capping the water and leaving the cover to protect the skin. Add the felt spacer that keeps a kettle from overwhelming it, wax on both faces, and a melting point chosen by poorly perfused skin. If the patent's claims cover a jacket, it has lapsed anyway.

Method, and why. The panels are made the way reusable ice packs are. Laminate film runs through a form-fill-seal line. The wax is dosed hot: above 43.8 °C it is a thin liquid that pumps and meters like oil. Each tube is filled with liquid at about 50 °C and impulse-sealed with about 4% headspace, enough for the liquid's own expansion up to 95 °C (from its two published liquid densities). As it freezes it shrinks by the 16% it will regain on melting, and the film simply relaxes onto it. The two layers are tack-sealed to each other, offset. The process is cheap, mature, and already making millions of sealed liquid-filled pouches a year. Injection-moulded rigid cells would be tougher, but they would stiffen a bag that has to drape over a body. The sleeve is ordinary cut-and-sew with a felt lining and two pockets per face, and the bottle is bought in unchanged. That keeps the design a sleeve that also fits bottles people already own.

Weight. Water 1.40 kg + bottle 0.35 kg + wax 0.76 kg + sleeve and film 0.12 kg = 2.63 kg, against 1.83 kg for the plain bottle in fleece. Empty, it is 1.23 kg against 0.43 kg, which matters while pouring (risk 10). The wax is the cost of the plateau. Using less wax brings back the kettle failures (try 4 mm in the calculator).

7. Pre-mortem: it is 2028 and this failed. Why?

Each number is my separate estimate of the chance that this failure alone sinks the product. They are not shares of 100%, and they are not meant to add up. If they were independent, the chance that none of them happens would be 12%.

  1. 40% People who love hot water bottles love them hot. A steady 40–41 °C reads as lukewarm next to the 46 °C a plain bottle gives in its first hour, and they go back to it.
  2. 25% The jacket adds 0.8 kg, 44% more than a filled plain bottle. On a belly or a sore back that is too much.
  3. 25% At three times the bill of materials it retails for £14–29, against £5–17 for a plain bottle, and stays a niche gift.
  4. 20% Heated skin opens its blood vessels, so real tissue resistance sits nearer the well-perfused end. The plateau is then 38–39 °C, and the warm part of the night is about four hours, not nine.
  5. 20% A tube seam opens within two winters, and greasy lauric acid ends up in a bed or a washing machine. Lauric acid can seriously damage eyes (hazard statement H318).
  6. 15% The seal lines run hotter than sideways conduction should allow. The fix is a form-stable wax sheet with no cells, and it costs more than the design can carry.
  7. 10% Real use finds a way to melt all the wax while hot water is still behind it. Kettle-hot water and two hours in an empty bed already do it in the model, briefly.
  8. 10% Technical-grade lauric acid loses latent heat, or drifts in melting point, over a few hundred nights.
  9. 10% Half the burns come from bursts and a third from spills. Judged as a safety product, the jacket fixes the smallest share, and buyers notice.
  10. 10% Empty, the jacketed bottle weighs 1.23 kg against 0.43 kg, about 2.9× as much. That makes it harder to hold steady while pouring, and spills while filling, already a third of burns, become more common. The fix would be a stand or a filling funnel, at more cost.

8. The cheapest test that could kill it

A skin phantom on the kitchen table, two nights, about £200, or £120 if you already own a sous-vide stick and a thermocouple logger. These are estimates, except the lauric acid, which is a UK lab supplier's list price. The phantom is a 12 L box of water held at 37 °C by a sous-vide circulator, with an acrylic plate floating flush on top. The plate stands in for skin and tissue: 5 mm of acrylic plus the bath's film is 0.0283 m²K/W, close to the model's 0.03. A 10 mm plate (0.0546) stands in for poor circulation.

Make one jacket by hand: lauric acid melted in a pan, poured into layflat tube, and impulse-sealed into tubes. Lay the bottle on the plate with the jacket face down and a folded duvet on top. Tape two thermocouples between sleeve and plate, one over the middle of a tube and one over a seal line. Put a third in the water through the neck. Log for nine hours.

  • Night A, the intended night: 80 °C, 1.4 L, 5 mm plate.
  • Night B, the bad night: 95 °C, 1.4 L, 10 mm plate.
  • On the same two nights, run the plain bottle in its fleece cover and in three fleece covers (about 0.15 m²K/W) as controls. They also check the rig: the model expects them to read 48.0 and 42.5 °C at peak on night A. And they test my case against the original. I found no published measurement of a hot water bottle's contact temperature, so section 1 is modelled, not measured. If the plain bottle peaks below 45 °C on night A, the original is less bad than I say.

Pass, if both hold: on both nights, neither probe goes above 43.0 °C; and on night A the tube probe spends at least as long between 38 and 43 °C as the thick-cover control on the same rig, within 0.5 h.

Kill, if either happens: on either night, either probe goes above 43.8 °C. That is the melting point, which a working jacket cannot exceed while it has solid wax, so crossing it means the wax melted through or a seal line is a hot line. Or night A gives fewer hours between 38 and 43 °C than the plain bottle in fleece on the same rig: warm and safe for less of the night than what it replaces, for 0.8 kg more.

Anything in between is inconclusive: fix the build (gaps, sleeve, seals) and run it once more. A second inconclusive result counts as a kill.

Why judge warmth against controls? Late in the night the jacket holds the skin only just above 38 °C, so its absolute hours in band are fragile. A phantom whose acrylic conducts 10% better than assumed cuts the model-perfect jacket from 8.9 to 6.8 h. The thick-cover control on that rig moves too, to 7.0 h, so the comparison holds where an absolute line would not.

The part that can only be felt

If the bench passes, the next question has no model. Five people who use a hot water bottle each get two sleeved bottles that look alike, one plain and one plateau, both filled at 80 °C on a cold evening. Ask them two things: after 20 minutes, which would you take to bed? And after a week of using each at home, which one did you keep reaching for? Kill it if fewer than two of the five pick the plateau bottle after the week. That would mean risk 1 has come true: a bottle that never burns is a bottle that never feels hot, and people want to feel the heat.

10. Revision note

I inherited a model of this idea from an earlier session that was cut off. I kept its structure (three slab stacks around one water node, enthalpy method) and changed these things after checking it:

  • The base case was unfair to the original. It filled at 85 °C, above the 80 °C maximum that makers state. It is now 80 °C, as instructed. The plain bottle's peak fell from 49.8 to 48.4 °C.
  • The obvious cheap fix was missing. A thicker cover matches the jacket on the as-instructed night. It is now front and centre, and the case for the jacket rests on the other nights.
  • The jacket melted through on bad nights. The inherited design had no spacer. Without one, the jacket peaks on kettle-hot water with poor circulation at 44.2 °C with 1.5 h above 43 °C. The felt spacer fixes that. The empty-bed pre-warm case was not modelled at all; now it is, and it is a stated failure.
  • Material data. The inherited draft used one wax conductivity (0.19) for both phases, a density of unstated phase (914 kg/m³) and the top-of-range latent heat (183 kJ/kg). Now solid and liquid are separate, liquid wax is 16% thicker, and latent heat is 175 kJ/kg, with the range tested.
  • The prior art was misread. The patent number in the earlier notes is a hot water bottle with PCM inside, the closest prior art there is, not a mug. Section 5 now says so.
  • "The jacket does nothing about bursts" was too strong. It makes a late burst cooler: 56 °C at one hour against 68 °C.
  • Prices. The wax price came from a South African retail listing. It now uses a bulk benchmark. The BOM fell to £4.75–9.80.
  • The test. There was none. Now there is one, and its verdicts are asserted in code.
