# By Eye
> Every pair of letters here is equal by the ruler. Make them look equal, see how far your eye bent the ruler, and get the alphabet your eye would have drawn.

**Kind:** experiment
**Run:** open `ideas/59-by-eye/index.html` in a browser (works from the file system; no server, build, network or font file). Tests: `node --test ideas/59-by-eye/by-eye.test.mjs` from the repo root. To re-measure the typefaces it compares you with: `node ideas/59-by-eye/measure.mjs` (prints the table; `--write` regenerates `typefaces.js`). To run the method against simulated readers: `node ideas/59-by-eye/simulate.mjs`.
**Made by:** Counterpunch

## What it is

A small type-design laboratory with one subject: you.

Type designers break the ruler on purpose. An O drawn exactly as tall as an H looks too small, so the O is drawn a little taller and deeper than the H ("overshoot"). A crossbar at exactly half height looks low, so it goes up. A horizontal stroke as thick as an upright one looks heavier, so it is drawn thinner. The top of an 8 drawn the same size as its bottom looks top-heavy, so it is drawn smaller. These corrections are the part of a typeface that can only be felt: the ruler says they are wrong, and the eye says they are right.

The page builds a geometric titling alphabet (A–Z, 0–9 and `. , - '`) from rules, so that by the ruler it is perfectly even, and that half is provable. Then it runs five tests:

1. **Round against flat**: make the O in *HOH* look as tall as the H.
2. **Points against flat**: make the points of A and V in *HAVE* look as if they reach the lines.
3. **Across against upright**: make the horizontals of *THE* look as heavy as its uprights.
4. **The middle**: put the bars of *HE* halfway up.
5. **Two halves**: make the top and bottom storeys of *B8S* look the same size.

Each test starts the letters deliberately off, and you drag a strip until they look right; you do that four times, from four different starting points. Only then does the page show the number: how many per cent of the cap height your O overshoots, how much thinner your horizontals are, and so on. Each result comes with a way to see through it: ruler lines and the ruler's own letters drawn in red outline over yours, or the letters *turned sideways* (your thinned horizontals stand up and suddenly look thin) or *turned over* (your raised crossbar drops below the middle and suddenly looks low). Each is also placed on a strip beside real, openly licensed typefaces whose corrections I measured from their font files (eight sans serifs; for the pointed test, the Vs of four serif faces), and for the thickness test beside the averages from a 2019 laboratory study. Those are context, not a yardstick: one person's four tries can be compared with them only for direction.

At the end the page sets the whole alphabet twice, *By the ruler* and *By your eye*, in any text you type, with an overlay mode that draws the ruler's letters in red over yours, a *Turn it over* button, and an SVG download of your alphabet. The title at the top of the page is set in your alphabet as it forms. Below that, the page re-checks the ruler's claims live and measures your five corrections back off the drawn outlines, with the same code that measured the typefaces.

## Why I made it

I wanted to make something out of letters where the interesting part is not the letters but the gap between two ways of judging them. Optical correction is the purest case of that gap I know. It is not a matter of taste in the usual sense: every typeface I measured makes the same corrections in the same direction, and the reason seems to lie in how eyes judge size and weight rather than in fashion. But it can only be learned by looking, and type designers learn it as craft, with tricks like turning a letter upside down or on its side. I wanted a page where a non-designer could find the corrections in their own eye, put a number on them, and then catch the illusion in the act by turning the letter over.

Before choosing I listed and crossed off: a broad-nib pen simulator writing an alphabet live (beautiful, but the felt part is only "which angle looks nice", and the stroke-model tools for designers already exist); a letter-morphing continuum to find where a c becomes an e (good psychophysics, dull to look at); the alphabet's descent from Phoenician, letter by letter (a well-worn chart, and every link a citation risk); Hangul's featural design as a block builder; the fewest display segments that keep A–Z distinguishable; an ambigram alphabet; area-based automatic spacing against spacing by eye. This one has the cleanest split between the provable and the felt: the ruler's alphabet is exactly even, testably, and your settings are exactly yours, and the distance between them is the experiment.

What surprised me building it: the five corrections, applied together, turn a stiff construction into something that looks like a real geometric typeface. The ruler's alphabet looks like a diagram of letters; with an ordinary eye's corrections it starts to look drawn.

## How it works

**The alphabet** (`letters.js`, a classic script that is also a CommonJS module). Cap height 700 units; strokes 100 units thick (exactly, for straight strokes and circles; see below for ellipses). Each glyph is the union of a few primitives: upright stems and horizontal bars (rectangles), straight diagonals (each defined by two points on its edges, with its slope solved so that it is exactly the right thickness), and elliptical rings or ring sectors (an outer ellipse and an inner one inset by the stroke). The glyph is written as one SVG path of `M`, `L`, `C` and `Z` commands filled with the nonzero rule, with every solid contour counter-clockwise and every hole clockwise, so overlapping pieces simply add up. Elliptical arcs are split at every quarter turn, so each extreme point of a curve is an exact path point; each quarter is the standard cubic approximation, within 0.03% of the radius of a true ellipse. Letter widths are geometric-sans proportions (O is a circle, E narrow, M and W wide); spacing is plain side bearings with no kerning.

**Five parameters** decide the drawing, and the *ruler's* values make it even:

| parameter | ruler | what it moves | letters |
|---|---|---|---|
| `round` | 0 | how far round shapes reach past baseline and cap height, per side, as a fraction of the cap height | O Q C G S U J 0 2 3 5 6 8 9 |
| `point` | 0 | the same for pointed apexes and vertices | A V W M |
| `bar` | 1 | horizontal stroke thickness ÷ upright stroke thickness | every glyph with a non-upright stroke |
| `middle` | 0.5 | centre of the H's crossbar and the E's and F's middle arms ÷ cap height | H E F |
| `halves` | 0.5 | the top storey's share of the two storeys' outer heights | B P R K X S 3 8 |

The `bar` setting is applied as if every stroke were drawn by an elliptical pen 100 units wide and 100 × `bar` tall: a straight stroke at angle *a* to the horizontal is exactly √((100 sin *a*)² + (100·`bar` cos *a*)²) thick (the tests check this on the N's diagonal), and a ring's inner ellipse is inset by 100 sideways and 100 × `bar` vertically, which is close to, not exactly, what such a pen would leave on a curve. A round overshoot makes the O, C, G, Q and 0 taller and deeper while their width stays put (so the round test changes height only, like the typefaces' height-only overshoot it is compared with), and moves the outer reach of the other round shapes; a pointed overshoot moves the apex and re-solves the strokes; `halves` resizes the two storeys (height and width) of B, S and 8 and moves the junction of K, the crossing of X and the bowl of P and R.

**By the ruler, these hold**, and the tests check each one with geometry written separately from the module (their own path parser, winding-number rasteriser and scan lines):
- every letter and figure touches the baseline and the cap height and goes no further (only the Q's tail hangs below);
- every straight stroke is 100 units thick measured across the stroke (H, E, T, L, I, Z and F bars; the diagonals of A V W M N X Y Z K R 7 4, the Q's tail and the 1's flag), except the 2's diagonal, which continues its elliptical bowl along the bowl's mid-line and is 99.7 thick;
- circular rings (O C G Q U J) are 100 thick along every radius; elliptical rings are exactly 100 at their four extremes and between 98.1 and 100 elsewhere (an ellipse inset inside an ellipse is not exactly parallel to it; the worst is the narrow 0);
- A H I M O T U V W X Y 0 8 are mirror-symmetric, B C D E H I K O X 0 3 8 are symmetric top to bottom, and H I N O S X Z 0 8 are unchanged by a half turn (grid-sampled, no mismatch beyond 0.03 units from an edge);
- the H's crossbar is centred at exactly 350 of 700 and the 8's storeys are exactly equal.

The comma's tail is shaped by hand and is exempt from all of this.

**The tests on the page** (`method.js`) use the *method of adjustment*: you change one thing until two things look equal. Its known weakness is the starting point: people often stop short, so where a try starts can pull on where it stops. Averaging one try from above and one from below cancels that pull only if the two starts sit evenly around the reader's own setting, which is unknown. The first version of this page centred its two starts on the typefaces' answers; see the revision note. Now each test takes four tries:

1. Tries 1 and 2 start far off, on either side of the *ruler's* value, at the same range of distances above and below. That is further off than any measured typeface goes (O: 4–5.5% of the cap height; horizontals 31–37% thinner or thicker). The order is random.
2. Tries 3 and 4 start nearer, on either side of the mean of tries 1 and 2 (O: 1.5–2.2%), again in random order.
3. The result is not the mean of the stops. The page fits a straight line, by least squares, stop = *a* + *b*·start + *c*·side, where side is +1 for a try that started above and −1 for one below. It reports the line's fixed point *a* / (1 − *b*): the setting from which you would not have moved, averaged over both directions. *b* is the share of the starting error you left in place.

If a reader stops the same fraction short on every try, or stops at the edge of a band within which they cannot tell the difference, or overshoots by a fixed fraction of less than half, or any mix of these, this fixed point is exactly their setting as long as they are steady (no scatter from try to try). The tests check that against simulated readers written separately from the method (`simulate.mjs` and the test file each have their own). If each try closed less than half the distance (*b* > 0.5), the page reports no setting, says why, and asks you to do it again. It also will not take a try in which the control never moved. There is no slider track either: a track's middle can act as an anchor, and the first version's track was centred near the typefaces' answers. The control is a ridged strip with no ends and no middle; dragging it changes the setting by how far you drag, and screen readers hear it as a spin button with no minimum or maximum. The setting does stop at the edges of the test's range, which is centred on the ruler, so a reader who dragged to both ends could work out where the ruler's value is; nothing on screen shows where it, or any other value, lies. Each result is drawn on a strip with your four stops, the result, the ruler's value and the typefaces' values.

Simulated readers, 2000 runs each. The cells give the median error of the reported result in the test's own units, as old method → new method. "Stops ¼ short" means each try closes three quarters of the distance to the reader's true setting, with scatter (0.3% of the cap height for the O).

| Round (O overshoot, % of cap height) | 1.67 | +0.33 → −0.01 (no result 1%) | −0.09 → +0.00 | +1.32 → no result |
| Points (A and V overshoot, % of cap height) | 2.31 | +0.61 → −0.01 | +0.04 → +0.00 | +2.49 → no result |

The old method pulled every result toward the centre of its starts. That centre sat in the typefaces' direction in all five tests: an O 1.3% taller, points 2.5% longer, uprights 8.7% thicker, a bar 0.4% high, a top storey 6.8% smaller. So a reader whose eye agrees with the ruler, but who stops short, was reported as correcting the way type does. A reader who never moved got a typeface-like answer and was told "To your eye it is equal". The new method recovers both readers, and gives the one who never moves no result.

Scatter costs something, and the second table shows how much. Its first two columns are a harder reader, who closes only 55% of the distance on each try and scatters twice as much (old → new, and how often the new method gives no result). The last column is how widely single results spread for the first table's reader whose eye is at the typefaces' median: the range that holds the middle 90% of errors, old / new.

| Test | Harder reader, eye at the ruler | Harder reader, eye at the typefaces' median | Spread of single results, old / new |
|---|---|---|---|
| Round | +0.59 → −0.02 (no result 38%) | −0.16 → −0.23 (no result 38%) | −0.46 to +0.30 / −0.38 to +0.44 |
| Points | +1.11 → −0.03 (no result 37%) | +0.07 → −0.29 (no result 37%) | −0.46 to +0.57 / −0.50 to +0.57 |
| Across | +3.76 → +0.12 (no result 37%) | −5.57 → −2.51 (no result 36%) | −6.51 to +0.22 / −3.75 to +4.84 |
| Middle | +0.16 → −0.02 (no result 35%) | −0.64 → −0.18 (no result 36%) | −0.75 to +0.05 / −0.37 to +0.40 |
| Halves | +3.12 → +0.11 (no result 38%) | −0.97 → −1.10 (no result 37%) | −2.42 to +1.25 / −1.85 to +2.19 |

About a third of the harder reader's runs end with no setting. When the eye is at the ruler, the rest come out right. When the eye is at the typefaces' median, the rest are pulled toward the ruler's value, which means reporting a smaller correction than the reader has: by 0.18% to 0.29% of the cap height for the O, the points and the middle, 2.5 points on the uprights' scale and 1.1 on the halves'. The tests check that, from either side of the ruler, this pull points toward the ruler and never away from it. The old method pulled the same reader toward its start centre instead. That was sometimes nearer the typefaces' median, since it was built around them (the O and the halves), and sometimes much further away (the uprights). A single result scatters about as widely as before, so four tries do not make one person's number precise.

I also drove the real page in headless Chromium by keyboard, at desktop and phone widths (a scratch check, not one of the folder's tests). A reader who stops a quarter short came back within half a step of the control of its true setting in all five tests, whether its eye was at the ruler or at the typefaces' median. A reader who nudged once and committed got "no setting" in all five. "Looks equal" stayed disabled until the control had moved.

**The typefaces** (`measure.mjs`) are eight openly licensed sans serifs installed on the machine I built this on: DejaVu Sans Book and Bold (2.37), Liberation Sans Regular and Bold (2.1.5), Carlito Regular and Bold (Debian package 20230309) and FreeSans Regular and Bold (GNU FreeFont 20211204). `measure.mjs` is a small TrueType reader (offset table, `head`, `maxp`, format-4 `cmap`, `loca`, `glyf`, including offset-only composites) that flattens the quadratic outlines of H, O, A, V, 8 and B and passes them to the same `measureSet` function that measures the page's own alphabet. Only the resulting numbers are stored in `typefaces.js`; no outline leaves the font files. What it found:

- **Round:** every face overshoots its O, by 1.1% to 2.4% of the cap height averaged over top and bottom (median 1.7%). Single sides run from 1.05% to 3.16%: FreeSans's O sinks 3.16% below the baseline but rises only 1.65% above the cap height. The eight faces are four designs in two weights, and in three of the four the regular and bold O overshoot by exactly the same amount, so this is closer to four data points than eight.
- **Bar:** every face makes the H's uprights thicker than its crossbar, by 13% to 42%.
- **Middle:** every face raises the centre of the H's crossbar above half height, by 0.3% to 4.0% of the cap height.
- **Halves:** every face makes the top of its 8 smaller, by 5.5% to 13.0% (in outer height, each storey counted to the far side of the shared waist), and the top of its B smaller too.
- **Point:** none of the eight has a pointed A or V. All cut their apexes flat at the cap height and baseline (the A is 15% to 31% of the cap height wide just 1% below its top), and a flat cut needs no overshoot. For the pointed test the strip uses the V of four serif faces (Liberation Serif and FreeSerif, regular and bold), whose Vs end in a narrow vertex that sinks 1.7% to 2.7% below the baseline. Narrow, not pointed: 1% of the cap height above its lowest point the vertex is 3.1% to 10.75% of the cap height wide, against 13.2% to 31.5% for the eight sans Vs, which stop flat on the baseline.

**Files.** `index.html`, `style.css`, `app.js` (the page), `letters.js` (the alphabet, the measuring code and the live checks), `method.js` (where each try starts, and how four tries become a setting), `typefaces.js` (the measured numbers), `measure.mjs` (the font reader that produces them), `simulate.mjs` (simulated readers, and the old method for comparison), `by-eye.test.mjs` (the tests). No dependencies; light and dark themes; touch and keyboard; turning animations respect `prefers-reduced-motion`; your settings are kept only in this browser's `localStorage`.

## Honest assessment

No typographer has reviewed this. The claims below are labelled by how sure I am.

- **[Certain] The ruler's alphabet is as even as the page says.** Every property in the list above is checked by the tests with independent geometry, and the page re-checks the main ones live on every load. "Even" means even *by the ruler*, which is the point: it does not look even.
- **[Certain] Your corrections are applied exactly.** For random settings across the whole allowed range, the tests measure the outlines back (O's extremes, A's and V's points, the H's bar thickness and centre, the 8's storeys) and recover the settings to 10⁻⁴; the page shows the same measurement for your settings.
- **[Certain] The typeface numbers are what those font files contain**, measured by the method described; the test re-measures every face whose file is installed and compares with `typefaces.js`. **[Likely]** that they mean what the page implies. A sans serif's thin crossbar is partly an optical correction and partly the designer's taste for contrast; a raised bar or a small top storey is partly correction and partly style. The faces all lean the same way as the illusions, which is the claim; the exact sizes are design decisions, not measurements of an eye.
- **[Certain] A laboratory has measured the thickness illusion; [Likely] it is general.** de Waard, Van der Burg and Olivers (2019) found that a vertical line had to be 5.4% thicker than a horizontal one to look equally thick (Experiment 1; the bias showed in 27 of 28 subjects), and 2.6% in Experiment 2. I read this in the paper itself. Those are group averages, and individuals varied: in Experiment 1 the spread of individual settings (SD 0.03°) was more than half the mean (0.05°). The authors link the illusion to the vertical–horizontal length illusion, but the two correlated only weakly (r = −0.32) and only in one of their two conditions. The page's strip shows both averages as "lab" diamonds in the same units as yours ("uprights thicker, %"). Their task was a same/different judgement on single lines, not an adjustment of letters, and one reader's four tries are not a group mean. So the page says that only the direction of your result is worth setting beside theirs.
- **[Likely] On average, healthy people bisect vertical lines slightly above their true middle** (Shelton, Bowers and Heilman 1990, from the abstract and as cited by Chieffi et al. 2018; Drakul et al. 2016 report "slight … upward (for vertical lines) errors in healthy subjects"). It is not true of everyone, and it is small: Drakul et al.'s 17 upright controls averaged 0.05° and 0.15° upward, with standard deviations of about 0.35° between people, and they did not test the vertical error against zero. So the page says "on average" and "varies from person to person", not "most people". **[Speculative]** that this is why crossbars are raised and top storeys shrunk. It is the obvious candidate and the direction is right, but I found no study that tests letterforms for it, and the page says "one plausible reason".
- **[Likely] Overshoot amounts.** That round letters need overshoot is standard type-design knowledge; how much is craft. Wikipedia's article on overshoot (which I could only see through a search summary) gives roughly 1–3% for round letters and cites the type-technology pioneer Peter Karow (*Digital Formats for Typefaces*, URW Verlag) as suggesting 3% for an O and 5% for an A; Tobias Frere-Jones's essay "Typeface Mechanics: 001" (2015, also seen only through a search summary) says pointed shapes get more overshoot than round ones. The page leans on my own measurements instead, and only says that designers' own accounts give points more.
- **[Certain] The method gets a steady simulated reader's setting back exactly; [Likely] it gets a real reader's back roughly, erring toward the ruler.** For readers who stop a fixed fraction short, stop at the edge of a can't-tell band, overshoot by a fixed fraction of less than half, or any mix of these, the fixed point is their setting exactly when they are steady. With the scatter of the first table it is off by a few hundredths of a unit at the median. An unsteady reader who stops well short often gets no result, and otherwise a correction smaller than their own (the second table), because the pull is toward the ruler's value, not the typefaces'. The tests check all three. Real readers need not follow a straight-line model: someone whose stopping point depends on the start in a curved way, or who changes strategy between tries, can still be pulled. A reader who leaves more than half the starting error in place gets no result rather than a biased one.
- **[Speculative] Your numbers say something about your eye.** They are one person, one screen, one size, one sitting, four tries per test, and the page cannot tell how much of your number is noise: four tries fix three unknowns, which leaves almost nothing to estimate noise from. Size matters in particular: corrections are usually judged at a given size, and the page shows letters at one large size. The direction of a clear correction is the robust part. Agreeing with the typefaces does not validate your eye, because their corrections are partly style, and disagreeing is not an error. The page treats your result as an anecdote and says so.
- **Generalisation is mine.** Only five corrections are asked about, and some letters inherit a correction I chose to extend: E and F take the H's bar height; K's junction, X's crossing, and P's and R's bowls take the 8's halves; V, W and M take the A's point. The A's own crossbar (at 27% of the cap height), the G's bar and the hyphen are fixed. Real faces also correct things the page ignores (round strokes slightly heavier than straight ones, thinned joins, spacing).
- **The letters.** It is a plain geometric construction, and it shows. My weakest glyphs: the S (two ellipses meeting at a horizontal spine; correct, slightly mechanical), the 1's flag and the comma (simple, a little crude), and the M and N, whose diagonals meet the stems with flat tops wider than the stems. Diagonal joins have no ink traps. There is no kerning, so pairs such as LT and TA gape in the specimen. I looked at every glyph large, by the ruler and with exaggerated corrections. The allowed range of each setting is exactly what its test can reach. Within it every glyph stays a valid outline. Wherever the O is no shorter than the H and the horizontals are no heavier than the uprights (the direction every typeface takes), the smaller counter of B, 3, 8 and S is at least 135 units tall (19% of the cap height; each counter measured at its tallest). Push several settings to their unusual extremes at once, though (round shapes pulled 7% of the cap height inside the lines, horizontals 40% heavier than the uprights and the smaller storey at its smallest), and the smaller counters of 3 and 8 close to about 32 units, 4.5% of the cap height: slits. The B's closes to about 73. "Does not break" is not "a typographer would ship it".
- **Rendering.** Verified headless in Chromium at 1280 px and 390 px, light and dark, with no console errors and no horizontal overflow; not checked in Safari or Firefox. The SVG masks that draw only the outside edge of the ruler's letters are standard SVG 1.1.

## Where it could go next

- **Size.** Run the same five tests at 12, 24 and 200 px. The page shows letters at one large size only; whether your corrections hold at text size is an open question here.
- **A forced-choice version.** Replace the strip with a staircase of "which looks taller?" questions, which gives a sharper estimate and a measure of your uncertainty. Short of that, six or eight tries per test instead of four would leave enough over to estimate your own noise.
- **The corrections the page ignores.** Heavier round strokes, thinned joins and ink traps, optical spacing ("make the gaps in HOH look equal").
- **Lowercase.** x-height overshoot, the shoulder of n, the e's bar.
- **A real font.** Export the alphabet with your corrections as an OpenType file. That needs a font compiler, which this folder deliberately does not have.

## Revision note

An independent reviewer rebuilt the geometry and the font measurements in separate code and reproduced every number. The serious problem was the experiment itself, and it is fixed:

- **The method could manufacture the answer it compared you with.** Both start ranges, and the slider's middle, were centred on the typefaces' corrections rather than on the ruler. So averaging the two tries cancelled nothing for a reader who stops short, and the result was pulled toward the typefaces. A reader who never moved the slider got a typeface-like answer and the verdict "To your eye it is equal". Now each test takes four tries. The first two start symmetrically about the ruler, beyond every typeface; the last two start about your own first answer. The result is the fixed point of a start-to-stop line fitted to all four. Simulated readers (both tables above, and the tests) show that the old method pulled a stop-short reader toward its start centre, while the new one recovers a steady reader's setting exactly. When noise leaves a residual pull, it points toward the ruler. Readers whose stops follow their starts, and tries in which nothing moved, get no result. The slider is now a strip with no track, ends or middle, and it does not announce its range to screen readers. Results saved by the old method are discarded.
- **The comparisons claimed more than one person's settings can show.** The page and this README now say three things: the lab figures are group averages for single lines, with a spread between people more than half the mean and only a weak, one-condition link to the length illusion; the typefaces are partly style; and only the direction of your result is worth setting beside either. The round verdict no longer says "To your eye it is equal".
- **The O grew in width as well as height**, so the round test did not match the typefaces' height-only overshoot. Its width now stays put.
- **Smaller fixes:**
  - The pointed comparison is now described as four serif Vs with a narrow vertex, not "two … nearly to a point".
  - The O range is per face, averaged over top and bottom. Single sides run from 1.05% to 3.16%, and the faces are four designs in two weights.
  - The 2's diagonal is 99.7 thick, not 100.
  - "Most people mark it too high … consistent" is now "on average … varies from person to person".
  - The smaller top storey has "the usual reason given", not a stated cause.
  - The allowed ranges are now exactly what the tests can reach. The README no longer says nothing breaks, and gives where extreme combinations close the counters of 3 and 8.
