Every pair of letters below is equal by the ruler. Make it look equal, see how far your eye bent the ruler, and get the alphabet your eye would have drawn.

Type designers break the ruler on purpose. An O drawn exactly as tall as an H looks too small; a crossbar at exactly half height looks low; a horizontal stroke as thick as an upright one looks heavier. So they draw the O a little bigger, the bar a little higher, the horizontal a little thinner, until the letters look right.

Below are five of those corrections. Each test draws its letters from rules that make them provably equal, puts one dimension on a slider, and knocks it off at random. Drag until the letters look equal to you, tap Looks equal, and do it once more from the other side. Then the page shows what you did, measures it against real typefaces, and shows you how to see through it.

    Honest limits of the ruler. Circular rings (O C G Q U J) are exactly 100 thick all the way round; elliptical ones are exactly 100 at their four extreme points and between 98 and 100 in between, because an ellipse inset inside an ellipse is not exactly parallel to it. Curves are drawn with cubic Bézier pieces, which stay within 0.03% of a true ellipse. The comma's tail is shaped by hand and follows none of these rules.

    The tests: how your settings are taken

    Each test is a method of adjustment, one of the classical methods of psychophysics: you change one thing until two things look equal. Two things can bias it. Where you start can pull on where you stop, so each test runs twice, once starting too big and once too small, and your setting is the average of the two. And the middle of a slider can act as an anchor, so the slider's range is shifted at random on every try and its middle means nothing. No number is shown until you have committed both tries.

    Your settings live only in this browser's storage. They are one person, on one screen, at one size, on one day: an anecdote, not data. What the page can show is the direction of your corrections, and whether it agrees with the typefaces.

    The typefaces: measured, not quoted

    The grey dots are eight openly licensed sans serifs, measured by measure.mjs: a small TrueType reader that flattens each face's H, O, A, V, 8 and B and scans them with the same code that measures your alphabet. Only these numbers are kept; no outline is copied.

    None of the eight has a pointed A or V: all cut their apexes flat (the flat top of each A is 15–31% of the cap height wide, 1% below the top), so a flat cut needs no overshoot. For the pointed test the comparison is the V of two serif faces whose V comes nearly to a point.

    Why the eye does this (what is known)

    Thick horizontals. In 2019 de Waard, Van der Burg and Olivers showed people one horizontal and one vertical line. On average the vertical had to be 5.4% thicker before the two looked equally thick (2.6% in a second experiment), and the bias showed in 27 of 28 people. They found it related to the older illusion in which vertical lengths look longer than horizontal ones. Before their paper, they wrote, the illusion was widely accepted among type designers but untested.

    High middles. Asked to mark the middle of a vertical line, most people mark it slightly too high; the effect is small and consistent (Shelton, Bowers and Heilman 1990, and later work). A mark that looks central is above the centre, so a bar placed at the true centre looks low, and the upper of two equal halves looks larger. That is one plausible reason for both the raised crossbar and the smaller top storey; it is not a settled account of either.

    Overshoot. A round or pointed shape touches a guideline at a single point, while a flat one sits on it along its whole width, so the round shape seems to stop short. How much to add is craft knowledge rather than a measured law. The eight faces here add 1.1% to 2.4% of the cap height to their O, and type designers' own accounts give points more than rounds.
