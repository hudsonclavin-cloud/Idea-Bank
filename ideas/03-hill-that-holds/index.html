Things to find 0/5

    What you're looking at

    A star and a planet on a circular orbit, filmed by a camera that turns with the planet so both hold still. The shading is the effective potential energy a marble feels in that turning frame: the gravity of both bodies plus the centrifugal pull outward. Bright means high. The bright ridge is the planet's orbit, and its two summits, L4 and L5, sit 60° ahead of and behind the planet.

    Why don't marbles roll off?

    Switch Coriolis off and they do. In a turning frame, anything that moves also feels the Coriolis force, a sideways shove at right angles to its motion. It does no work, so it can't lift a marble or let it down. It only steers. When a marble starts to roll downhill, Coriolis turns it sideways, and "rolling off" becomes "circling the summit". Here that circling is clockwise, the same way wind circles a high-pressure system in the northern hemisphere.

    The energy fence

    The hatched patch that appears while you aim is where that marble can never go: getting there would take more energy than it has. That energy is its Jacobi constant, which the simulation conserves. A marble dropped at rest starts right on the fence. The fence is identical with Coriolis on or off, so energy alone does not keep anything on the hill.

    Why about 1/26?

    Steering only wins if the planet is light enough. The summits can hold only while 27μ(1−μ) < 1, i.e. μ < 0.0385, about 1/26 of the system's mass (Gascheau and Routh worked this out in the 19th century). Jupiter (μ ≈ 0.00095) keeps thousands of Trojan asteroids at its L4 and L5. Pluto–Charon (μ ≈ 0.11) can't hold any in this model.

    That condition is necessary, not a guarantee. A tadpole has two wobbles, a slow one round the summit and a fast one about once per planet-year. At a few masses below the limit their rhythms lock, 3:1 near μ ≈ 0.0135 and 2:1 near μ ≈ 0.0243, and wide tadpoles there can be shaken loose. Drag the slider to one and try the swarm. Above the limit it is different: even the smallest nudge grows.

    Small print

    Circular restricted three-body problem: two bodies on a circular orbit, a massless marble, flat 2-D, nothing else in the universe. Bodies are drawn, and collide, at exaggerated sizes. Integrator: 4th-order symplectic (Yoshida) with an exact frame-rotation step, 1,200 fixed steps per planet-year. The README has the tests and numbers.
