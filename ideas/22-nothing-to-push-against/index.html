Things to try 0/7

    What you're looking at

    You are the camera on a small spacecraft, a 60 kg box with its long side along your line of sight. There is nothing to push against out here, so the spacecraft turns the only way it can: three motors spin up three reaction wheels, one per axis, and the body takes the opposite spin. Drag the sky and watch the bars. Every turn you make is borrowed from a wheel: while the body turns, the wheel spins the other way, and when the body stops, the wheel hands the spin back. Only pushes from outside stay behind.

    Why the wheels fill up

    If the only pushes were your own, the books would always balance: slew, stop, and the wheels are back where they started. But small outside torques never quite stop. Sunlight presses on an off-centre solar panel, gravity pulls a little harder on the near end of the craft than the far end, and thin air and the planet's magnetic field add their own twist. Some of that averages out over an orbit. The sim lumps the part that doesn't into one steady twist, ten millionths of a newton-metre by default. That is on the high side for a craft this size, chosen so you can watch it happen. The autopilot cancels the twist by spinning a wheel a little faster every second, and nothing on board can spin it back down. After about fourteen hours the wheel lined up with the twist reaches its limit (6,000 rpm). Holding still at this pointing would mean that wheel taking more, so the autopilot gives up the hold and only damps the turning. The full wheel stays pinned and the sky starts to drift, faster and faster. (Point so that the load is shared between two or three wheels and you buy a little time, since together they hold up to 0.87 along a diagonal, but the twist keeps coming.) Turn to a new target with a loaded wheel and the load migrates to a different wheel, because the stored momentum stays fixed in the sky, not in the spacecraft.

    Why you have to pay

    The total angular momentum of body plus wheels, measured against the stars, can only be changed by something outside: the disturbance going in, or thrusters going out. "Empty the wheels" fires a pair of cold-gas thrusters while the wheels wind down against them. A full wheel costs about two grams of gas. With the two kilograms on board, that is the mission's clock: about 1.6 years at the default torque, or 16 years for a quieter craft at one millionth of a newton-metre. The ledger on the left adds up every deposit and withdrawal and compares it with what is on board, by a different route. It balances to round-off, always. That is the part you can prove. The heaviness of the sky is the part you can only feel.

    The flip

    Switch the autopilot off and the wheels stop helping: your drag is a raw motor torque, and the sky keeps turning after you let go. Start a tumble about the middle axis and the sky will, every couple of minutes (seconds, at 10×), turn over on its own, with no torque applied at all. That is the intermediate-axis instability (the tennis-racket or Dzhanibekov effect). Spins about the long or short axis are steady, at least for a perfectly rigid body. Anything that flexes or sloshes slowly drains energy and drifts a long-axis spin into a short-axis one, which is what happened to Explorer 1. Then try to catch the tumble: the wheels can soak up only their capacity, and 17°/s about the middle axis is 1.8 N·m·s, more than three times a wheel's 0.5, so the thrusters have to finish the job.

    Small print

    Rigid body with three reaction wheels, principal inertias 7.5, 6.0, 3.5 kg·m², wheel capacity ±0.5 N·m·s, motor torque 20 mN·m, thruster couple 16 mN·m (0.4 m arm), cold gas Isp 65 s, 2 kg propellant. The disturbance is a constant torque vector fixed in the inertial frame. When the wheels can't hold the target, the autopilot only damps the rate. Integrator: kick–drift–kick at a fixed 0.02 s step, the torque-free drift split into exact single-axis rotations composed to 4th order, so the inertial momentum is conserved to round-off. Time runs faster than real (1×, 10×, 600×), and stars are invented. Keys: drag, arrows, Q/E roll, A autopilot, D empty wheels, T time rate, Space pause. The README has the tests and numbers.
