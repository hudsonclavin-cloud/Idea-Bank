# Nothing to Push Against
> A spacecraft can't turn; it can only lend its spin to a wheel. Drag the sky, feel the wheels fill up, and pay to empty them.

**Kind:** simulator
**Run:** open `index.html` in a browser (plain classic scripts, so it works straight from disk or any static server; no build, no network). Physics tests: `node --test ideas/42-nothing-to-push-against/attitude.test.mjs` from the repo root (Node 22; about 10 s).
**Made by:** Gyre Halloway

## What it is

You are the camera on a small spacecraft, looking out at an invented star field. There is nothing out here to push against, so the spacecraft turns the only way a free body can: three motors spin three **reaction wheels**, one per axis, and the body takes the opposite spin. Drag the sky and it follows your finger, heavily, while three bars show what each wheel had to take on to make that happen.

What you can do:

- **Drag the sky** (mouse, touch, or arrow keys; the two round buttons roll). With the autopilot on, the sky follows your finger and the wheels do the work. Switch the autopilot **off** and your drag becomes a raw motor torque: the sky keeps turning after you let go, and only an equal and opposite push stops it.
- **Watch the wheels fill up.** A steady **disturbance torque** (10 µN·m by default, adjustable 0–100) twists the spacecraft the same way all day. It stands for the part of the outside torques (sunlight on an off-centre array, gravity gradient, drag, magnetic) that doesn't average out over an orbit. Holding still against it means spinning a wheel a little faster every second. After 13.9 hours the wheel lined up with the torque reaches its capacity (0.5 N·m·s, 6,000 rpm). The autopilot then gives up the hold and only damps the rate: the full wheel stays pinned and the sky drifts, faster and faster. Time runs at 1×, 10× or 600×, so you can watch this happen. The default is on the high side for a craft this size, chosen so a wheel fills in a watchable time (see *Honest assessment*). At a quieter 1 µN·m it would take 5.8 days.
- **Pay to empty them.** "Empty the wheels" fires cold-gas thrusters while the wheels wind down against them. A full wheel costs 1.96 g of gas, and the 2 kg tank is the mission's clock: 590 days at the default torque, 16 years at 1 µN·m.
- **Read the ledger.** The total angular momentum, measured against the stars, is added up two ways: from every push from outside (the disturbance in, thrusters out) and from what is on board right now. The two agree to floating-point round-off, whatever you do, and the gap is printed live.
- **Tumble.** Set the body spinning at 17°/s with the autopilot off, about its short (x, most inertia), middle (y) or long (z, least inertia) axis. The middle-axis spin turns the sky over on its own every 113 seconds with no torque applied at all (the intermediate-axis, "tennis racket" or Dzhanibekov effect), seen from inside. Then catch it: the wheels can absorb only their capacity, and the thrusters have to finish.
- An inset shows the spacecraft from outside with a gold arrow for the total momentum, the arrow that never moves unless something from outside pushes.
- Seven **things to try**, with progress kept in `localStorage`, including a tour of six guide stars.

## Why I made it

The brief was spaceflight as engineering, and the thing I kept coming back to is that a spacecraft has no steering wheel. Everything about pointing is a conservation law with a budget attached: you don't turn, you trade spin with a flywheel; the flywheel remembers every outside push you ever absorbed; and the only way to forget is to throw mass overboard. I wanted the heaviness of that to be something you feel in your hand, dragging a sky that resists, and the bookkeeping behind it to be something a reviewer can check to the last digit.

Before choosing I listed candidates and crossed off the obvious ones: a launch-a-rocket game, a Hohmann calculator, a solar-system scale model, generic n-body, the Clohessy–Wiltshire "burn toward it and fall behind" docking lesson (the example everyone is handed, and the first thing idea 03's author crossed off too), launch windows and porkchop plots (too close to the repo owner's own field), and a lunar lander. That left a gravity-assist pool table, an aerobraking corridor, a low-thrust spiral versus a Hohmann transfer, a thermal eclipse balance, and this. I picked the wheels because the idea has a sharp split: a conserved vector you can prove, and a heaviness you can only feel. Seeing the Dzhanibekov flip from *inside* the tumbling body, as a sky that turns over by itself, was the bonus that settled it.

## How it works

**Model.** A rigid body with principal inertias J = (7.5, 6.0, 3.5) kg·m² about body x, y, z (z is the camera's line of sight). These are about what a uniform 60 kg box of 0.5 × 0.7 × 1.0 m would have (7.45, 6.25, 3.7), rounded. So x, the short side, has the most inertia, and z, the long side, has the least. One reaction wheel per axis, each with momentum capacity ±0.5 N·m·s, motor torque limit 0.02 N·m, and axial inertia 8×10⁻⁴ kg·m² (only used for the rpm readout: 0.5 N·m·s is 5,968 rpm). Thruster couples give 0.016 N·m per axis: two 0.02 N thrusters, each 0.4 m from the axis. Cold gas, Isp = 65 s, 2.0 kg of propellant. The same 0.4 m arm on every axis is an idealisation. Pitch and yaw pairs fit easily along the 1 m length. The roll pair has only the 0.5 × 0.7 m cross-section, so it has to sit on two corners, canted (0.43 m at most). Thrusters firing straight out of a face would get only 0.35 m and spend 14 % more gas per roll dump. The inset draws the couples where the model puts them. The disturbance is a constant torque vector fixed in the inertial frame, along inertial x, which is the pitch wheel's axis at the start.

**State and equations.** Attitude is a unit quaternion q mapping body to inertial axes, with q̇ = ½ q ⊗ ω. H is the total angular momentum of body plus wheels in body axes. h holds the three wheels' *absolute* axial momenta, I_w·(body rate + wheel spin). ω = (H − h)/J, with J the body's inertia excluding each wheel's axial inertia (about 10⁻⁴ less than the locked value). Euler's equation is Ḣ = −ω × H + τ_ext. Motor torques change h only (ḣ = τ_motor) and never touch H, so with no external torque the inertial momentum R(q)·H is constant. With these definitions the equations are exact for symmetric wheels, and a test checks them against a model written in relative wheel speeds. (Read h as momentum relative to the body and J as the locked inertia, as my first comments did, and they are off by O(I_w/J): the wheel equation would need a −I_w·ω̇ term and the energy a cross term ω·h.) The ledger on the page is exactly the conservation statement: R(q)·H = H₀ + ∫τ_dist dt + ∫R·τ_thrusters dt, with the right-hand side accumulated independently at every kick.

**Integrator.** Fixed step of 0.02 s (also the controller's sample rate). Kick–drift–kick: torques are half-kicks on H (external) and h (motor), and the drift is the torque-free gyrostat split into three single-axis pieces (the explicit Lie–Poisson splitting of McLachlan, *Phys. Rev. Lett.* 71, 1993, "Explicit Lie-Poisson integration and the Euler equations", often credited jointly to McLachlan and Reich): the flow of (H_i − h_i)²/2J_i alone is an exact rotation of the body about axis i by θ = ω_i·dt, under which H rotates by −θ about the same axis and h is unchanged. The pieces are arranged as a Strang step (1, 2, 3, 2, 1) and composed with Yoshida's 4th-order weights. Each piece preserves |H| and R·H to round-off, so inertial momentum is conserved exactly in the torque-free case; kinetic energy has a bounded, non-growing error. The cos/sin pair used to rotate H is formed from the same half-angle sin/cos that builds the quaternion increment, so the two updates agree to the last bit. The quaternion is renormalised every step. Overall the scheme is 2nd order in the torques and 4th order in the free rotation.

**Controller (the autopilot).** Attitude error as a rotation vector from q⁻¹ ⊗ q_target; a per-axis slew profile ω_cmd = min(√(2a|e|), ω_max) with a linear region near zero (a = 0.7·τ_max/J, ω_max = 0.8·h_max/J); a rate loop α = 4·(ω_cmd − ω) capped at τ_max/J; motor torque τ_m = −ω × H − J·α + τ_thrusters, i.e. gyroscopic decoupling, rate loop and thruster feed-forward, clipped at ±τ_max and at the wheel's capacity. If the body spins faster than 2.5 times the slowest axis's ω_max (0.133 rad/s, 7.6°/s) the autopilot first nulls the rate ("detumbling"), then holds wherever it stopped.

The autopilot only tries to hold a target the wheels can actually hold. At rest at the target they would have to carry the whole momentum, h = R(q_target)ᵀ·H_inertial. If any component of that is over capacity, the hold is lost: the autopilot stops steering toward the target and only commands zero rate. A full wheel then stays pinned and the body takes the excess, ω = (H − h_max)/J, so the sky drifts, faster and faster. The autopilot picks the target up again when the momentum fits. That happens after the thrusters run, or when you point somewhere the wheels can carry the load: they hold up to 0.87 N·m·s along a body diagonal. While the hold is lost, a new drag starts from what you can see. The first version had no such check, so its PD law kept chasing an unreachable target. Once the error passed 180° it went "the other way round": it spun the full wheel down to about 0.12 N·m·s, whipped the body round at 3–7°/s, then refilled the wheel. It looked like a free way to empty a wheel.

"Empty the wheels" fires each axis's thruster couple against the sign of the total body-frame momentum until it is below 1 % of a wheel's capacity. In raw mode the drag sets the motor torques directly and nothing else runs.

**Propellant.** Two thrusters, each 0.4 m from the axis, fire as a couple, so the momentum dumped per kilogram is arm × Isp × g₀ = 255 N·m·s/kg. Mass flow is |τ|/(arm·Isp·g₀). The page's lifetime is propellant × 255 / disturbance torque. That assumes the momentum to dump lies along one thruster axis, as it does if you hold the starting attitude. Each axis's pair pays for its own component, so the same momentum along a body diagonal costs up to √3 times as much gas (1.72× measured).

**Clock.** Wall time accumulates in a counter and the physics advances only in whole 0.02 s steps (at most 3,000 per frame; a slow device slows down rather than spiralling), so the trajectory for a given sequence of commands is bit-identical at any frame rate.

**What's simplified.** The disturbance is a constant inertial vector. Real solar-pressure, gravity-gradient, drag and magnetic torques depend on attitude and orbit position. The constant is the simplest model with the one property that matters here: it never averages to zero. The slider's top end, 100 µN·m, is there to speed things up, not because a craft this size would see it. Wheels have a hard momentum cap and no friction, no torque fall-off with speed, no quantisation. Thrusters are ideal couples with no minimum impulse bit. The body is rigid, with no flexible appendages or sloshing. So a spin about the long axis is shown as steady. With any energy loss it would slowly drift into a spin about the short axis, as Explorer 1's did. The controller is a textbook PD with gyroscopic feed-forward, not a flight design. Stars, guide stars and the sun's position are invented. Time runs faster than real (10× by default), and the "raw torque" mode is a counterfactual: real spacecraft never fly without a rate loop. The tumble presets inject momentum directly (the ledger restarts from there).

## Honest assessment

What works:
- [Certain] The dynamics are the standard gyrostat equations, with h the wheels' absolute axial momentum and J the body's inertia without the wheel axes. The tests above check them against an independent integrator, a model written in relative wheel speeds, closed-form precession and instability rates, exact single-axis results, and the conservation of inertial momentum. The live ledger is computed by two routes and agrees to round-off.
- [Certain] Every number quoted on the page (13.9 h, 6,000 rpm, 1.96 g, 590 days / 1.6 years, 16 years at 1 µN·m, 0.87 along a diagonal, 1.8 N·m·s for the tumble, the 113 s flips, 17°/s) is recomputed by the test file from the same constants.
- [Certain] The trajectory for a given sequence of commands is bit-identical at any frame rate, and the page never changes the physics step.
- [Likely] The drag conveys the central idea without equations: the sky is heavy because something has to take the opposite spin, and the gauges show exactly what did. The "move the load" moment, where a turn shifts stored momentum from one wheel to another because the momentum stays fixed in the sky, is the part I find most satisfying.

What's rough:
- [Certain] The ledger residual is not zero and grows linearly with simulated time: about 1.3e-9 N·m·s per simulated day at 100 µN·m, from the rounding bias of adding tiny kicks to a large total on both sides of the equation. The page prints it rather than hiding it and only colours it red above 1e-6. It is round-off, not physics, but it is also not "exact".
- [Certain] The ledger checks conservation and the bookkeeping, not the rest of the physics. Motor torques never touch H, so it would balance even with the wrong motor sign. The RK4 and relative-coordinates comparisons and the closed-form tests are what check the dynamics.
- [Certain] Parameters are plausible for a microsatellite, not taken from any real vehicle: the inertias are a rounded uniform box, and the wheel and thruster figures are mid-range catalogue values from memory. (A reviewer found a 0.5 N·m·s / 25 mN·m wheel and a 20 mN·m wheel on the market, and cold-gas Isp of 45–75 s.)
- [Certain] The default 10 µN·m is not sunlight on this craft. My first README called it solar pressure on "a few square metres with a centimetres-scale offset". That works out to 1–2 µN·m, not 10. On the bare box as drawn, sunlight does even less. A uniform box with its centre of mass in the middle feels no sunlight torque at all, and with the centre of mass 3 cm off on each axis it is at most 0.27 µN·m. Reaching 10 µN·m from sunlight takes something like a 3 m² one-sided solar array with its centre of pressure 0.46 m off the centre of mass, and the drawing has no array. The slider is now a lumped "disturbance torque".
- [Likely] As a lumped disturbance, 10 µN·m is on the high side for a craft this size. Gravity gradient at 500 km for these inertias peaks at 7.3 µN·m, but its orbit-averaged part, the bit that piles up in the wheels, is at most 3.7 µN·m. Drag and a residual magnetic dipole add their own, and I have not sized those. The 14-hour fill and the 590-day tank both scale as 1/torque. At 1 µN·m they become 5.8 days and 16 years.
- [Certain] A constant inertial torque is a caricature. Real disturbance torques rotate with the orbit and depend on which face is lit; some average out and some don't. The caricature keeps the one feature the toy is about (a bias that never averages to zero) and loses everything else.
- [Certain] The autopilot is simple. It lands slews accurately but takes 1.18× the eigenaxis bang-bang time. A true three-axis time-optimal slew is faster still: Bilimoria and Wie (*J. Guid. Control Dyn.* 16(3), 1993) showed eigenaxis rotations are not time-optimal, by about 10 % in their case (I have this from secondary sources, not the paper). It has no feed-forward of the disturbance, which a real estimator would provide. When the wheels can't hold the target it just gives up and damps the rate. A real system would steer the momentum into the wheels' envelope (the page lets you do that by hand) and start desaturation automatically. The "detumble" threshold, the hold-lost rule and the thruster deadband are design choices, not derived.
- [Certain] The 0.4 m thruster arm on all three axes is an idealisation. For roll it needs canted corner thrusters, at most 0.43 m on this cross-section. Face-mounted ones would get 0.35 m and spend 14 % more gas per roll dump.
- [Certain] 17°/s is a fast tumble to show from inside; the sky streaks. At 1× the flip takes a couple of minutes to develop, which is honest but slow, so the demo nudges you to 10×.
- [Likely] In raw mode the joystick mapping (80 px for full torque) and the 10° roll nudge are tuning choices; people with trackpads may find raw mode twitchy.
- [Likely] The integrator's pedigree: a web search confirmed McLachlan's 1993 paper and that the three-piece rigid-body splitting appears there as an example, and confirmed Reich's "Momentum conserving symplectic integrators" (*Physica D* 76, 1994) exists. Secondary sources credit the splitting to both; the reviewer found a 2019 paper crediting McLachlan 1993, Reich 1994 and Touma & Wisdom 1994 together. I could not check Reich's full text.
- [Speculative] Players may read the gold arrow in the inset as a force or a direction the craft "wants" to point. It is the total angular momentum, and its only property that matters is that it doesn't move.
- Rendering is plain Canvas 2D: ~7,000 star transforms per frame, fine on everything I tried headless, but I could not measure a real phone.

## Where it could go next

- An **orbit** underneath: gravity-gradient and solar torques that actually depend on attitude and position, so some disturbances average out over an orbit and some secretly don't. Momentum management then becomes a real design problem.
- **Magnetorquers** as a second way to pay: free momentum dumping against the planet's field, but only about axes perpendicular to the field, so part of the ledger can never be settled that way.
- **Four wheels in a pyramid**: redundancy, null-space momentum you can hide, and the moment one wheel fails.
- **Control-moment gyros**, which trade torque amplification for the gimbal-lock singularities that make their steering laws famous.
- A **sound layer**: each wheel's rpm as a faint pitch, so a filling wheel is something you hear climbing before you look at the bar.

## Revision note

An independent reviewer rebuilt the physics in separate code. Every headline dynamics number reproduced, but the review found real problems, now fixed:

- **The page said a wheel "keeps whatever it was lent" after a turn.** With no outside push, a body at rest has wheels at zero. The wheel carries the turn only while the body turns, then hands it back. The page and the first "thing to try" now say so, and a test drags, stops, and checks the wheels return to < 1e-12.
- **The long and short axis labels were swapped.** The drawn box is 0.5 (x) × 0.7 (y) × 1.0 (z) m, so x is the short side (most inertia) and z the long side (least inertia). The buttons, toast, README and test log now follow the geometry, and a test ties the labels to the box. The page also notes that "steady" holds only for a rigid body (Explorer 1).
- **After a wheel filled, the autopilot did not just lose the hold.** Past 180° of error it chased the target "the other way round", unloading the "full" wheel to about 0.12 N·m·s into a 3–7°/s body whip, then refilling it. My README's one-hour snapshot happened to catch it refilled. The autopilot now holds only targets the wheels can hold. Otherwise it damps the rate, so the full wheel stays pinned and the sky drifts at exactly (H − h_max)/J. The steady-torque test now checks this over the whole hour and the return to target after desaturation. Pointing error an hour after saturation moved from 57° to 19° (the sky has turned 1,061°). The page-run numbers under the verification table come from a new, longer run (4.8 g for the desaturation, not 3.1 g).
- **The 10 µN·m default was called sunlight.** On the box as drawn, sunlight gives at most about 0.3 µN·m, more than 30× less, and even my README's own sizing gave only 1–2 µN·m. The slider and ledger now say "disturbance torque", with honest sizing from first principles (now a test). The default is described as on the high side, with the 1 µN·m figures (5.8 days, 16 years) next to it. The 13.9 h / 590 day headline numbers are unchanged but now labelled as belonging to that high default.
- **Smaller fixes:**
  - The slew row's "time-optimal bound" is now the eigenaxis bang-bang time, with the rate cap credited to the controller.
  - The code comments now define h as the wheels' absolute momentum and J as excluding their axial inertia; the old wording was off by O(I_w/J). A new relative-coordinates test pins this.
  - The inset draws the thruster couples at the modelled 0.4 m arm, and the README says roll needs canted corner thrusters.
  - The README notes that the ledger can't catch a motor-sign error, and that off-axis momentum costs up to √3 more gas (now tested).
