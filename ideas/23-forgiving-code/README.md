# The Forgiving Code
> One wrong letter in a codon usually lands you on a similar amino acid. Shuffle the genetic code and that luck vanishes: it is one-in-ten-thousand luck.

**Kind:** explorable
**Run:** open `index.html` in a browser (plain classic scripts, so it works from `file://` or any static server; nothing is fetched). Tests, from the repo root: `node --test ideas/23-forgiving-code/code.test.mjs` (Node 18+, about 7 s; it scores over four million random codes) and `node --test ideas/23-forgiving-code/ui.test.mjs` (about 6 s; drives the page in headless Chromium through the global Playwright that `tools/smoke.mjs` uses, and skips itself without it).
**Made by:** Codon Wobble

## What it is

The genetic code, drawn as the usual 4 × 4 × 4 table but coloured by chemistry, with three things to do to it:

1. **Look at it as a landscape.** Each codon's cell is coloured by its amino acid's *polar requirement* (how water-loving it is; Woese 1966), amber for hydrophobic, blue for polar. The real code is smooth: the whole middle-U column is amber, half of the boxes of four are a single colour, neighbouring boxes hold neighbouring chemistry. Hover or tap any codon to see where its nine one-letter typos land and by how much the chemistry moves. Then press **Shuffle the amino acids**. The table turns to static, and the score (mean squared change in polar requirement per typo) roughly doubles. Press **Improve** and watch simulated annealing smooth the static into a landscape gentler than the real one.
2. **Deal the amino acids a million ways.** A histogram of random codes, each keeping the real code's blocks of synonymous codons and its three stops, with only the amino-acid-to-block assignment shuffled. The real code sits far in the gentle tail: roughly 1 in 10,000 random codes beat it. That is the round figure; my samples give about 1 in 8,000. Freeland & Hurst 1998 and Geyer & Madany Mamlouk 2018 each sampled a million codes and report P = 0.0001; Haig & Hurst's 10,000 codes gave 0.02%. Switch the weighting to Freeland & Hurst's, which counts each kind of typo by their estimate of how often ribosomes make it, and it becomes a few in a million.
3. **The same typos, two dictionaries.** A real human mRNA (preproinsulin or β-globin, from RefSeq) takes random single-letter typos. Two panels read the *same* mutating message, one with the real code, one with the shuffled code. Both panels see exactly the same silent typos, because the shuffle keeps the redundancy. All of the difference is how far each missense typo moves the chemistry, and you can watch one protein keep its colours while the other turns to noise. For β-globin there is a button for the real sickle-cell mutation (GAG→GUG, Glu→Val), a reminder that the real code is forgiving on average, not always.

The part that can be proven is the score and the one-in-N results; the test suite recomputes them from the raw tables. The part that can only be felt is the two tables side by side, and the two proteins.

## Why I made it

I was handed a list of molecular machines to animate and kept coming back to the thing that is not a machine at all: the dictionary. Everyone learns that the code is redundant, and most people learn it as trivia, "61 codons for 20 amino acids". What I did not appreciate until I computed it is that the redundancy is the smaller half of the story. Hold the redundancy fixed, shuffle only which amino acid goes with which block of codons, and almost every shuffle is worse than the real thing at absorbing typos. A typo in the real code moves polar requirement by 5.19 squared units on average; a random deal moves it by 9.4. That is a fact about an arrangement, not about chemistry or physics, and it is one that a hostile reviewer can recompute in a few lines.

Before settling I listed candidates and crossed off the ones any agent would reach for first: Hodgkin–Huxley (listed in my brief, and everyone's first pick), the repressilator, chemotaxis run-and-tumble, Michaelis–Menten sliders, "why cells are small" diffusion, HP-lattice folding, and kinetic proofreading (a wonderful mechanism, but its feel is weak and its ribosome rate constants are contested). The code's error-minimization won because it is surprising, because the published numbers are specific enough to be checked (5.19; 263 pairs; 0.02%; one in a million), and because it has a strong feel: a smooth table turning to static.

## How it works

**The model** (`code.js`, shared by the page and the tests; no DOM).

- *The code.* NCBI translation table 1 as a 64-letter string in UCAG order. The 61 sense codons form 20 blocks, one per amino acid (serine, leucine and arginine each own six codons). The three stops are fixed in every code considered.
- *Properties.* Polar requirement (Woese and colleagues' two 1966 papers, PNAS 55:966 and Cold Spring Harb Symp Quant Biol 31:723; the values used by Haig & Hurst and reprinted in Geyer & Madany Mamlouk 2018, Table 1): Ala 7.0, Arg 9.1, Asn 10.0, Asp 13.0, Cys 4.8, Gln 8.6, Glu 12.5, Gly 7.9, His 8.4, Ile 4.9, Leu 4.9, Lys 10.1, Met 5.3, Phe 5.0, Pro 6.6, Ser 7.5, Thr 6.6, Trp 5.2, Tyr 5.4, Val 5.6. Hydropathy (Kyte & Doolittle 1982): Ile 4.5 … Arg −4.5.
- *Substitutions.* Every single-base change from a sense codon to a sense codon: 526 ordered (263 unordered pairs, Haig & Hurst's "263 single-step connections"). Another 23 go into a stop and are excluded from the score, as in the papers. Of the 526, 134 are synonymous: 8 at the first position (the Leu and Arg six-codon families), 0 at the second, 126 of 176 at the third.
- *The score.* MS = Σ w·(p<sub>from</sub> − p<sub>to</sub>)² / Σ w over the 526 substitutions. With w = 1 and polar requirement this is Haig & Hurst's MS<sub>0</sub>; the real code gives **5.1936**, which rounds to their published **5.19**. By position: 4.88 / 10.56 / 0.14. The Freeland & Hurst (1998) weighting is w = 1 / 0.5 / 1 for transitions and 0.5 / 0.1 / 1 for transversions at codon positions 1 / 2 / 3 (as tabulated in Geyer & Madany Mamlouk 2018, Table 2); the real code gives **2.628**.
- *Random codes.* A uniformly random permutation of the 20 amino acids over the 20 blocks (Fisher–Yates), the convention of both papers. Because stops are fixed and synonymous substitutions contribute zero under every permutation, the score of a code depends only on the total weight connecting each pair of blocks, which is precomputed once; a million codes take under a second in Node.
- *Better codes.* Simulated annealing over swaps of two amino acids. In 120 restarts of 200,000 steps, 67 end at 3.4886 (unweighted) and none lower; 35 end at 1.4874 (weighted) and none lower. For hydropathy the corresponding minima are 5.3176 and 1.9005. The first of the four is the known optimum. Goldman (1993) found the best code for this unweighted measure by a heuristic search. Buhrman et al. (2011) cast the problem as a quadratic assignment problem and proved that code is the global optimum of this fixed-block model. My best code scores 2.194 under the FH weighting and 3.508 with transitions weighted twice; Geyer & Madany Mamlouk list Goldman's code at 2.19 and 3.50 (their Table 8). The other three minima are only the best I found; the search space is 20! and I have not enumerated it.
- *The storm.* The real coding sequences of human INS (NM_000207.3, 110 codons + stop) and HBB (NM_000518.5, 147 codons + stop). A typo picks a sense codon uniformly, then one of its neighbours, equally under the uniform scheme or in proportion to the FH weights. Neighbours that are stop codons are skipped, as the score skips them (an earlier version allowed them, and one early nonsense typo dimmed the rest of the protein for the rest of the round, which taught nothing about the arrangement). Both panels translate the same codon list; drift is the mean over residues of (current − original)² in the property. Each round is 60 typos. Sixty draws from a heavy-tailed distribution is a noisy sample (one round in my testing came out 4.02 versus 4.26), so each panel also keeps a running tally across rounds, "mean squared jump per typo so far", beside its exact expectation for that gene's own codons (`geneScore`: a sense codon uniformly, then a non-stop neighbour with the scheme's weights). Those expectations are 5.62 for the INS codons and 6.11 for HBB under the real code, against 5.19 for the table as a whole, because real genes use codons unequally; a random code averages 9.31 on the INS codons. That last figure is exact: under a random deal each non-synonymous neighbour costs the mean squared difference over the 380 ordered pairs of distinct amino acids, 12.63, and synonymous neighbours cost nothing.

**What's simplified.** Random codes keep the real code's block structure and stops; the redundancy is never varied, only the arrangement. One property at a time, and squared change is a cost, not a fitness. Typos are substitutions only, uniform over codons; real mutation and misreading spectra are biased, and real genes use some synonymous codons far more than others. The colour scale is OKLCH with fixed lightness, hue from amber to blue; it is a presentation choice. The term "typo" covers both a mutation in the gene and a misreading by the ribosome; the score is the same for both because it is a property of the table.

## Honest assessment

What works:
- [Certain] The real code's MS<sub>0</sub> with polar requirement is 5.19, as Haig & Hurst published, and the random-code mean and SD (9.41 ± 1.51) match the independent million-code re-sampling by Geyer & Madany Mamlouk (9.42 ± 1.51). Roughly 1 in 10,000 random codes beat it on this measure (about 1 in 8,000 in my samples; both published million-code samples give P = 0.0001); under the Freeland & Hurst weighting the real code scores 2.63 and only a few in a million beat it. Every one of these is recomputed by the test from the raw tables, by a slower route than the page uses.
- [Certain] The two real coding sequences are the RefSeq ones and translate to the RefSeq proteins; the sickle-cell mutation is applied at the right codon (c.20A>T, codon 7 of the CDS, β6 in mature numbering).
- [Certain] In the storm, the silent typos are identical in both panels for any shuffled code, because the shuffle preserves the blocks (and nonsense outcomes would be identical too, because the stops are fixed). The page says this, and a test checks it.
- [Certain] Freeland & Hurst's own weighted value for the natural code is 2.63, and they found 1 better code in a million. Geyer & Madany Mamlouk (Table 6) list both from the 1998 paper beside their own recomputation: 2.63, and 2 better in a million (2.8 per million over ten million). The weights I use are the ones G&MM tabulate, and they give 2.63. I have not read the 1998 paper itself.
- [Likely] The smooth-versus-static table and the two proteins get the idea across without the formula.

What's rough:
- [Certain] "One in ten thousand" is not "optimal". Annealing finds codes a third gentler (3.49 vs 5.19 unweighted, 1.49 vs 2.63 weighted). [Likely] 3.49 is the true optimum of the unweighted measure. Goldman (1993) found the optimal code and Buhrman et al. (2011) proved it optimal. My best code matches the weighted score G&MM list for Goldman's (2.19), and in 120 long runs nothing went lower. I have the proof from the Buhrman abstract and G&MM's account, not from the paper itself.
- [Likely] This matches the literature's later consensus (for example Novozhilov, Wolf & Koonin 2007, *Biology Direct* 2:24; Massey 2008, *J Mol Evol* 67:510): the real code is far better than random but far from the best. Whether that is adaptation, a frozen accident, a by-product of how the code grew, or a stereochemical constraint is unsettled. I have read these papers' abstracts, not the full texts. The page names the main positions, each with a citation: Crick's frozen accident, selection against errors, coevolution with amino-acid biosynthesis, stereochemistry, and neutral emergence. It also says that it measures the pattern, not its origin.
- [Certain] The result depends on the property and on the weighting. With every typo counted equally, Kyte–Doolittle hydropathy gives about 1 in 130 here, not 1 in 10,000. Under the Freeland & Hurst weighting it gives about 1 in 10,000, against a few in a million for polar requirement.
- [Certain] Haig & Hurst tested four properties: polar requirement, hydropathy, molecular volume and isoelectric point. Their abstract singles out polar requirement and hydropathy. An earlier version of this page said they found the code robust for volume too. I had that from secondary sources, and it does not hold up.
- [Likely] Recomputed here with the same method, molecular volume shows only a weak signal and isoelectric point none. On volume, 6.9% of random codes beat the real code with Zamyatnin's 1972 scale and 10.8% with Grantham's 1974 scale; on isoelectric point, 58.5% do. I typed these scales from the standard tables and did not check them against the originals here. They are in the test, not on the page. That the property on which the code looks best is a 1966 chromatography measure is a point critics have raised.
- [Likely] The attribution of the polar-requirement values to the specific 1966 paper: Woese's group published two papers that year, and I took the twenty numbers from Geyer & Madany Mamlouk's reprint rather than from either original.
- [Certain] The random-code model is the field's convention, not the only reasonable one. Holding the blocks fixed means the result is about the assignment of amino acids to blocks, nothing more.
- [Likely] The "feel" panel, the storm, uses mutation to show what is really a joint property of mutation and mistranslation. I use the word "typo" to cover both and say so.
- [Certain] A single 60-typo round can fail to show the effect; the tally across rounds is what converges. [Likely] The direction of the codon-usage effect in my two genes (5.62 and 6.11, worse than the table's 5.19) agrees with Archetti (2004, "Codon usage bias and mutation constraints reduce the level of error minimization of the genetic code", *J Mol Evol* 59:258–266). He argued that codon usage lowers the real code's apparent error minimization. I have read his abstract but have not checked his numbers.
- [Speculative] Some viewers may read the amber-to-blue colours as "good to bad". They are hydrophobic to polar; the legend says so, but colour carries connotations.
- The histogram's bar heights are log-scaled so the tail near the real code is visible; a linear scale makes the real code's position dramatic but the tail invisible. The axis label says so.

## Where it could go next

- A **frameshift** mode: Geyer & Madany Mamlouk 2018 score codes for robustness to +1/−1 slips; the storm already has the codon list it would need.
- **Relax the block structure**: let random codes have random degeneracy patterns as well, to separate "the redundancy helps" from "the arrangement helps".
- **Codon usage**: weight each codon by how often human genes use it (Archetti 2004 argues this lowers the real code's apparent robustness).
- **The twenty-first and twenty-second amino acids** (selenocysteine, pyrrolysine) and the non-standard mitochondrial codes, which reassign stop codons and so would need the stops to move.
- **Sound**: play each typo as a pitch jump proportional to Δ, so the real code sounds like a melody with small steps and the shuffled one like noise.

## Revision note

An independent reviewer rebuilt the model in separate code. It reproduced every headline number: 5.19 and 2.63, about 1 in 8,500 random codes better, a few in a million under the weighting, and the minima 3.4886 and 1.4874. It also found three bugs in the page and several overstatements and citation slips. All are now fixed:

- **The stop-codon inspector was wrong.** Hovering UAA said all nine neighbours "read on", but two of them (UAG, UGA) are stops; UAG and UGA each have one stop neighbour. `effect()` now has a stop-to-stop case, and the inspector counts it.
- **The sickle-cell button biased the tally.** It fed a hand-picked typo into the random-typo tally (47.61 after one typo) and used up one of the round's 60 typos. It now changes the protein but stays out of the counts and the tally.
- **The Weighting menu overflowed every phone width.** It was 519 px wide. The smoke test missed it, because mobile emulation widens the viewport to fit. The menus now shrink, and `ui.test.mjs` checks 320–414 px with and without that emulation.
- **I overstated the robustness across measures.** The page said Haig & Hurst found the code robust for molecular volume, but their abstract does not say so. Recomputed here, about 1 random code in 10 beats the real code on volume, and more than half do on isoelectric point. The hydropathy figure of 1 in 130 holds only with every typo equal; under the Freeland & Hurst weighting it is about 1 in 10,000.
- **3.49 is the known optimum, not just my best.** Goldman found the optimal code in 1993, and Buhrman et al. proved it optimal in 2011. My best code matches the scores G&MM list for Goldman's. Both papers are now cited.
- **The page now names the competing explanations,** including Crick's frozen accident, and cites them.
- **Found while fixing these: tapping a codon did nothing on a phone.** The tap's emulated mouseenter focused the codon, and the click then toggled it straight off. A desktop click did the same. A tap or click now pins the codon, and a second one unpins it.
- **Smaller fixes:** Geyer & Madany Mamlouk found 124 better codes, not 114, and the second author's name is Madany Mamlouk. Archetti's 2004 title is corrected. A random code averages 9.31 on the INS codons, not 9.35. The "1 in 10,000" sentence now cites the papers that support it and gives my own figure, about 1 in 8,000. Freeland & Hurst's 2.63 is now [Certain], from G&MM's Table 6.
