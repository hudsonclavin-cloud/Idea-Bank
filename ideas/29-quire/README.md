# Quire
> Fold any PDF into a booklet with the printer you already have.

**Kind:** tool
**Run:** open `ideas/29-quire/index.html` in a browser. It works straight from disk: no server, no build, no network, nothing uploaded. Type a page count, or drop a PDF on it. `probe.html` is the two-sheet printer test. Tests, from the repo root: `node --test ideas/29-quire/impose.test.mjs ideas/29-quire/preview.test.mjs` (Node 22; when poppler's `pdftotext` and `pdfinfo` are installed, the tests also read every reordered PDF back with them, and when Playwright is installed, `preview.test.mjs` drives the page itself in headless Chromium).
**Made by:** Bone Folder

## What it is

A booklet is a stack of sheets folded once down the middle, so each sheet carries four pages and they have to be printed two to a side in an order that looks like nonsense: `24,1,2,23,22,3,4,21,…`. Every home printer can make one, but the step in between, the *imposition*, is where people lose an evening and a ream of paper.

Quire does that step. You tell it how many pages the document has (or drop the PDF in, and it counts), whether you want one stapled booklet or sewn signatures of *n* sheets, whether the printer does both sides itself, and which way the book reads. It gives you:

- **The page order**, as a string to paste into a print dialog's "Pages" box, with the settings to go with it (2 per sheet, two-sided, flipped on the short edge). Per-signature strings, a cover-sheet-only string, and two-pass strings for single-sided printers.
- **A reordered copy of your PDF.** For apps whose dialogs sort the page list (browsers appear to), or when you simply don't want to type: drop the file, click Save, and you get your PDF with the pages already in booklet order and the blank padding pages inserted. It is made in the page, with no library, by appending a short update to your file; the original bytes are untouched.
- **A preview that folds.** The printed sheets are drawn as they should come out of the printer, fanned so you can read every front; press Fold and they fold together into a booklet; then turn the pages and watch 1, 2, 3… come out in order, blanks included. The page-turn is not drawn by counting: it prints your page order on the same model printer the tests use, folds the result with the same simulator and draws whatever that reads, so a wrong order would show up as wrong numbers. It checks the order, not your printer; that is the probe's job.
- **A probe**: a two-sheet, eight-page booklet already in print order. Print it (two-sided on the short edge, one page per sheet) and fold it, and it tells you whether your driver's "short edge" is the right one, or, for a single-sided printer, which way to turn the stack between passes. Its table names each symptom: a wrong flip, for example, puts 7 behind the cover, upside down, with 2, 4 and 5 also upside down.

## Why I made it

I wanted a thing that is useful on the day you find it. Someone, right now, is trying to print a 22-page PDF as a little A5 booklet for a wedding, a zine, a knitting pattern, a liturgy, a kid's story, and is about to discover that "booklet mode" in their print dialog doesn't exist, doesn't work with their PDF, or prints half the pages upside down. The arithmetic is a few lines. What makes it hard is everything around the arithmetic: the padding to a multiple of four, the fact that a print dialog cannot print a page that isn't there, the two meanings of "flip", the apps that silently sort your carefully typed page list, and the single-sided printers where the second pass depends on which way you turned the stack.

The thing I most wanted to exist was the reordered-PDF part done honestly: no upload, no 500 KB library, no re-rendering of the file. PDF has had the answer since 1993, an *incremental update*, where you append new copies of only the objects you change. A 24-page booklet from a file with a flat page tree needs about 300 bytes; the update grows by about 9 bytes a page, plus a fresh copy of each page dictionary (typically 150 to 300 bytes each) when the file's page tree has to be flattened, as files saved from Chrome do. Either way it is small. I liked that very much.

The brief for this batch asked for a part that can be proven and a part that can only be felt. The proof is the fold: a simulator that folds a signature from the physical description and reads it, and a model of a two-up duplex printer, so the tests check the print string by printing it and folding the result. The felt part is the preview, when the fanned sheets with their absurd numbers close up and the pages start to turn in order.

## How it works

**The order** (`impose.js`, pure, shared by the page and the tests). A padded book of *N* pages in a signature of *S* sheets: sheet *j* (outermost first) carries pages `first = 2j+1` and `last = N−2j` on its front, as `[last | first]`, and `[first+1 | last−1]` on its back. For a right-to-left book the halves are mirrored. Blanks can go at the end, just before the last page (so your last page stays the back cover), or inside the front cover. Signatures are consecutive runs of *S* sheets; the last takes what is left.

**Two simulators** check it. `readingOrder` folds a signature with the front outward and reads it, outermost front-right half, then the back of that half, inward to the middle and out again, exactly as a hand would. `printerModel` takes a page sequence and does what a printer does when told "2 per sheet, two-sided, short edge": 1st page front-left, 2nd front-right, 3rd and 4th on the back, left and right as seen after the sheet is turned over around its short edge. The tests build every plan for 1–120 pages, both reading directions, three blank placements and seven binding variants (over 4,000 plans), print each one with the printer model, fold the result and require it to read 1…N. The two-pass single-sided model is checked both ways round. The classic strings (`8,1,2,7,6,3,4,5`; the 16-page one) are checked literally.

**The PDF** (`pdfupdate.js`, also pure, also dependency-free, with its own small inflate). A PDF's pages are the leaves of a tree hanging off the catalog's `/Pages` object. The reorderer scans the whole file for `N G obj` and keeps the last definition of every object number (which is what readers do when they repair a file), unpacks compressed object streams (`/ObjStm`, Flate, with PNG predictors if any), merges every trailer it finds (linearized files split `/Root` and `/Size` across two), walks the tree collecting inherited `MediaBox`, `CropBox`, `Rotate` and `Resources`, and then appends an update: a new root `/Pages` with `/Kids` in booklet order, a fresh copy of each page that used to hang off an in-between node (with the attributes it inherited written in, so flattening loses nothing), one empty page object per blank, and a cross-reference table or stream to match the file's own style, with `/Prev` pointing back at the old one. Encrypted files work, whether or not they need a password to open (Quire never decrypts anything, and the copy keeps the same protection), as long as no rewritten dictionary holds a string (strings would need encrypting); encrypted files whose page tree lives inside object streams are refused with a message that says to print to a new PDF first. Every output is read back by the reorderer's own parser in the tests, and by poppler when it is installed.

**The preview** is one SVG drawn from the plan: a fan of the first signature's sheets (front sides, outer sheet on top, each captioned with its back), a fold animation in which the non-cover halves rotate behind, the fore-edges of nested sheets creeping out of the closed booklet, and a page-turn animation whose spreads come from `printAndRead`: the page's own print string run through `printerModel` (or the two-pass model), grouped into its signatures and read with `readBook`. The troubleshooting note's example (which page a wrong flip puts behind the cover) comes from `wrongFlip`, the same printer with every back turned half a turn. It honours `prefers-reduced-motion` by jumping straight to the result.

**The probe** is four landscape pages carrying `8|1`, `2|7`, `6|3`, `4|5` with "this way up" arrows and fold lines, so it needs no page list and prints the same from any app.

## Honest assessment

- [Certain] The page orders are right. They are checked by simulation against a printer model, and the standard sequences match those published in GNOME's own booklet-printing documentation for Evince.
- [Certain] The reordered PDF is the original file plus an appended update; the first *n* bytes of the output are the input, and the tests assert it. In the shipped tests, synthetic PDFs of six shapes (xref tables, xref streams with packed object streams, nested trees with inherited attributes, wrong `/Length`, junk after deflate data, a linearized-style split trailer) all read back correctly with the module's own parser and with poppler. Outside the shipped tests, I also ran 42 variants of six real-world files (ReportLab, LibreOffice, Chromium/Skia, pypdf, and qpdf re-saves of each with object streams, linearization and AES encryption) through three independent readers, poppler, pypdf and qpdf's `--check`; 36 passed with zero warnings, and the other six were encrypted files with object streams, which are refused on purpose with a clear message.
- [Certain] The Chromium file exercised the tree-flattening path for real: Skia builds a tree with eight kids per node, so all eleven pages were re-parented and still rendered with their resources.
- [Likely] "Flip on the short edge" is the right two-sided setting for a two-up landscape sheet. It is the standard advice and what the GNOME documentation uses, but drivers name the options inconsistently, and I have no printer in this sandbox. That is exactly why the probe exists: your printer gets the last word, at a cost of two sheets. The probe is a one-up landscape job, though, and a booklet is two portrait pages to a side; a driver that applies "short edge" relative to the document could treat the two differently. So the page also suggests printing the real job's first sheet on its own before a long run.
- [Certain] The upside-down and out-of-place symptoms in the probe's tables, and the page's wrong-flip example, are simulated, not guessed. A wrong flip (or a stack re-fed rotated) turns every back half a turn, so in the probe 2, 4, 5 and 7 come out upside down and 7 lands behind the cover; the tests check the probe's rows and the page's example against that simulation. Whether a real printer's paper path produces exactly these cases is the [Speculative] item below.
- [Likely] Browser print dialogs sort a custom page list into document order rather than printing it as typed. I could not find documentation either way; I am going from memory of Chromium's print-preview code, and I say "appear to" on the page. The reordered PDF sidesteps the question entirely.
- [Likely] Creep is about a paper thickness per nested sheet, and more than about eight nested sheets of office paper is unpleasant to fold and staple; the page warns at nine. These are rules of thumb, not measurements.
- [Speculative] The single-sided two-pass instructions cover the three things I know can go wrong (stack turned the wrong way, rotated, or reversed by a face-up tray), and the last two together. There may be printers whose paths produce a combination I have not described; the probe will still show *something* is wrong, but the diagnosis table might not name it.
- What's rough: the typed route cannot invent blank pages, so you have to add them to the document yourself (the page says where). The reordered PDF leaves orphaned page-tree nodes in the file, which is harmless but means a fussy optimizer may report unreferenced objects. Only the first signature, and at most eight of its sheets, is drawn in the preview. Pages of different sizes in one PDF will be scaled oddly by any 2-up print, and Quire does nothing about that. There is no quarto (two folds) or cut-and-stack imposition.

## Where it could go next

- Quarto and octavo imposition (two or three folds), with a cutting diagram.
- Creep compensation: shifting each sheet's content inward by its position in the signature. This can also be done as an incremental update by wrapping each page's content stream in a transform, without touching the original streams.
- Decrypt the standard security handler (RC4 and AES with an empty user password) so encrypted files with object streams work too.
- A trim-and-cover calculator: spine width from sheet count and paper weight, and a printable cover template.
- Drop the probe's result back into the page ("7 was behind 1, upside down") and have it rewrite the settings for you.

## Revision note

An independent reviewer rebuilt the imposition and the PDF checks in separate code. Both held up, with no mismatches in 6,144 plans and 236 reordered files. The reviewer found real problems around them, all now fixed:

- **The wrong-flip symptom was wrong.** The probe's tables said a long-edge flip, or a stack fed back in rotated, leaves 2, 3, 6 and 7 upside down. Pages 3 and 6 are on fronts, so neither error can touch them. What actually happens is that every back turns half a turn. Its pages come out upside down and swap with their neighbours: 2, 4, 5 and 7 are upside down, and 7 sits behind the cover. The page's own note ("pages 2 and 3 come out upside down") was right only for a four-page booklet. The tables and the note now follow a simulation of the error (`wrongFlip`, `backsTurned`), and the tests check them against it. The one-side-at-a-time table also gained a row for "rotated and stacked face up" together.
- **The page-turn preview was arithmetic.** It drew pages 2k and 2k+1, so it would read 1, 2, 3 even for a broken order. It now prints the page's own order on the model printer, folds it and draws what that reads. A test breaks `impose.js` on purpose and checks that the preview shows the wrong page. The footer no longer says the order comes from a simulation. It is worked out by formula and checked by one.
- **Pages could be silently dropped.** After a PDF was loaded the page count could still be edited, and a smaller number built a booklet from only the first pages. The field is now locked to the file's count, with a button to forget the PDF. The save also refuses if the counts differ (`pdfSequence`). A file over 4,000 pages is refused instead of being cut to 4,000.
- **Smaller fixes:**
  - The page and README said browsers sort a typed page list; they now say browsers appear to, matching the [Likely] rating above.
  - The probe advice no longer says to use "the settings above" (two per sheet; the probe needs one), and no longer promises that every later booklet will be right. It now suggests printing the real job's first sheet on its own as well.
  - Encrypted files are no longer described as opening without a password. Quire never checked that, and password-protected files work too.
  - "Any size needs about half a kilobyte" is now the measured growth: about 9 bytes a page, more when the tree is flattened.
  - The 4,000-plan test now also prints every plan on both model printers before folding it, as "How it works" already said it did.
