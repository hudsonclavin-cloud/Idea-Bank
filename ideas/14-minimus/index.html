Twelve months in a ringing chamber, on four bells.

How it’s rung

How it’s rung

In an English church tower the bells are not played as tunes. They are rung in rows: every bell once, in some order. Each row differs from the one before only by swapping neighbouring pairs, because a bell is half a ton of bronze swinging full circle, and all a ringer can do is hold it back or let it go a little. A bell can move one place at a time. A method is a fixed pattern of those swaps. Plain Bob Minimus is one of the oldest and simplest, for four bells: twenty-four changes, every possible order of the four bells exactly once, and then rounds, the order you started in.

Here the bells are words.

  • round — the treble
  • hand — second
  • still — third
  • home — the tenor

Each stanza is one row: its four line-endings, read down, are the four bells in that row’s order. The first stanza is rounds. The twenty-fifth is rounds again. The twenty-three between are the rest of the extent, in the method’s own order, so no word ever jumps: between any stanza and the next, a word keeps its line or trades with the line beside it. The treble, round, plain hunts from the first line to the last and back for the whole year. The others dodge and make places. Show the bells colours the endings and marks each stanza with its row; Ring the year sounds the extent, one stanza at a time, with the open handstroke gap after every whole pull.
