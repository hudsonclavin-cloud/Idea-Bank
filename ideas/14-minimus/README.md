# Minimus
> Twelve months in a village bell tower, told in four words that ring every change of Plain Bob Minimus and come round.

**Kind:** poetry
**Run:** open `index.html` in a browser (works from the file system; no server, build or network). The poems themselves are the plain-text file `poems.txt`. Tests: `node --test ideas/14-minimus/form.test.mjs ideas/14-minimus/review.test.mjs` from the repo root.
**Made by:** Sally Treble

## What it is

A cycle of twelve poems, one a month, set in the ringing chamber of an English church tower over one year. A band of four. An old man who has rung the tenor for fifty years and lives alone at the end of the lane with a dog and a mantel clock. A learner who turns up on a sleety practice night in February, rings her first rounds on the treble at Easter, and by Remembrance Sunday is ringing his bell. The things bells are rung for: the old year out and the new one in, a practice night, Good Friday's silence and Easter morning, a wedding, a striking competition, an eightieth birthday, a funeral, harvest, the two minutes' silence, Christmas, and midnight again.

The form is change ringing. In a tower the bells are not played as tunes but in *rows*, every bell once in some order, and each row differs from the last only by swapping neighbouring pairs, because a bell can be half a ton of bronze swinging full circle and can be moved at most one place at a time. Here the four bells are four words, **round**, **hand**, **still**, **home**, and every stanza is a row: its four line-endings, read downward, are the four words in that row's order. Twenty-five stanzas ring the plain course of Plain Bob Minimus, which on four bells is the full extent: every one of the twenty-four orderings of the words exactly once, and then rounds again. The first stanza ends *round / hand / still / home*. So does the last. Everything between is the long way home.

The page is designed for reading: a serif measure, generous leading, lines that never break on a phone, light and dark. Two quiet aids sit under the title. *Show the bells* colours the line-endings by bell and labels each stanza with its row (and the lead ends). *Ring the year* sounds the extent on four synthesised bells, one stanza at a time, with the open handstroke gap after every whole pull, lighting each stanza as it is rung; or tap one stanza to hear just its row. At the foot of the page is the form explained for a reader, the *blue line* (the ringers' diagram of each bell's path through the rows, drawn from the poems' own line-endings), and a live check that re-verifies the whole structure from the text every time the page loads.

## Why I made it

Change ringing is a thing I keep coming back to. For more than three centuries people in English villages have climbed towers on Tuesday nights to ring their way through permutation groups by heart, for the dead and the married and the new year, without ever calling it mathematics. What makes it what it is is a physical fact: a swinging bell can only be held back or let go a little, so no bell can leap. It can only trade places with a neighbour. Every order has to be reached one swap at a time, and the only way to get home is through all of them.

I wanted a poem with that physics in its form. The sestina is the obvious ancestor, six words rotating through the line-ends, and I love it, but a sestina's permutation moves the last word to the first place in a single jump, which no bell could do. A sestina says *obsession*: the same words will not leave you. Change ringing says something I find truer and sadder: *you get home by going through every arrangement, slowly, and when you arrive the order is the same but the hands on the ropes are not*. That is what a year is like in a small band, and the poem's year is built so that the form carries the plot. The treble, *round*, plain hunts all year, the simplest path, which is the learner's path; at each lead end the treble lies at the front for two rows while the other bells dodge, and those three lead ends land on her first rounds at Easter, on the band restarting after the old man dies in August, and on midnight.

Forms I listed and crossed off: haiku sequences, acrostics, found text from the repo, Markov text, poems about being an AI, odes to punctuation (all the obvious first ideas); a crown of sonnets and a plain sestina (the brief's own examples, and anyone's second idea); a lipogram or univocalic cycle (Perec and Bök own that ground); a Gray-code poem, eight poems each differing from the last by one line (a real mathematical object, but it has only six distinct lines in it); a tide-table poem with line lengths following the day's water heights (the constraint decorates, it does not argue). The ringing form was the one where the constraint *is* the meaning.

## How it works

**The method.** Plain Bob Minimus is the four-bell form of Plain Bob, the first method most ringers learn. Its place notation is `x.14.x.14.x.14.x.12`, repeated for three leads. Place notation names places, not bells: `x` swaps the bells in places 1–2 and in places 3–4; `14` keeps whatever bells are in first and fourth places where they are and swaps the pair in second and third; and `12` at the lead end keeps first and second places and swaps third and fourth. From rounds, `1234`, that gives

```
 1  1234      9  1342     17  1423     25  1234  (rounds)
 2  2143     10  3124     18  4132
 3  2413     11  3214     19  4312
 4  4231     12  2341     20  3421
 5  4321     13  2431     21  3241
 6  3412     14  4213     22  2314
 7  3142     15  4123     23  2134
 8  1324     16  1432     24  1243
```

The 24 rows before the return are all different, so the plain course is the extent (4! = 24). Ringers count it as 24 *changes*: the opening rounds is not a change, and the 24th change is the return to rounds. The table, the page and the poems number *rows* from the opening rounds instead, so that return is row 25 (and in July the band comes home "at the twenty-fifth"). The treble (bell 1) plain hunts: its place runs 1 2 3 4 4 3 2 1 1 2 3 4 … for the whole course. At each lead end (rows 8→9, 16→17, 24→25) the treble leads twice, one bell makes seconds and the other two dodge in 3–4.

**The mapping.** Bell 1 (treble) = *round*, 2 = *hand*, 3 = *still*, 4 (tenor) = *home*. Stanza *k* of the cycle, counting straight through the twelve poems, has four lines, and the end-word of line *i* is the word of the bell in place *i* of row *k*. The end-word is the last run of letters in the line, case-insensitive, with any closing punctuation ignored; it must be exactly one of the four bare words (no plurals, no other inflections, and not the tail of a longer word such as *around*). Each word therefore ends exactly 25 of the 100 lines, and occupies each of the four line positions in exactly the pattern the method dictates.

**The cycle.** Twelve poems named for the months, January to December, two stanzas each (rows 1–24), except December, which carries three: rows 23 and 24 and the return to rounds. 25 stanzas, 100 lines. No line is longer than 44 characters, which leaves 14% headroom on a 390 px phone in Liberation Serif. The page also hangs any wrapped line with an indent, printed-poetry fashion, and if a reader's serif is wider (Android's Noto Serif is about 18% wider) it shrinks the verse a little (never below 86%) before letting that indent take over.

**Why this form for this subject.** The constraint that a word may move only one place between stanzas is the constraint on a bell. Over 25 stanzas each word passes through every position and every neighbourhood of the others, which is what a year in a small band does to four people. The repetition is heavy (each word 25 times), as it is when you stand under four bells for an extent; I chose words that can be noun, verb, adjective and adverb (*round*: a bell swinging full circle, a round of drinks, round the tower, come round (back into rounds, or back to consciousness), the year comes round; *still*: silent, motionless, yet, and the still after the bells stop; *hand*: a ringer, a helper, the hand on the rope; *home*: the tenor's place, the end of the walk, where he dies) so the bells could change meaning while keeping their place. In practice *round* falls back on one move more than it should; see the Honest assessment.

**What the test checks** (`form.test.mjs`, node:test, reads `poems.txt`): that an independent implementation of the place notation gives the extent above with the stated lead heads and treble path; that there are twelve poems with the months' names, 25 four-line stanzas and 100 lines; that each stanza's end-words are that row; that, read straight from the text without reference to the method, every stanza ends on each word once, no word jumps between consecutive stanzas, the 24 rows before the last are all different, and both ends are rounds; that every end-word is one of the four bare words standing alone; that no line exceeds 44 characters or carries trailing whitespace; and that `index.html` embeds `poems.txt` verbatim, so the page cannot drift from the source. `review.test.mjs` pins the fixes from the independent review (see the Revision note): the ringers' count of 24 changes, place notation naming places, the poems' ringing usage and band arithmetic, and the README's own claims about the text (which March images return, how often *round* runs on as a preposition, the run of *her hand*, the iPhone face). The page repeats the structural checks in the browser on load and prints the result under *How it's rung*.

**What the test cannot check.** Whether the lines are any good, whether a word's sense actually shifts from stanza to stanza, and whether the lead ends land as events. I claim no metre. The lines are a loose four-beat speaking line, mostly end-stopped, and the test does not count stresses or syllables. The bell sound is seven decaying sine partials per bell; the test does not listen.

**Files.** `poems.txt` (the source), `index.html` (page, with a verbatim copy of the poems in a `text/plain` block), `style.css`, `app.js` (parser, method, rendering, blue line, check, bells), `form.test.mjs`, `review.test.mjs`. No dependencies.

## Honest assessment

- **[Certain] The form is kept exactly as stated.** All 25 stanzas follow the extent, checked both against the generated method and from the text alone; every end-word is a bare word; 100 lines, none over 44 characters. The tests pass and the page's own check agrees.
- **[Certain] The plain course of Plain Bob Minimus is the full extent.** Computed from the place notation in the test; the rows above are what it produces.
- **[Likely] Ringing facts in the poems and the explanation.** *Look to; treble's going; she's gone* is the standard call to start; *that's all* and *stand* are the calls to come into rounds and to stop; half-muffled ringing (a leather muffle on one side of each clapper, so alternate strokes are hushed) is used for funerals, Remembrance and ringing out the old year; bells in many towers are silent from Good Friday to Easter morning; the sally, the stay, the tail end, handstroke and backstroke, "making places" and "dodging", are all real. I wrote these from what I know of English ringing; the independent reviewer later checked the calls, half-muffling and the Good Friday silence (a custom of many towers, not all) against ringing sources and found them right. On Plain Bob's age I first wrote "Stedman's books of the 1670s", which was wrong twice over: *Tintinnalogia* is 1668 and is usually credited mainly to Richard Duckworth, with Fabian Stedman as publisher, and only *Campanalogia* (1677) is Stedman's alone. Wikipedia's Stedman article says *Tintinnalogia*'s first cross changes, "Doubles and Singles on four bells", are what is now called Plain Bob Minimus, and a ringing-theory list post says the name "Plain Bob" appears in neither book. I could not open either text in this session, so where the name first appears is unchecked, which is why the page says only "one of the oldest". A band of four ringing a Minimus test piece at a district striking competition is plausible but not typical (most competitions are on six or eight). Grounded swifts being held up on an open hand to relaunch is a common practice, not advice.
- **[Likely] No line of this cycle existed before it.** I wrote every line here. Three short phrases are deliberately not mine: the three ringing calls above, which belong to every tower.
- **Strongest poems, in my judgement:** August (the swifts leaving in a night, "he does not come round", the dog, the neighbour, the doctor's hand, and then the sally worn to the shape of his hand); May (the swift lifted on a hand, and the wedding, with *round* leading the lead end); November (the names on the war memorial being the names on the old boards all round the chamber walls); and the final stanza, which repeats April's "Look to. Treble's going. She's gone. Round / and over" with a different bell under her hand and ends on the same four ways home as January. March earns its keep because July and August need it: July's *her* is the woman in March's photograph, and August's *chair*, *dog* and *lane* are his. The mantel clock never comes back; the clocks of October and December are other clocks.
- **Weakest:** June's first stanza (the competition is reported rather than felt; "We come fourth" is light relief and little more) and December's first (Christmas is a breather between the funeral months and midnight, and reads like one). The 25 repetitions strain *home* most ("His is tied up, home / on its hook" leans on a ringer's sense of a rope put away, and "he's the first one home / to the tower" uses *home* for arriving; I mean the tower as his real home, but it is a strain). *Still* is the most flexible of the four and carries more than its share. *Round* falls back on one move: 11 of its 25 line-endings are a preposition whose object opens the next line (*round / the tower*, *round / the ropes*, *round / its hook*), and three more are *round / and over*. *Her hand* ends a line in five stanzas running (20–24, October to December). These are texture, not errors, and I have left them; a formalist editor would ask for fewer.
- **[Certain] The band's arithmetic holds.** The band is four in January with the old man and four in December with the learner ("four ways home" both times), so at his funeral they could ring all four bells themselves. September now gives his bell to "an old friend's hand" as an honour; it used to say "a borrowed hand", with no reason why a band of four would borrow one. At harvest they leave his bell down by choice and ring on three, so "short of a hand" is his hand, not a missing ringer.
- **[Likely] November's boards.** November first had "the peal boards all round / the chamber walls". On a ring of four that is a stretch: a peal is 5,040 changes, 210 extents of Minimus, and the Composition Library gives the first peal of Plain Bob Minimus on tower bells as 1961, so a four-bell tower would have had few or no peal boards with names from before 1914. They are now "the old boards", which a small tower does have: restorations, ringers' rules, its own roll of honour. (The Central Council's Rolls of Honour do record about 1,400 ringers killed in the First World War, by tower.)
- **Voice.** The whole cycle speaks in short declaratives and fragments ("Muffles on. Bells up."). It is a choice, the way ringers talk, but a tough editor could call it mannered by September.
- **[Certain] Mid-line uses.** *round* appears inside a line once (May: "the tower, and round, and are never still") and *hand* once (December: "hand over hand"). Only line-endings are bells; these two are ghost strokes, and the test ignores them by design.
- **[Certain] The page.** Zero dependencies, nothing fetched, works from `file://`. Verified headless at 1280 px and 390 px in light and dark: no console errors, no horizontal overflow, and no wrapped verse line. Headless Chromium here falls through the page's font stack to Liberation Serif (Times metrics), in which the longest line ("The swifts are back. Dusk. They scream round") takes 309 of the 358 px phone measure, 14% headroom. That is not what most phones show. The stack starts with Iowan Old Style and Charter, so an iPhone (or a Mac) sets the verse in Iowan Old Style; Android sets it in Noto Serif, and Windows in Georgia. I loaded Noto Serif from its open font file and measured it: it is about 18% wider than Liberation on the longest line ("on his rope. The names that never came home", 365 px unscaled), so it does not fit 390 px by itself. The fit step scales it to 98% at 390 px and 91% at 360 px with no wraps; at 320 px it hits the 86% floor and 13 lines wrap onto the hanging indent. Georgia's average glyph width is about 10% above Times's (Capsize font metrics), so it should fit 390 px unscaled [Likely]. Iowan Old Style I could neither render nor find metrics for. The fit step absorbs a face up to about 34% wider than Liberation at 390 px (24% at 360 px, 11% at 320 px) before the hanging indent takes over, so an iPhone wraps lines only if Iowan is more than a third wider than Times, which I doubt but have not checked [Likely]. With DejaVu Serif forced, wider than any phone default I know of, the fit step scales the verse to 91% at 390 px and no line wraps; at 360 px it hits the floor and two lines wrap. Without JavaScript the hanging indent catches any long line instead. The smoke screenshots were read by eye. Motion is limited to the "ringing" highlight and a scroll that follows the ringing, which switches to an instant jump under `prefers-reduced-motion` and stops the moment the reader scrolls.
- **The bells are a sketch of a bell.** Seven sine partials with a hum, prime, tierce, quint and nominal in roughly the right ratios; it sounds like a small ring heard across a field, not from the chamber. Open handstroke gaps are kept; the funeral rows are not muffled.

## Where it could go next

- **Muffle the dead months.** Ring the September and November rows half-muffled (every backstroke hushed) and the last January row too, so the sound of the page does what the poems describe.
- **Let the reader ring.** Tap the four words of a stanza in any order and have the page say whether that change was legal, which is how learners are taught the rule.
- **Doubles.** The same four words plus a fifth on five bells; a plain course of Grandsire Doubles is 30 rows, which is a cycle of similar size with a stronger, odder music (the treble and the second both hunt). The 120-row extent is out of reach for a poem.
- **A broadside.** A printable sheet with the blue line in the margin beside the stanzas, which is how the method would be pricked in a ringer's notebook.
- **Other towers.** The twelve-month frame would hold other bands and other bells; the extent would not change.

## Revision note

An independent reviewer rebuilt the method and the text checks in separate code and confirmed the form exactly: 25 rows, every change an adjacent swap, the extent before the return, every stanza on its row. All its findings were low-severity, and I checked each one before acting. Every one held up at least in part. What changed:

- **README misdescribed its own cycle.** It said July and August need March's photograph and mantel clock. Neither comes back. What they need is *her*, the chair, the dog and the lane.
- **Plain Bob's history was wrong.** It said "Stedman's books of the 1670s". *Tintinnalogia* is 1668 and mainly Duckworth's; *Campanalogia* (1677) is Stedman's. The README now says what the sources say, and that the name's first appearance is unchecked.
- **The band's arithmetic didn't add up.** September borrowed a ringer for no reason when the band was four again. That line is now "an old friend's hand". Harvest's "short of a hand" stands, read as his hand.
- **Three lines bent to the end-word rule, and two of them were wrong.** December's "We ring round" (ringers ring *rounds*; "ring round" is British for phoning) is now "We come round". April's "walked from home … with the rope in hand" had her carry the bell rope from her house; it is now "in the dark, early. Now, the rope in hand." March's "first one home / to the tower" is kept and admitted as a strain.
- **Ringing terms on the page and in the README.** "Twenty-four changes … and then rounds" counted 25: the page now says the twenty-fourth change brings the bells back to rounds. The README says how ringers count changes and how the table counts rows. Place notation now keeps *places*, not bells. "A bell is half a ton" is now "can be".
- **Peal boards in a four-bell tower.** These were implausible before 1914. November's boards are now "the old boards", and the README no longer says "true of real towers".
- **Phone fonts were misstated.** The README said Georgia and Noto Serif were the likely phone defaults and should fit. An iPhone actually sets the verse in Iowan Old Style. I measured Noto Serif here: it is about 18% wider than Liberation, not a tenth, and fits 390 px only through the page's fit step. The page bullet now gives measured numbers and names what is unchecked.
- **Advisory notes taken.** The README now admits that *round* runs on as a preposition in 11 of its 25 endings and that *her hand* ends a line in five stanzas running. Its list of the words' senses now gives only senses the cycle uses. The poems' texture is otherwise unchanged.

No end-word moved. The form test passes as before, and `review.test.mjs` pins each fix; each of its checks fails on the pre-review text.
