# Minimus
> Twelve months in a village bell tower, told in four words that ring every change of Plain Bob Minimus and come round.

**Kind:** poetry
**Run:** open `index.html` in a browser (works from the file system; no server, build or network). The poems themselves are the plain-text file `poems.txt`. Test: `node --test ideas/14-minimus/form.test.mjs` from the repo root.
**Made by:** Sally Treble

## What it is

A cycle of twelve poems, one a month, set in the ringing chamber of an English church tower over one year. A band of four. An old man who has rung the tenor for fifty years and lives alone at the end of the lane with a dog and a mantel clock. A learner who turns up on a sleety practice night in February, rings her first rounds on the treble at Easter, and by Remembrance Sunday is ringing his bell. The things bells are rung for: the old year out and the new one in, a practice night, Good Friday's silence and Easter morning, a wedding, a striking competition, an eightieth birthday, a funeral, harvest, the two minutes' silence, Christmas, and midnight again.

The form is change ringing. In a tower the bells are not played as tunes but in *rows*, every bell once in some order, and each row differs from the last only by swapping neighbouring pairs, because a bell is half a ton of bronze swinging full circle and can be moved at most one place at a time. Here the four bells are four words, **round**, **hand**, **still**, **home**, and every stanza is a row: its four line-endings, read downward, are the four words in that row's order. Twenty-five stanzas ring the plain course of Plain Bob Minimus, which on four bells is the full extent: every one of the twenty-four orderings of the words exactly once, and then rounds again. The first stanza ends *round / hand / still / home*. So does the last. Everything between is the long way home.

The page is designed for reading: a serif measure, generous leading, lines that never break on a phone, light and dark. Two quiet aids sit under the title. *Show the bells* colours the line-endings by bell and labels each stanza with its row (and the lead ends). *Ring the year* sounds the extent on four synthesised bells, one stanza at a time, with the open handstroke gap after every whole pull, lighting each stanza as it is rung; or tap one stanza to hear just its row. At the foot of the page is the form explained for a reader, the *blue line* (the ringers' diagram of each bell's path through the rows, drawn from the poems' own line-endings), and a live check that re-verifies the whole structure from the text every time the page loads.

## Why I made it

Change ringing is a thing I keep coming back to. For more than three centuries people in English villages have climbed towers on Tuesday nights to ring their way through permutation groups by heart, for the dead and the married and the new year, without ever calling it mathematics. What makes it what it is is a physical fact: a swinging bell can only be held back or let go a little, so no bell can leap. It can only trade places with a neighbour. Every order has to be reached one swap at a time, and the only way to get home is through all of them.

I wanted a poem with that physics in its form. The sestina is the obvious ancestor, six words rotating through the line-ends, and I love it, but a sestina's permutation moves the last word to the first place in a single jump, which no bell could do. A sestina says *obsession*: the same words will not leave you. Change ringing says something I find truer and sadder: *you get home by going through every arrangement, slowly, and when you arrive the order is the same but the hands on the ropes are not*. That is what a year is like in a small band, and the poem's year is built so that the form carries the plot. The treble, *round*, plain hunts all year, the simplest path, which is the learner's path; at each lead end the treble lies at the front for two rows while the other bells dodge, and those three lead ends land on her first rounds at Easter, on the band restarting after the old man dies in August, and on midnight.

Forms I listed and crossed off: haiku sequences, acrostics, found text from the repo, Markov text, poems about being an AI, odes to punctuation (all the obvious first ideas); a crown of sonnets and a plain sestina (the brief's own examples, and anyone's second idea); a lipogram or univocalic cycle (Perec and Bök own that ground); a Gray-code poem, eight poems each differing from the last by one line (a real mathematical object, but it has only six distinct lines in it); a tide-table poem with line lengths following the day's water heights (the constraint decorates, it does not argue). The ringing form was the one where the constraint *is* the meaning.

## How it works

**The method.** Plain Bob Minimus is the four-bell form of Plain Bob, the first method most ringers learn. Its place notation is `x.14.x.14.x.14.x.12`, repeated for three leads: `x` swaps both pairs (1–2 and 3–4), `14` holds the first and fourth bells and swaps the middle pair, and `12` at the lead end holds the first two and swaps 3–4. From rounds, `1234`, that gives

```
 1  1234      9  1342     17  1423     25  1234  (rounds)
 2  2143     10  3124     18  4132
 3  2413     11  3214     19  4312
 4  4231     12  2341     20  3421
 5  4321     13  2431     21  3241
 6  3412     14  4213     22  2314
 7  3142     15  4123     23  2134
 8  1324     16  1432     24  1243
```

The 24 rows before the return are all different, so the plain course is the extent (4! = 24). The treble (bell 1) plain hunts: its place runs 1 2 3 4 4 3 2 1 1 2 3 4 … for the whole course. At each lead end (rows 8→9, 16→17, 24→25) the treble leads twice, one bell makes seconds and the other two dodge in 3–4.

**The mapping.** Bell 1 (treble) = *round*, 2 = *hand*, 3 = *still*, 4 (tenor) = *home*. Stanza *k* of the cycle, counting straight through the twelve poems, has four lines, and the end-word of line *i* is the word of the bell in place *i* of row *k*. The end-word is the last run of letters in the line, case-insensitive, with any closing punctuation ignored; it must be exactly one of the four bare words (no plurals, no other inflections, and not the tail of a longer word such as *around*). Each word therefore ends exactly 25 of the 100 lines, and occupies each of the four line positions in exactly the pattern the method dictates.

**The cycle.** Twelve poems named for the months, January to December, two stanzas each (rows 1–24), except December, which carries three: rows 23 and 24 and the return to rounds. 25 stanzas, 100 lines. No line is longer than 44 characters, which is the measured limit for an unbroken line at 390 px in the page's type; the page also hangs any wrapped line with an indent, printed-poetry fashion, and if a reader's serif is unusually wide it shrinks the verse a little (never below 86%) before letting that indent take over.

**Why this form for this subject.** The constraint that a word may move only one place between stanzas is the constraint on a bell. Over 25 stanzas each word passes through every position and every neighbourhood of the others, which is what a year in a small band does to four people. The repetition is heavy (each word 25 times), as it is when you stand under four bells for an extent; I chose words that can be noun, verb, adjective and adverb (*round*: rounds, a round of drinks, round the tower, come round, the year comes round; *still*: silent, motionless, yet, a photograph; *hand*: a ringer, a clock's, a helper, the thing on the rope; *home*: the tenor's place, the end of the walk, where he dies) so the bells could change meaning while keeping their place.

**What the test checks** (`form.test.mjs`, node:test, reads `poems.txt`): that an independent implementation of the place notation gives the extent above with the stated lead heads and treble path; that there are twelve poems with the months' names, 25 four-line stanzas and 100 lines; that each stanza's end-words are that row; that, read straight from the text without reference to the method, every stanza ends on each word once, no word jumps between consecutive stanzas, the 24 rows before the last are all different, and both ends are rounds; that every end-word is one of the four bare words standing alone; that no line exceeds 44 characters or carries trailing whitespace; and that `index.html` embeds `poems.txt` verbatim, so the page cannot drift from the source. The page repeats the structural checks in the browser on load and prints the result under *How it's rung*.

**What the test cannot check.** Whether the lines are any good, whether a word's sense actually shifts from stanza to stanza, and whether the lead ends land as events. I claim no metre. The lines are a loose four-beat speaking line, mostly end-stopped, and the test does not count stresses or syllables. The bell sound is seven decaying sine partials per bell; the test does not listen.

**Files.** `poems.txt` (the source), `index.html` (page, with a verbatim copy of the poems in a `text/plain` block), `style.css`, `app.js` (parser, method, rendering, blue line, check, bells), `form.test.mjs`. No dependencies.

## Honest assessment

- **[Certain] The form is kept exactly as stated.** All 25 stanzas follow the extent, checked both against the generated method and from the text alone; every end-word is a bare word; 100 lines, none over 44 characters. The tests pass and the page's own check agrees.
- **[Certain] The plain course of Plain Bob Minimus is the full extent.** Computed from the place notation in the test; the rows above are what it produces.
- **[Likely] Ringing facts in the poems and the explanation.** *Look to; treble's going; she's gone* is the standard call to start; *that's all* and *stand* are the calls to come into rounds and to stop; half-muffled ringing (a leather muffle on one side of each clapper, so alternate strokes are hushed) is used for funerals, Remembrance and ringing out the old year; bells in many towers are silent from Good Friday to Easter morning; the sally, the stay, the tail end, handstroke and backstroke, "making places" and "dodging", are all real. I wrote these from what I know of English ringing without checking a source in this session. Plain Bob appearing in Stedman's books of the 1670s is my recollection, which is why the page says only "one of the oldest". A band of four ringing a Minimus test piece at a district striking competition is plausible but not typical (most competitions are on six or eight). Grounded swifts being held up on an open hand to relaunch is a common practice, not advice.
- **[Likely] No line of this cycle existed before it.** I wrote every line here. Three short phrases are deliberately not mine: the three ringing calls above, which belong to every tower.
- **Strongest poems, in my judgement:** August (the swifts leaving in a night, "he does not come round", the dog, the neighbour, the doctor's hand, and then the sally worn to the shape of his hand); May (the swift lifted on a hand, and the wedding, with *round* leading the lead end); November (the names on the war memorial being the names on the peal boards all round the chamber walls, which is true of real towers); and the final stanza, which repeats April's "Look to. Treble's going. She's gone. Round / and over" with a different bell under her hand and ends on the same four ways home as January. March earns its keep because July and August need "her photograph" and the mantel clock.
- **Weakest:** June's first stanza (the competition is reported rather than felt; "We come fourth" is light relief and little more) and December's first (Christmas is a breather between the funeral months and midnight, and reads like one). The 25 repetitions strain *home* most ("His is tied up, home / on its hook" leans on a ringer's sense of a rope put away). *Still* is the most flexible of the four and carries more than its share.
- **Voice.** The whole cycle speaks in short declaratives and fragments ("Muffles on. Bells up."). It is a choice, the way ringers talk, but a tough editor could call it mannered by September.
- **[Certain] Mid-line uses.** *round* appears inside a line once (May: "the tower, and round, and are never still") and *hand* once (December: "hand over hand"). Only line-endings are bells; these two are ghost strokes, and the test ignores them by design.
- **[Certain] The page.** Zero dependencies, nothing fetched, works from `file://`. Verified headless at 1280 px and 390 px in light and dark: no console errors, no horizontal overflow, and no wrapped verse line. The headless browser here only has a Times-metric serif (Liberation Serif), in which the longest line ("The swifts are back. Dusk. They scream round") takes 309 of the 358 px phone measure, 14% headroom. Georgia and Noto Serif, the likely phone defaults, are roughly a tenth wider, so they should still fit, but I could not render them here [Likely]. With DejaVu Serif forced, which is wider than any phone default I know of, the fit step scales the verse to 91% and still no line wraps; without JavaScript the hanging indent would catch them instead. The smoke screenshots were read by eye. Motion is limited to the "ringing" highlight and a scroll that follows the ringing, which switches to an instant jump under `prefers-reduced-motion` and stops the moment the reader scrolls.
- **The bells are a sketch of a bell.** Seven sine partials with a hum, prime, tierce, quint and nominal in roughly the right ratios; it sounds like a small ring heard across a field, not from the chamber. Open handstroke gaps are kept; the funeral rows are not muffled.

## Where it could go next

- **Muffle the dead months.** Ring the September and November rows half-muffled (every backstroke hushed) and the last January row too, so the sound of the page does what the poems describe.
- **Let the reader ring.** Tap the four words of a stanza in any order and have the page say whether that change was legal, which is how learners are taught the rule.
- **Doubles.** The same four words plus a fifth on five bells; a plain course of Grandsire Doubles is 30 rows, which is a cycle of similar size with a stronger, odder music (the treble and the second both hunt). The 120-row extent is out of reach for a poem.
- **A broadside.** A printable sheet with the blue line in the margin beside the stanzas, which is how the method would be pricked in a ringer's notebook.
- **Other towers.** The twelve-month frame would hold other bands and other bells; the extent would not change.
