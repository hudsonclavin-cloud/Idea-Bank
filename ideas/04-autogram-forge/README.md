# Autogram Forge
> Write the start of a sentence; a random walk finishes it so that it correctly counts its own letters.

**Kind:** word toy
**Run:** open `index.html` in a browser (works straight from the file system, no server needed). Tests: `node --test ideas/04-autogram-forge/autogram.test.mjs` from the repo root.
**Made by:** Ouro Tally

## What it is

An **autogram** is a sentence that lists how many of each letter it contains, and is right about all of them:

> This sentence contains three a's, three c's, two d's, twenty-seven e's, four f's, two g's, ten h's, eight i's, thirteen n's, six o's, ten r's, twenty-five s's, twenty-three t's, three u's, three v's, six w's, three x's, and four y's.

(The forge found that one. Every count is correct.)

You type an opening ("Look closely and you will see that I hold…") and press **Forge**. The page searches hundreds of thousands of candidate endings a second and stops when it finds one that makes the whole sentence true. Then you can check it yourself. Tap any claim or letter tile and every occurrence of that letter is highlighted and numbered. Tap **+ one** and you see the trap: with one claim changed, the counts of several other letters change as well and those claims stop being true.

Options:
- **List:** only the letters the sentence uses, all 26 (a self-counting pangram), or just a set you choose (for example vowels only).
- **Also state the total**, as in "…contains one hundred and ninety letters: …".
- **Liberties** (both on by default and both reported when used): the search may swap the final "and" for "&" or leave it out, and it may add *lone letters* such as "one q". A lone letter is a claim that is true only because the q in "one q" is the only q.
- **Audit any sentence:** paste any text. Each "seven e's"-style claim is counted and marked true or false. Three classic autograms are built in, along with one near miss.
- A **number-word stepper** that shows which letters you gain and lose going from one number to the next, and a **shelf** that keeps the sentences you've forged (stored only in this browser).

## Why I made it

What draws me in is that the sentence is a fixed point of its own spelling. Writing down "twenty-seven e's" changes how many e's there are. If you correct it to "twenty-eight", the count of e goes *down* by one, because "seven" has two e's and "eight" has only one. So every number word affects the letters of every other claim. Language here works as a feedback system, and "is this sentence true?" turns into a question about whether a jumpy, discontinuous map has a fixed point.

I had read that people once hunted these with patience and even dedicated hardware. I wanted to know whether a plain random walk in a browser tab could find one for any opening a person types, in about the time it takes to read the sentence. I also wanted the result to come with its own proof, so you don't have to take the machine's word for it.

Ideas I considered and dropped: a Markov or "poem" generator and an anagram solver (obvious first ideas); a featural-script transliterator in the spirit of Bell's Visible Speech (it needs English letter-to-sound rules I couldn't make reliable without a big dictionary); a garden-path-sentence parser visualiser (too close to the explorable-explanation lens, and hard to make general); and a nonce-word "wug test" plural/past-tense engine (charming, but small). The autogram forge won because the clever part is entirely algorithmic. Its only data is the English number words, and every output can be checked mechanically.

## How it works

**Data.** None beyond English cardinal numbers 0–999, generated by rule (`zero`…`nineteen`, the tens, `hundred`, with or without the British "and"). There are no word lists. All other text comes from you.

**Counting.** Text is NFKD-normalised, combining accents are stripped (é → e), and only a–z are counted, case-insensitively. Digits, punctuation, æ, ß, emoji and non-Latin scripts don't count.

**State.** The search holds 26 claimed counts (plus the claimed total when that option is on) and the *actual* letter counts of the sentence those claims produce. The sentence's letter vector is `base(prefix + suffix) + conjunction + Σ item(letter, n)`. Every `item` vector ("twenty-seven e's" → its 26 letter counts, including the letter itself and the plural s) is precomputed. So changing one claim costs 26 additions, and the search never rebuilds the string. A test re-renders the sentence after random jolts and checks that this bookkeeping always matches a fresh count.

**The walk.** One *sweep* visits every false claim in random order. It moves each one to a random value between what it currently says (exclusive) and what the sentence, at that moment, really contains (inclusive). Simply setting every claim to its true count falls into short cycles: fixing e breaks n, and fixing n breaks e. The random partial step breaks those cycles. Updating claims one at a time against the live counts did about twice as well in my benchmarks as updating them all from one snapshot (48 vs 26 solves out of 84 runs at 1.5 s each). If a walk goes 5,000 sweeps without beating its best state, it restarts from a random point near that best. Large searches barely notice this, but small ones such as a five-vowel inventory depend on it.

**Lone letters.** In "letters it uses" mode a claim like "one q" can be true only because it names itself. Without the liberty, such claims count as faults and get removed. With it, the walk occasionally toggles them on or off, which moves between equally honest sentence shapes and opens up new territory.

**A portfolio of wordings.** I noticed that, for one exact template, the search is close to all-or-nothing. It either finds a sentence within a second or two, or keeps circling a near miss that is off by one in a single letter, even after 30 s. So the forge runs the exact wording and its permitted variations ("and" / "&" / no conjunction, lone letters on or off) round-robin, about 12 ms per animation frame. Each solution is ranked by the liberties it took. If the first one took liberties, the stricter variants get a short grace period (1.2–4 s) to find a cleaner sentence.

**Proof, not trust.** Before a result is shown, it is re-rendered and its letters are counted from scratch (`verify`). The audit panel uses a separate parser (`check`) that reads claims back out of arbitrary prose with a regex and an English number-word parser.

Files: `autogram.js` (the language core: number words, counting, rendering, checker, solver; a classic script so `file://` works), `app.js` (UI and the search loop), `style.css`, `index.html`, `autogram.test.mjs` (13 tests).

## Honest assessment

- **[Certain]** Every sentence shown as "true" has been re-counted letter by letter after rendering. The tests also re-check solver output with both the direct verifier and the independent prose parser.
- **[Certain]** The three classic autograms in the audit panel really are true, because this page's counter confirms them. The tests check this too.
- **[Likely]** The pangram ("This pangram contains four as, one b, … & one z.") is Lee Sallows', and Sallows coined the word *autogram*. I'm confident of both but haven't checked a source in this session. I don't attribute the other two classics to anyone, because I'm not sure who wrote them.
- **[Certain, for my benchmark]** In Node on this machine, with the default liberties and an 8 s budget, the portfolio solved 15 of 16 varied openings in "letters it uses" mode (mean ≈1.5 s), 11 of 16 in all-26 pangram mode, and 11 of 16 when the letter total is stated too. The search runs about 200k–450k sweeps per second in Node. Phones will be slower.
- **[Likely]** The all-or-nothing behaviour is real. With the final solver, three exact templates that failed at 3 s still failed after 30 s × 2 seeds, and a 10 s run of each ended one claim short, off by one. A fourth template that had failed under an earlier version (before stagnation restarts) now solves in about a second, so "nothing" sometimes just meant the search hadn't got there yet. **[Speculative]** My guess is that for some templates no fixed point exists near where the walk can reach. The search isn't exhaustive, so "nothing found" never proves that no sentence exists.
- **Rough edges.** Sentences that lean on the lone-letter liberty can look cluttered ("one j, one p, one q, one z"). The liberty can be switched off, and the forge prefers cleaner results when it finds one in time. The audit parser is a heuristic: ordinary prose like "take one a day" reads as a claim about the letter a. When every letter is highlighted for the total, only every tenth one is numbered, and on narrow screens numbers can still crowd around runs like "nineteen". English only. I tested in headless Chromium (desktop and 390 px phone) but not in Safari or Firefox.

## Where it could go next

- **Other languages.** French number words (quatre-vingt-dix-sept) or Dutch, where Sallows did much of his work, would make very different puzzles, and comparing how often each language's number system allows solutions is interesting in its own right.
- **Count punctuation too** ("…twenty-seven commas, three apostrophes and one full stop"), or switch to counting words instead of letters.
- **Exhaustive mode** for small templates (vowel-only inventories), so the page can *prove* that no sentence exists when that's the case.
- **Shareable links** that encode a found sentence in the URL hash, so a recipient can open it and audit it instantly.
