A Little of Every Year

Four rows of casks. Once a year a quarter of the bottom row is drawn off and bottled. The bottom row is topped up from the row above it, that row from the one above, and the top row with young wine. Each cask loses 3.5% a year through the wood, and that is made up from above too. Nobody has emptied a cask since 1826.

What the arithmetic says will happen, and when. Keep running stops at each of these.

    The arithmetic can say how much of every year is in the glass, and from which year a glass holds, on average, not even one molecule of 1826. It cannot say whether it is still the same wine. The chalk on the bottom row says it is.

    Why the numbers are exact

    Take one molecule in this year’s glass and follow it backwards, a year at a time. Every year the bottom row had just been topped up from the row above: a quarter to replace the draw and 3.5% to replace what the wood breathed out, 28.5% in all. The casks here are perfectly mixed, and evaporation takes every molecule with the same chance, so our molecule was one of those newcomers with chance 28.5%, whatever happened in any other year. The row above passes that much down and loses its own 3.5%, so 32% of it is new each year; and so on up, to 39% at the top. It went in at the top on its fourth arrival, counting backwards.

    So its age is the number of tosses it takes to get a head from each of four coins in turn, landing heads 28.5%, 32%, 35.5% and 39% of the time, and every share on this page is a count of coin tosses:

    • Share of the glass that is k years old P4(k), where Pd(k) = (1 − gd) Pd(k−1) + gd Pd−1(k−1), starting from P0(0) = 1 and zero everywhere else, with g = 39%, 35.5%, 32%, 28.5% from the top row down
    • Share that is still the founding wine the chance that the coins have not all shown their heads in (year − 1827) tosses
    • Average age, once the founding wine is gone 1 ÷ 28.5% + 1 ÷ 32% + 1 ÷ 35.5% + 1 ÷ 39% = 12 years

    With no evaporation every coin is the same, and these become the textbook ones: the age is negative binomial, the founding wine a binomial tail, and the average age rows ÷ draw.

    To turn a share into molecules takes one more number. A 100 ml glass of dry 17% wine holds about 3.0 × 1024 molecules of water and ethanol (about 84 g of water and 13 g of ethanol). A year counts as in the glass when its share times that number is at least one.

    What the arithmetic simplifies or leaves out

    • A real cask is not perfectly mixed. Wine is drawn and added with care, and mixing inside a cask is slow, so a real solera only approximates this one.
    • Evaporation is counted, simply, and it is not a small correction. Real sherry butts lose about 3.5% of their wine a year through the wood (the Consejo Regulador’s average: about 3% in wet years and 4% in dry). Here every row loses that much and is topped up from the one above, so the young wine at the top replaces the draw and every row’s loss. For the solera as first set, that moves the last glass holding a molecule of 1826, on average, from 2062 back to 2010. Every molecule, water or ethanol, is taken to be equally likely to leave. The real loss is mostly water, which would keep old ethanol longer: if only water left, a glass would hold less than one molecule of 1826 from 2023 rather than 2011, and its wine would by now be 26.5% alcohol, well above the 17–22% the sherry rules list for an oloroso. At 3% or 4% a year, with either extreme, a glass of 2026 holds less than one molecule of 1826, on average.
    • Molecules do not sit still chemically. Ethanol oxidises, esters form, and water molecules trade hydrogen atoms constantly; “a molecule of 1826” means a molecule’s worth of that year’s wine.
    • Real soleras differ in the number of rows, how often and how much they draw, and how young the young wine is. This one is invented.
    • Years that hinge on “one molecule” move by a year or two with the size of the glass: a 75 ml glass holds less than one molecule of 1826, on average, from 2010.
