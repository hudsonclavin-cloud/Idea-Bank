Two Sheets of Nothing

Two sheets of random noise. Either one on its own says nothing, and that nothing can be proved. Lay one over the other and they spell a message.

The light table

Drag the top sheet until it sits exactly over the other one.

One subpixel off and the clean reading is lost, though not the information: a misaligned pair is still not secret (more below). Line them up by hand if you can; the sheets snap when they are within two subpixels.

The exact version of this proof lives in vc.test.mjs: for every value of the secret, all 144 key values a cell can take are enumerated, and the histogram of patterns on each sheet comes out identical, pattern for pattern.

What the proof does not cover: a pair. It is about one sheet on its own. Two sheets hold the message between them however they lie. A slip hides it from your eye, which averages how many black subpixels each cell has, but not from a camera and a computer that see every subpixel. For a random two-sheet message (one bit per cell), a one-subpixel slip sideways or up/down still leaves 0.51 bits per cell, a diagonal one at least 0.43, and a slip of whole cells 0.55 bits; the page's scattered layout leaves at least 0.50. A computer's best guess gets 80% of cells right after a one-subpixel slip and 74% after a whole-cell slip, where a coin toss gets 50%. So keep the two sheets apart until you mean to read them, and do not photograph them together.

Make it real

Print each sheet on transparency film (overhead-projector or inkjet acetate) at 100% scale. Each sheet comes out 176 mm wide with a corner mark at each corner. Hold them up to a window and slide one over the other until the marks meet; the message appears in the film, with no computer in the loop.

One rule, the same as for any one-time pad: a key is for one printing. Sheets made against the same sheet A for two different messages show, cell by cell, where the messages differ; made for the same message in two schemes, or under two different pictures, they can give the message itself away. So the page draws a fresh key whenever you switch scheme, and the first time you change anything after printing, or after saving any sheet but A. New key does it by hand.

This browser has no secure random number generator (crypto.getRandomValues), so the sheets on this page come from Math.random, which is not fit for a key. Printing and saving are switched off.

Made by Acetate for the Idea Bank. The two-sheet and three-sheet schemes are Naor and Shamir's visual cryptography (1994). Sheets that carry pictures of their own go back to the same paper, for two sheets; Ateniese, Blundo, De Santis and Stinson (2001) generalised the idea.
