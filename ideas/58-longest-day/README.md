# The Longest Day of Your Life
> Since 1973, the length of every day has been worked out against atomic clocks. The Moon, the wind and the Pacific are all in the differences.

**Kind:** data essay
**Run:** open `ideas/58-longest-day/index.html` in a browser (plain classic scripts; works from `file://` or any static server; no network; nothing is stored or sent). Checks, from the repo root: `node ideas/58-longest-day/check.mjs` (data integrity; prefix `FINALS_PATH=/path/to/finals2000A.all` to compare every embedded value with a fresh download), `node --test ideas/58-longest-day/claims.test.mjs` (every number recomputed independently), and `node tools/smoke.mjs ideas/58-longest-day`.
**Made by:** Leapwood

## What it is

A one-page data essay built on a single file: the U.S. Naval Observatory's daily record of how long each day actually lasted, as measured against atomic clocks. The number for each day is the *excess length of day*: the time Universal Time (UT1, the Earth's own clock) took to advance one day, minus 86,400 SI seconds. There are 19,650 days of it, from 3 January 1973 to 21 October 2026, embedded exactly as published.

The page, top to bottom:

1. **Every measured day.** The whole record as one line, with the longest day (2 April 1973, +4.03 ms), the shortest (5 July 2024, −1.66 ms), and the single day the file lists as exactly 86,400 s to its last decimal (19 July 2008, ±3.3 µs). Hover or drag for any date.
2. **How long was your day?** Type any date. The page gives that day's length to five decimals of a second with its stated error, its rank among all recorded days, the day taken apart into slow drift, season, Moon and the rest, the longest and shortest days since, how many seconds the Earth has fallen behind atomic time since then, and how many leap seconds civil time added to wait for it. Your days are then marked in the charts. Nothing leaves the page.
3. **Fifty-four years, one row each.** A carpet of every day, coloured on a diverging scale around 86,400 s; switched to *rhythms only*, the seasons show as vertical bands and the Moon as fine diagonal threads.
4. **The Moon.** A periodogram of the days from 1985 on (before that, the file's short-period detail is mostly the producers' tide model; see below), whose peaks sit at 13.661, 9.133, 27.555, 14.765 and 31.807 days, the periods of the Moon's equator crossings, its closest approaches and their combinations with the Sun, with the 29.53-day cycle of phases conspicuously empty. A forecast check (six lunar sine waves fitted to 1985–2015 predict 2016–2026), the fortnightly ripple's size year by year against the Moon's 18.6-year standstill cycle, and a button that plays fifty-three years of days as sound, with and without the Moon.
5. **The wind.** The average year: days run longest around 9 April and shortest around 22 July, so the northern summer, the season of longest daylight, is when the planet spins fastest.
6. **The Pacific.** Twelve-month changes in day length beside twelve-month changes in NOAA's Oceanic Niño Index, with the correlation and an honest p-value.
7. **The missing seconds.** The daily excesses summed into the seconds the Earth has lost to atomic time, against the staircase of leap seconds.
8. **What the record is, and isn't**, then sources, and a CSV download of the daily series.

## Why I made it

I wanted a dataset where the numbers are tiny, exact and everywhere, and where nobody alive has ever felt the thing they measure. A millisecond a day is far below anything a body can notice, and yet the record of those milliseconds turns out to contain the Moon's orbital periods, matched to within minutes, the jet streams, El Niño and the reason the world's clocks have been held back by a second twenty-seven times.

The part that can be proven is the Moon. Compute the periods from the Moon's orbit first, then ask which periods repeat in the forty-one measured years since 1985: the answer comes back as 13.661 and 27.555 days, within about a minute of the 13.6608 days between the Moon's equator crossings and the 27.5545 days between its closest approaches, while the 29.53-day cycle of phases, the one everybody knows, is not there at all. Then a forecast made only from 1985–2015 tracks the next ten years. One wrinkle, which the page states plainly: the file's producers use a tide model while combining measurements, and before the mid-1980s the file's short-period detail is a heavily smoothed curve with that tide put back, so there the Moon in the file is mostly the model. That is why everything about the Moon is tested on 1985 on, where the strongest peaks still stand thousands of times above the background.

The part that can only be felt is the date box. Everybody has a date they would like to know more about, and the record will tell you, exactly, how long that day was: a day you were born, a day you lost someone. The longest day of your life, in this sense, was not a June solstice. For 75% of days of birth from 3 January 1973 to the end of 2008, the longest day since was one of three ordinary days: 21 October 1976, 18 March 1992 or 10 March 2016. (Anyone born before 1973 gets 2 April 1973, but only because that is where the record begins; the page says so.) Nobody marked any of them. I found that oddly moving, and the title is that feeling.

I crossed off several candidates first: Kyoto cherry blossoms (beautiful, but now a famous climate chart), a tide gauge (the interesting part needs hourly data, far too big to embed), meteorite falls (the open catalogue gives the year of each fall, not the hour). The length-of-day file downloaded cleanly from a U.S. government server, is public domain, and has a measurement error attached to every value, which is what made it possible to be honest about the 1970s.

## How it works

### The data

- **Length of day.** `finals2000A.all` from the IERS Rapid Service/Prediction Center at the U.S. Naval Observatory (https://maia.usno.navy.mil/ser7/finals2000A.all), retrieved 23 October 2026, SHA-256 `95a61292…de9da`. Columns 80–86 are Bulletin A's LOD in milliseconds, columns 87–93 its error. U.S. government work, public domain in the United States; the accompanying bulletin is marked "Approved for public release: distribution unlimited." Bulletin A lists its inputs as VLBI, satellite laser ranging, GPS, lunar laser ranging and meteorological predictions of the atmosphere's angular momentum.
- **Leap seconds.** `tai-utc.dat` from the same server, kept in full.
- **El Niño.** NOAA Climate Prediction Center's Oceanic Niño Index, `oni.ascii.txt`, kept in full. Public domain.
- **What it measures and what it doesn't.** The excess length of the mean solar day as defined through UT1. It is not daylight, not the sidereal day, and not the apparent solar day. Early values are much rougher: the stated error is 0.19 ms on the record's first days and about 0.004 ms (median, 2016–2025) now. Early values are also heavily smoothed (see the Honest assessment). At the file's 0.0001 ms resolution, 15,394 distinct values cover the 19,650 days, so many days share a published value.

Full provenance, every processing step and every checksum are in [`data/SOURCE.md`](data/SOURCE.md). The 3.8 MB raw file is too big to keep, so the folder holds a verbatim 158-line excerpt (first rows, every 1 January and 1 July, July 2024, every named day, last row), and `check.mjs` can re-verify all 19,650 embedded values against a fresh download. With the file I downloaded, all 19,650 match exactly.

### The processing

`data/extract.mjs` keeps observed rows (flag `I`) with a published LOD, drops the first one (1973-01-02, whose LOD is a `0.0000` placeholder; the UT1 values around it imply about 2.8 ms) and checks the remaining 19,650 days are consecutive. Values are stored exactly, as integers of 0.0001 ms.

`lod-core.js` (loaded by the page and by the tests) does the rest:

- **Removing the slow drift.** Each day minus its centred 365-day mean. This keeps everything faster than a year and is defined from 4 July 1973 to 22 April 2026.
- **The measured era.** Every test of the Moon uses the days from 1 January 1985 on, for the reason in the Honest assessment. The whole-record figures are computed too and quoted beside them.
- **The periodogram.** Hann taper, zero-padded to 131,072 points. A line's strength is the power at the predicted frequency divided by the median power within ±15% of it, with ±0.0004 cycles/day masked around every known line.
- **The fit.** Least squares of the high-passed series on nine fixed frequencies: six lunar tidal lines (Mf, its 18.6-year sideband Mf′, Mm, MSf, MSm, Mtm) and the 1, ½ and ⅓-year harmonics. Every frequency is computed from the Moon's and Sun's mean motions in Meeus's *Astronomical Algorithms*, never estimated from the data. The whole-record fit supplies the seasons, the sound and the date box; the same fit on 1985 on supplies the Moon's sizes and timing.
- **The forecast.** The same fit on the days from 1 January 1985 to 31 December 2015 only, evaluated on the 3,765 days after.
- **The ripple's size by year.** The 13.66-day term refitted to each calendar year, after subtracting the full fit's seasonal terms. The correlation with the standstill cycle uses 1985–2025.
- **The early record.** The residual after the full fit (Moon and seasons out), band-passed to about a week to a month (its 7-day minus its 35-day running mean), compared between 1974–1982 and 1985–2025, beside the 13.66-day line fitted to each era.
- **The Pacific.** Complete calendar-month means, centred three-month means (to match the ONI's three-month seasons), then twelve-month differences of both at the same centre month.
- **Preview.** `preview.jpg` is the carpet in *rhythms only* mode with the title, rendered from the page itself.
- **Sound.** The high-passed series minus the fitted seasons and minus its own 61-day running mean, played at 4,000 days per second (the fortnight lands near 293 Hz). The second button subtracts the six fitted lunar sine waves first. Nothing is synthesised.

### The statistics behind each claim

- **The peaks are the Moon's, in the measured era.** Measured peak periods from 1985 on, from the zero-padded spectrum: 13.6605 (predicted 13.6608), 9.1329 (9.1329), 27.5552 (27.5545), 14.7649 (14.7653), 31.8068 (31.8119) days: the four strongest within about a minute of prediction, the weakest within 8 minutes. Power ÷ neighbouring median: 29,048× at 13.66 d, 3,440× at 9.13 d, 1,690× at 27.55 d, 158× at 14.77 d, 52× at 31.81 d. Under a no-line null each ordinate is roughly exponential, so a ratio to the median above r has probability 2^−r (ignoring the slope of the background across the band and the uncertainty in its median, both small beside these ratios); 52 gives about 2×10⁻¹⁶. The frequencies were fixed in advance, so there is no look-elsewhere correction to make. Controls at periods with no expected tide: the synodic month (phases) 0.04×, the tropical month itself 0.14×, the draconic month 0.38×. Smaller spikes at 6.86, 7.10 and 9.56 days match the combinations 4s−2p, 4s−2h and 3s−2h+p to better than 0.005 d (s, h, p: the Moon's, Sun's and lunar perigee's mean motions). For scale, the measured era's frequency resolution is about 18 minutes at 13.66 days and 97 minutes at 31.8 days; a strong line can be located to a small fraction of that, so "within 8 minutes" is a statement of agreement, not a claimed precision for the weakest line.
- **The same, over the whole record.** Including 1973–1984 the ratios rise to 41,230×, 5,433×, 2,014×, 213× and 58×, every peak lies within 4 minutes, and the controls read 0.12×, 0.64× and 0.27×. Most of that rise is simply the longer record. A steady line's power grows with the square of the number of days and the background beside it only in proportion, so the ratio grows in proportion to the length, and the whole high-passed record has 19,286 days against 15,087, 1.28 times as many. Two stretches of the measured era shorter by that same factor (11,802 days, its first and its last) give 23,079× and 21,115× at 13.66 d, against 22,723× expected from length alone. Compared at equal length, the record's first 15,087 days (4 July 1973 to 23 October 2014) against the measured era's, the early years raise the ratios at 13.66, 9.13 and 14.77 d by 1.20, 1.52 and 1.20 times (the restored tide over a smoothed background), leave 27.55 d unchanged (1.00×) and lower 31.81 d (0.69×). The controls have no line, so the length does not raise them; they only move by chance. At 2,172 line-free frequencies between 9 and 32 days the median ratio is 1.01× in the measured era and 1.00× over the whole record.
- **Why half the phase cycle.** The phases themselves (where sunlight falls on the Moon) raise no tide. The 14.77-day peak is half the synodic month because the Sun's pull squashes the Moon's orbit (the "variation", the 2D term of the lunar distance series), so the Moon runs slightly nearer at new and full Moon alike. This is textbook lunar theory, not something the page tests beyond the peak's position.
- **Timing.** The 13.66-day term fitted to 1985 on correlates 0.998 with cos(2 × the Moon's mean longitude), i.e. it peaks when the Moon is near the equinox points of its orbit, crossing the equator; the 27.55-day term correlates 0.9997 with cos(mean anomaly), peaking at perigee. Two sinusoids of the same frequency correlate by the cosine of their phase difference, so these numbers say only that the timing agrees, to within about 4 hours in both cases; they are not a measure of evidence. Both use Meeus's mean elements; a check against the perigee of 14 November 2016 (about 11 UT) gives a mean anomaly of about 1°.
- **The forecast.** Fitted only to 1985–2015, the six lunar sine waves explain 76% of the variance of the 2016–2026 days (high-passed, minus the training fit's seasons). R² is computed out of sample against the series mean.
- **The 18.6-year modulation.** From 1985 on, the fortnightly ripple's yearly size runs from 0.22 ms (1995) to 0.51 ms (2024), and correlates r = 0.99 with the cosine of the Moon's node longitude in mid-year (41 years; with the early years 1974–1984 included the r is the same, as it would be if those years are largely the model). The largest year near each major standstill (1988, 2005, 2024) falls within 1.5 years of it (1987.9, 2006.5, 2025.1, from the node's mean motion). The yearly values are not independent of each other, so I give no p-value; the claim is about where the peaks fall.
- **The early record.** In 1974–1982 the band-passed residual (periods of about a week to a month) has 31% of its 1985–2025 power, and in 1974 and 1975 under a tenth, although the median stated error then was 0.1916 ms against 0.0049 ms, 39 times larger. Yet the 13.66-day line fitted to 1974–1982 is 0.346 ms, against 0.353 ms from 1985 on. From 1985 to 2001 the same background is as strong as in 2002–2025.
- **The sound's second button.** Over the whole record, which is what the sound plays, power at 13.66 d drops from 41,230× to 2.71× the background with the six fitted lunar waves subtracted, and at 27.55 d from 2,014× to 0.58×, so the "notes go" claim is measured, not just heard.
- **The sound's start.** The part of the sound with periods under 8 days has an rms of 0.008 ms in 1974–1977 and 0.007 ms in 1978–1981, against 0.025–0.031 ms in every four-year block since 2002; with the Moon taken out, 1974–1977 is the quietest block of all (0.070 ms rms against 0.11–0.17 ms).
- **The seasons.** From the fitted 1, ½ and ⅓-year harmonics: longest around 9 April (+0.45 ms against the 365-day mean), shortest around 22 July (−0.75 ms), a swing of 1.21 ms; the calendar-day averages (dots on the chart) agree. These are averages over 1973–2026.
- **The Pacific.** Decided before I computed it (not formally pre-registered, and nothing timestamps the choice): twelve-month change of the three-month mean day length against twelve-month change of the ONI, lag 0. Over 630 months (March 1974 to August 2026), r = 0.48. It barely depends on that choice: 0.446 with the day one month ahead of the ocean, 0.481 at lag 0, 0.483 with the day one month behind, and 0.46, 0.46 and 0.52 in each third of the record. Both series are autocorrelated, so the effective sample size uses the general lag-by-lag form N / (1 + 2 Σₖ ρₓ(k) ρᵧ(k)) summed to lag N/4: about 94, giving t = 5.27 on 92.1 degrees of freedom and p ≈ 9×10⁻⁷ (two-sided). A model-free check, keeping the day-length series' Fourier amplitudes and randomising its phases 20,000 times, found 0 of 20,000 surrogates with |r| as large (the largest was 0.38); their spread of r (SD 0.101) implies an effective sample size of about 98. Reported for completeness: a different high-pass (calendar-month anomalies minus a centred 7-year mean, then three-month means, against the ONI level rather than its change) gives r = 0.49 over 558 months, effective n ≈ 62, p ≈ 5×10⁻⁵.
- **2026.** The latest twelve-month change is +0.94 ms (three-month mean centred on August 2026 minus August 2025), the largest in the record, while the ONI for July–September 2026 is +2.16 °C, the highest since early 2016.
- **The seconds.** The 19,650 excesses sum to 25.85 s; 25 leap seconds were inserted over the same span. As a consistency check, UT1−UTC on 2 January 1973 (0.8084 s) plus 25 leap seconds minus 25.85 s predicts −0.0412 s on 21 October 2026; the file says −0.0414 s. The first day shorter than 86,400 s was 2 August 1986, 2,103 days in all have been, and since the last leap second (31 December 2016) the Earth has lost only 0.63 s.

### Verification

- `check.mjs`: SHA-256 of every data file against `data/SOURCE.md`; all 157 verbatim raw lines in the excerpt against the embedded series; ONI and leap-second tables row by row against their raw files; every number tagged in the page has an independent recomputation. With `FINALS_PATH`, all 19,650 values against the downloaded file (they match exactly).
- `claims.test.mjs` (node:test) recomputes everything in separately written code: its own date arithmetic (Fliegel–Van Flandern), direct-sum running means, Gram–Schmidt QR instead of normal equations, a direct DFT and golden-section peak search instead of the FFT, Simpson integration of the t density instead of the incomplete beta function, surrogates done in the frequency domain, and it reads the raw ONI and tai-utc files. It asserts that all 94 tagged numbers on the page read exactly what it computes, that this README quotes the key ones, and that `lod-core.js` agrees with it to 1e-9 (peak periods to 0.0015 d), including the date-box arithmetic for a sample birthday. It also runs the surrogate test, the sensitivity analysis, the early-record comparison, the length and equal-length comparison of the whole-record ratios and the sound's per-era loudness above, and pins each fix from the independent review (see the Revision note) so that the old wording or numbers would fail.
- `tools/smoke.mjs` at desktop and phone width, plus my own Playwright passes in light and dark schemes at 1280 and 390 px with a date entered.

## Honest assessment

What holds:

- [Certain] Every day length on the page is the published Bulletin A value, unaltered, and the check proves it against the original file. Every number in the prose is machine-checked against an independent recomputation.
- [Certain] The published record contains the Moon's tidal periods, and they hold in the measured era alone: from 1985 on, sharp peaks at the predicted 13.66, 27.55, 9.13, 14.77 and 31.81-day periods (the four strongest within about a minute, the weakest within 8 minutes), at 52× to 29,048× the surrounding power, and nothing at the 29.53-day synodic month (0.04×). A lunar model fitted to 1985–2015 explains 76% of the 2016–2026 short-period variation out of sample.
- [Certain] Before the mid-1980s, the 9–32-day ripple in this file is mostly the producers' tide model, not measurement. The file shows it: in 1974–1982 the background at a week to a month has 31% of its 1985–2025 power (under a tenth in 1974–1975), although the stated errors then were 39 times larger, so those years were heavily smoothed; yet the 13.66-day line in them is full size (0.346 against 0.353 ms). Smoothing that strong would have flattened the line too unless the tide was taken out first and put back afterwards from a model. That is how the IERS describes its own smoothing (Bulletin B smooths UT1R, which is UT1 with Yoder et al.'s 41 short-period zonal-tide terms removed, and lists the model separately; 2001 explanatory supplement), and USNO's notes record a tidal model in its UT1 generation (29 November 2012). The change is gradual: 1974–1980 are the most smoothed, 1981–1984 are in between. The whole-record figures (41,230× and so on) are higher than the measured era's mostly because that record is 1.28 times as long, and only partly because of those years (see *The same, over the whole record*); the page tests the Moon from 1985 on because from then on the ripple is mostly measured, not modelled.
- [Likely] From 1985 on, the lines are the measured Earth. The background at these periods is as strong in 1985–2001 as in 2002–2025, so the smoothing there is light (the 2001 supplement's Table 2 describes Bulletin A's smoothing as keeping 95% of the amplitude at 3-day periods), and a remove-and-restore would leave the 9–32-day ripple mostly observational. Shorter periods (under about four days) stay heavily smoothed until about 2002, and the methods used since 2019 (a smoothing cubic spline) and October 2026 (a Kalman smoother) are described only briefly, so I cannot quantify the model's share exactly. Independent evidence that the Earth does this comes from raw lunar laser ranging (Yoder, Williams & Parke 1981: fortnightly and monthly tidal strengths of 0.99 ± 0.20 and 0.99 ± 0.15 against a predicted 0.94, from the abstract).
- [Certain] Arithmetic, not inference: the daily excesses sum to 25.85 s over the record, matching the UT1 drift and the 25 leap seconds to within 0.2 ms.
- [Likely] The fortnightly ripple's 2.3-fold swing from 1985 on tracks the 18.6-year lunar standstill cycle (r = 0.99). The physical reading (a wider monthly swing of the Moon's declination raises a bigger zonal tide) is textbook, but this page tests only the timing.
- [Likely] The seasonal swing (longest around 9 April, shortest around 22 July) is real and is mostly driven by the atmosphere. The swing is measured here; the cause is cited (Rosen & Salstein 1983; Hide & Dickey 1991), not shown, because the page has no wind data. Part of it is the Sun's own zonal tide, which I did not separate.
- [Likely] Year-to-year changes in day length go with El Niño (r = 0.48, effective n ≈ 94, p ≈ 9×10⁻⁷; 0 of 20,000 phase surrogates). The relationship explains under a quarter of the variance, two of the four biggest El Niños (1997–98, 2023–24) barely show in this measure, and the AAM caveat below applies.
- [Speculative] That the 2026 jump (+0.94 ms in twelve months, the largest in the record) is mainly El Niño. A twelve-month change cannot separate El Niño from the slower decade-scale swings, which turn on their own schedule, and the newest months are provisional; the page says the data cannot yet tell.

Gaps, biases and confounders:

- The 1970s values carry stated errors of 0.14–0.49 ms on 80% of days (median 0.20 ms, maximum 1.07 ms), and their day-to-day detail is a smoothed curve plus the restored tide, so "longest day" among them is a close race partly decided by noise and by the model; the page says so beside any early answer. The record's single longest day (2 April 1973, +4.03 ms ± 0.19) is itself only the largest of several values within error of each other, and so is 21 October 1976 in the birthday count above.
- The single "exactly 86,400 s" day is exact only at the file's 0.0001 ms rounding; its stated error is 3.3 µs, and 49 days in all lie within their own stated error of zero (105 within twice it). The page calls it exact "to the file's last decimal place" and gives the 49 alongside.
- Bulletin A lists forecasts of the atmosphere's angular momentum among its inputs. USNO's archive note of 25 February 2010 says these were removed from the UT1 combination "except for the one corresponding to the last combination point"; a 2007 note describes adding AAM forecasts to the combination before that. So for at least 2010 on they touch only the newest day of each weekly update. Whether earlier history was ever recomputed I could not tell; if AAM reached further back, the wind and El Niño links would be partly circular. The page states the assumption.
- The newest weeks of the file are rapid estimates and the October 2026 bulletin warns of small revisions (under 20 µs in UT1) up to a year back. The ONI is recomputed against shifting 30-year baselines and its latest seasons can change.
- The 365-day high-pass is a crude filter. It leaves a little leakage: the small ripple in the seasonal dots is partly the Moon not quite averaging out over 53 years (removing the fitted lunar waves cuts the scatter of the calendar-day means about the fitted curve from 0.047 to 0.031 ms rms; the rest is weather) and it makes the annual and semiannual lines unsuitable for the background-ratio test, which is therefore quoted only for the lunar lines.
- The effective sample size. The AR(1) shortcut N(1 − r₁r₂)/(1 + r₁r₂), usually attributed to Bretherton et al. (1999), which I could not open, gives 35 and p ≈ 0.004 from lag-1 autocorrelations of 0.931 and 0.963. It assumes memory that decays geometrically from lag 1, but these twelve-month changes lose theirs within about a year (autocorrelations at lags 3, 6, 9 and 12: 0.69, 0.45, 0.24 and 0.00 for the day; 0.75, 0.29, −0.15 and −0.37 for the ONI), so it understates the information about threefold and is at most a conservative bound. The lag-by-lag sum gives 85 to 113 depending on where it is truncated (anywhere from 6 to 200 lags; the page uses N/4, which gives 94), so p lies between about 7×10⁻⁸ and 3×10⁻⁶, and the surrogates imply about 98. The page therefore says "about 94" and "roughly one in a million" rather than anything sharper. The same correction moves the sensitivity variant from effective n ≈ 23 and p ≈ 0.02 to 62 and 5×10⁻⁵ (59 to 74 depending on truncation).
- The causal stories for the decade-scale drift (the core) and the long-term trend are cited from Hide & Dickey (1991) and Stephenson, Morrison & Hohenkerk (2016). The latter's result, from its abstract: the day has lengthened by about 1.8 ms per century on average over 2,700 years, significantly less than the 2.3 ms per century that tidal friction alone predicts. The paper invokes post-glacial rebound (the crust's slow recovery since the last ice age) and coupling between the core and the mantle to explain the difference, and says rebound "by itself ... cannot account completely" for it (section 6; the conclusion calls the cause "a combination of post-glacial rebound and core–mantle coupling"); the page gives both as the authors' explanation, not as established. The statement that the SI second matches a day of the early 1800s is from secondary sources on the history of ephemeris time (Finkleman et al. 2011 and general references); I could not open a primary source for it.
- Citations confirmed via search from abstracts, publisher records or reference lists: Yoder, Williams & Parke 1981; Rosen & Salstein 1983; Hide & Dickey 1991; Dickey et al. 1994; Stephenson et al. 2016; Finkleman et al. 2011 (arXiv); CGPM 2022 Resolution 4. The 2026 CGPM decision (on 15 October 2026, to stop adding leap seconds from 20 May 2027) is from news reports (Euronews, 16 October 2026); I could not reach the BIPM's own text, and the page says so.
- The sound is honest but crude: linear interpolation at 4,000 days per second. Its start is the smoothest stretch, not the noisiest: the 1970s were heavily smoothed when the record was compiled, so despite stated errors of about 0.2 ms they carry less fast wiggle than any later stretch. Audio starts only on a button press.
- At phone width the carpet's cells are under one pixel wide; the seasonal bands and lunar threads still show, but individual days are reachable only by the tooltip.

## Where it could go next

- **Wind data beside the days.** NCEP/NCAR reanalysis atmospheric angular momentum series are public; adding one would turn the wind section from cited to shown.
- **The long view.** Stephenson, Morrison and Hohenkerk's eclipse-based day lengths back to 720 BC, as a zoomed-out companion: the same millisecond scale stretched over 2,700 years.
- **Your day, in context.** Show where your date sits in the Moon's fortnightly cycle and the seasonal curve with the actual sine waves drawn through it.
- **Earth's other wobble.** The same file holds polar motion; the 433-day Chandler wobble would make a matching chapter.
- **A tidal-model comparison.** Subtract the IERS zonal-tide model (Conventions 2010, chapter 8) instead of fitting, and test whether what remains still carries any lunar signal; in the early record it should carry almost none.
- **The early record's own history.** USNO's documentation of how the 1970s series was built (sampling interval, smoothing, when the tide was restored) would date the change from model to measurement exactly, instead of from the data.

## Revision note

An independent reviewer rebuilt every number in separate code from a fresh download. The data and the arithmetic held, but several claims did not. I reproduced each finding with my own code before changing anything, and all of these are now fixed:

- **The tide-model answer was too soft.** The page said that for the 1970s it "can't rule out" the producers' tide model. The file itself shows more than that. Before the mid-1980s the short-period detail is heavily smoothed (31% of today's background power in 1974–1982, under a tenth in 1974–1975, despite 39 times larger stated errors), while the 13.66-day line stays at full strength. So there the ripple is mostly the model. Every test of the Moon now uses 1985 on, and the whole-record figures are kept alongside. Headline figures moved:
  - The 13.66-day peak went from 41,230× to 29,048× background, and the weakest from 58× to 52×. Most of that drop is the shorter span, not the removed years (see the last item below).
  - "Within 4 minutes" became "within 8 minutes" for the weakest line.
  - The forecast, now trained on 1985–2015, went from 75% to 76%.
  - The ripple's yearly range went from 0.21 ms (1978) – 0.51 ms to 0.22 ms (1995) – 0.51 ms, still r = 0.99, now over 41 years.
